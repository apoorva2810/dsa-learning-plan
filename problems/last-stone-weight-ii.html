Last Stone Weight II Medium 250

LeetCode #1049 ↗
Status

Smash stones together in any order you like and find the smallest weight that can remain at the end.

Problem

You have a list stones, where stones[i] is the weight of stone i. In each turn you choose any two stones with weights x <= y and smash them together:

  • if x == y, both stones are destroyed;
  • otherwise the stone of weight x is destroyed and the other one becomes a stone of weight y - x.

After at most n - 1 turns at most one stone is left. Return the smallest possible weight of that last stone, or 0 if no stone is left.

Examples

Example 1
Input: stones = [6, 11, 4, 5]
Output: 4
Smash 11 and 6 into a 5, smash the two 5s into nothing, and the 4 remains. Seen as two groups, {11} weighs 11 and {6, 4, 5} weighs 15. The gap is 4, and no other split is closer.
Example 2
Input: stones = [2, 3, 5]
Output: 0
Smash 5 with 3 to leave a 2, then the two 2s destroy each other. As groups, {2, 3} and {5} both weigh 5.
Example 3
Input: stones = [7]
Output: 7
A single stone is never smashed, so it stays as it is.

Constraints

  • 1 <= len(stones) <= 30
  • 1 <= stones[i] <= 100

Think first try before reading the solution

Follow one smash. Smashing x and y leaves y - x: the heavier stone keeps its sign and the lighter one flips it. After all the smashes, what does the last stone's weight look like in terms of the original stones?
It is a sum in which every original stone has a plus or a minus. Call the plus stones group A and the minus stones group B: the last weight is the difference of the two group totals. So you want two groups whose totals are as close as possible, and the best such split can always be carried out.
If the smaller group weighs s, the gap is total - 2s. Find the largest s up to total // 2 that some subset of the stones can sum to. That is a 0/1 knapsack over sums.

Key idea

Follow what a smash does to the signs. Smashing x and y (with x <= y) leaves y - x: the heavier stone keeps its sign and the lighter one flips it. Carry that through every smash and the last stone weighs a sum in which each original stone carries a plus or a minus. Put the plus stones in group A and the minus stones in group B: the last weight is the difference of the two group totals, so it can never be smaller than the best way to split the stones into two groups.

The best split can also always be played out. Take one stone from each group and smash them; the leftover joins the group of the heavier stone, and the gap between the group totals does not change. When one group is used up, the other holds at most one stone, because with two or more you could move one across and get a smaller gap than the best one.

So the task is: split the stones into two groups whose totals are as close as possible. If one group weighs s and s <= total / 2, the other weighs total - s and the gap is total - 2s. We want the largest s <= total // 2 that some group of stones can reach.

Which sums can a group reach? That is a 0/1 knapsack (each item is used at most once). Keep can[s], true when some subset of the stones seen so far sums to exactly s. Start with can[0] = True (the empty subset). A new stone x turns can[s - x] into can[s]. Updating from high s to low s guarantees the stone is added at most once per pass.

Approaches from the obvious one to the one you want in an interview

By the argument above, the answer is the smallest |sum(A) - sum(B)| over all ways to split the stones into two groups A and B. Try every split: best(i, diff) gives stone i a plus sign (group A) or a minus sign (group B), keeping the running difference, and at the end abs(diff) is a candidate answer.

There are 2^n splits. That is fine for about 20 stones and hopeless for 30 (over a billion). The recursion also repeats itself: many splits of the first few stones reach the same running difference, and what remains to do depends only on the stones left and on that difference.

Dry run

  1. stones [2, 3, 5] have 2^3 = 8 sign choices; the answer is the smallest absolute total among them
  2. +2 +3 +5 = 10, +2 +3 -5 = 0, +2 -3 +5 = 4, +2 -3 -5 = -6, and the other four are mirror images
  3. the smallest is 0: groups {2, 3} and {5} both weigh 5, so nothing is left

Python

class Solution:
    def lastStoneWeightII(self, stones: List[int]) -> int:
        n = len(stones)

        def best(i: int, diff: int) -> int:
            """Smallest |difference| reachable when stones[i:] still need a group."""
            if i == n:                                   # every stone is in a group
                return abs(diff)
            return min(best(i + 1, diff + stones[i]),    # stone goes to group A
                       best(i + 1, diff - stones[i]))    # stone goes to group B

        return best(0, 0)
Time O(2^n)Space O(n)

Two calls per stone, n levels deep.

When to use: To confirm the reduction on tiny inputs. Beyond about 20 stones it is too slow.

The recursion only needs to know which totals a group can reach. Keep a set sums of all subset sums found so far, starting with {0} (the empty group). For each stone, every sum s already in the set can also take the stone, giving s + stone, so add all of those. After the last stone, sums holds every total group A can have. The other group has total - s, so the answer is the smallest |total - 2s| over s in sums.

The set has at most total + 1 members, so each stone costs O(total) and the whole run is O(n · total) instead of 2^n. The set is easy to read but keeps sums up to the full total, although only sums up to total // 2 can matter.

Dry run

  1. stones [2, 3, 5], total 10: sums = {0}
  2. after 2: {0, 2}; after 3: {0, 2, 3, 5}; after 5: {0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10}
  3. |10 - 2s| over those sums is 10, 6, 4, 0, 4, 6, 10; the smallest is 0

Python

class Solution:
    def lastStoneWeightII(self, stones: List[int]) -> int:
        total = sum(stones)
        sums = {0}                                       # totals some group of stones can reach (empty group: 0)
        for stone in stones:
            sums |= {s + stone for s in sums}            # every old total can also take this stone
        # a group of weight s leaves a group of weight total - s; their gap is |total - 2s|
        return min(abs(total - 2 * s) for s in sums)
Time O(n · total)Space O(total)

Each stone touches at most total + 1 sums, and the set never holds more than that.

When to use: The shortest correct Python version, and a good first step before the array.

Watch it run

Only the smaller group matters, and it weighs at most half = total // 2. Keep a list can of length half + 1, where can[s] is true when some subset of the stones seen so far sums to exactly s, and can[0] = True. Process the stones one at a time. For a stone x, every reachable s - x makes s reachable: set can[s] = True whenever can[s - x] is true.

Scan s from half down to x. When can[s] is updated, can[s - x] (a smaller index) has not been touched in this pass yet, so it still means subsets without this stone: the stone is used at most once. Scanning from low to high would read cells already updated in this pass and add the stone again and again.

At the end, take the largest s with can[s] true. The groups weigh s and total - s, so the last stone weighs total - 2 * s.

Dry run

  1. stones [2, 3, 5]: total 10, half = 5, can = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0] (only the empty group)
  2. stone 2: can[2] becomes 1 → reachable {0, 2}; stone 3: can[5] (from 2) and can[3] (from 0) → {0, 2, 3, 5}
  3. stone 5: can[5] is already 1, nothing changes; the largest reachable sum is 5
  4. answer = 10 - 2 × 5 = 0

Python

class Solution:
    def lastStoneWeightII(self, stones: List[int]) -> int:
        total = sum(stones)
        half = total // 2                                # the smaller group never weighs more than this
        can = [False] * (half + 1)                       # can[s]: some subset of the stones seen so far sums to s
        can[0] = True                                    # the empty subset
        for stone in stones:
            for s in range(half, stone - 1, -1):         # high to low, so each stone is used at most once
                if can[s - stone]:
                    can[s] = True
        best = max(s for s in range(half + 1) if can[s]) # the heaviest reachable group that is at most half
        return total - 2 * best                          # the other group weighs total - best
Time O(n · total)Space O(total)

n passes over at most total / 2 + 1 cells; one list of booleans.

When to use: The standard answer: the same subset-sum table as Partition Equal Subset Sum, plus one subtraction at the end.

Common mistakes

  • Simulating the smashes greedily, always taking the two heaviest stones as in Last Stone Weight. Here you choose the pairs, and the heaviest-two rule is not optimal: for [4, 4, 3, 3, 2] it leaves 2, but 4 + 4 against 3 + 3 + 2 leaves nothing.
  • Scanning the sums from low to high in the one-list version. The cell can[s - x] has then already been updated for the same stone, so the stone is used again and again (an unbounded knapsack). Scan from half down to x.
  • Returning total - best instead of total - 2 * best. The groups weigh best and total - best, and the answer is the gap between them.
  • Searching the sums beyond total // 2. It still gives the right answer when you take the minimum over abs(total - 2 * s), but the table doubles in size for no gain; a group heavier than half is just the other group.
  • Trying all 2^n sign choices for n = 30. That is over a billion splits; the table needs about 30 * 1500 steps.

Python notes

  • total // 2 is the largest weight the smaller group can have; a group heavier than that is just the other group.
  • A set of reachable sums, sums |= {s + stone for s in sums}, is the shortest correct version. The comprehension is evaluated completely before the union, so the stone is used once.
  • A list of booleans scanned from high to low is the textbook 0/1 knapsack. range(half, stone - 1, -1) is empty when the stone is heavier than half, so such stones are skipped correctly.
  • A single integer works as a bitset: bits |= bits << stone adds every reachable sum shifted by the stone in one operation, and bits >> s & 1 tests whether sum s is reachable.
  • max(s for s in range(half + 1) if can[s]) always has an answer, because can[0] is true.

Follow-up questions

What an interviewer might ask next, and what changes.

  • Partition Equal Subset Sum LC 416 — the yes/no version: can the gap be exactly 0? The same table with booleans and the single goal total / 2.
  • Target Sum LC 494 — count the plus/minus assignments that reach a given total instead of minimising the gap: the table holds counts, not booleans.
  • Last Stone Weight LC 1046 — a different game: the rules force you to smash the two heaviest stones, so there is no choice to optimise and a max-heap is enough.
  • Partition Array Into Two Arrays to Minimize Sum Difference LC 2035 — values up to 10^7, so a table over sums is impossible. Split the array in half, list every subset sum of each half grouped by size, and match them with a sort and two pointers (meet in the middle).
  • Return the two groups, not only the gap — keep the set of reachable sums after every stone and walk back from the best sum: if the sum was reachable before stone i, skip the stone, otherwise it belongs to group A.

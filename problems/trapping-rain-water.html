Trapping Rain Water Hard

LeetCode #42 ↗
Status

Given bar heights, add up how much rain water stays trapped between the bars.

Problem

height[i] is the height of a bar of width 1 standing at position i. Rain falls on all of them, and water collects in the dips between bars. Water above a bar stays only if there is a taller (or equal) bar somewhere on both sides; past the first and last bar it runs off.

Return the total number of units of water trapped.

Put precisely: the water above bar i rises to the lower of two walls, the tallest bar in height[0..i] and the tallest bar in height[i..n-1], so bar i holds min(left wall, right wall) - height[i] units.

Examples

Example 1
Input: height = [0, 1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 1, 3, 2, 1, 2, 1]
Output: 6
Water per bar: [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0], which adds up to 6.
Example 2
Input: height = [4, 2, 0, 3, 2, 5]
Output: 9
Every inner bar fills up to level 4 (the lower of the walls 4 and 5): 2 + 4 + 1 + 2 = 9.
Example 3
Input: height = [3, 2, 1]
Output: 0
Heights only fall, so all water runs off to the right.

Constraints

  • 1 <= len(height) <= 2 * 10^4
  • 0 <= height[i] <= 10^5

Think first try before reading the solution

Forget the total for a moment and look at one bar. How high can the water above it rise before it spills to the left or to the right?
It rises to min(tallest bar on its left, tallest bar on its right), counting the bar itself in both. Recomputing those two maxima for every bar is O(n²); you can precompute them in two passes.
To drop the extra arrays, walk two pointers inward while tracking left_max and right_max. Whichever is smaller is the side whose water level you already know for sure: settle that bar and move that pointer.

Key idea

Think about a single bar. The water above it can rise until it would spill over the lower of its two walls: the tallest bar anywhere to its left and the tallest bar anywhere to its right. So water[i] = min(left_max[i], right_max[i]) - height[i], where both maxima include bar i itself (that keeps the result from going negative for a tall bar).

Precomputing left_max and right_max as arrays gives an O(n) solution with two extra arrays. The two-pointer version drops them. Keep left_max, the tallest bar from the left end up to left, and right_max, the tallest bar from the right end down to right. If left_max <= right_max, bar left is already settled: its left wall is exactly left_max, and its right wall is at least right_max (that bar exists on its right), which is at least left_max. The lower wall is left_max, so the bar holds left_max - height[left]. Otherwise the same argument settles bar right with right_max.

Each step settles one bar and the pointers never move back, so the walk is O(n) time with O(1) extra space.

Approaches from the obvious one to the one you want in an interview

Apply the definition directly. For each bar i, scan left to find the tallest bar in height[0..i] and scan right to find the tallest in height[i..n-1]; the bar holds min(left_wall, right_wall) - height[i].

It is correct, but each bar rescans almost the whole array: about n² steps in total. Neighbouring bars share nearly all of their walls, so most of that scanning repeats work already done.

Dry run

  1. height = [4, 2, 0, 3, 2, 5]
  2. bar 1 (2): left wall max(4, 2) = 4, right wall max(2, 0, 3, 2, 5) = 5 → level 4 → holds 2
  3. bar 2 (0): walls 4 and 5 → level 4 → holds 4
  4. bars 3 and 4 hold 1 and 2; the end bars hold 0 → total 9

Python

class Solution:
    def trap(self, height: List[int]) -> int:
        n = len(height)
        water = 0
        for i in range(n):
            left_wall = 0
            for j in range(i + 1):                 # tallest bar in height[0..i]
                left_wall = max(left_wall, height[j])
            right_wall = 0
            for j in range(i, n):                  # tallest bar in height[i..n-1]
                right_wall = max(right_wall, height[j])
            water += min(left_wall, right_wall) - height[i]
        return water
Time O(n²)Space O(1)

For every bar, two scans that together cover the whole array.

When to use: Only to state the formula; it is too slow for n = 2·10⁴.

The brute force recomputes the same maxima over and over. Compute them once instead:

  • left_max[i] = max(left_max[i - 1], height[i]), filled left to right;
  • right_max[i] = max(right_max[i + 1], height[i]), filled right to left;
  • water[i] = min(left_max[i], right_max[i]) - height[i].

Three linear passes give O(n) time. The cost is two extra arrays of size n. This is the same "precompute everything to the left and everything to the right" idea as Product of Array Except Self.

Dry run

  1. height = [4, 2, 0, 3, 2, 5]
  2. left_max = [4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 5]
  3. right_max = [5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5]
  4. min − h = [0, 2, 4, 1, 2, 0] → total 9

Python

class Solution:
    def trap(self, height: List[int]) -> int:
        n = len(height)
        if n == 0:
            return 0
        left_max = [0] * n                         # left_max[i] = max(height[0..i])
        left_max[0] = height[0]
        for i in range(1, n):
            left_max[i] = max(left_max[i - 1], height[i])
        right_max = [0] * n                        # right_max[i] = max(height[i..n-1])
        right_max[-1] = height[-1]
        for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
            right_max[i] = max(right_max[i + 1], height[i])
        return sum(min(left_max[i], right_max[i]) - height[i] for i in range(n))
Time O(n)Space O(n)

Three passes over the array; two helper arrays of length n.

When to use: Easiest O(n) version to explain and to get right; a good first answer in an interview.

Watch it run

You never need both full arrays at once: to settle a bar you only need to know its lower wall.

Keep left and right pointers and the running maxima left_max = max(height[0..left]) and right_max = max(height[right..n-1]). At each step:

  • if left_max <= right_max: bar left has a left wall of exactly left_max and a right wall of at least right_max, so its lower wall is left_max. Add left_max - height[left] and move left right;
  • otherwise bar right is settled by right_max in the same way; add it and move right left.

Each step settles exactly one bar, so the loop (while left <= right) runs n times. Only a handful of variables are stored.

Dry run

  1. height = [4, 2, 0, 3, 2, 5]; L=0, R=5
  2. left_max 4, right_max 5 → 4 ≤ 5: bar 0 is the wall itself, holds 0
  3. bar 1: left_max 4 ≤ 5 → holds 4 − 2 = 2; bar 2 → 4 − 0 = 4; bar 3 → 4 − 3 = 1; bar 4 → 4 − 2 = 2
  4. L = R = 5: left_max becomes 5, bar 5 holds 0 → total 9

Python

class Solution:
    def trap(self, height: List[int]) -> int:
        left, right = 0, len(height) - 1
        left_max = right_max = 0                   # tallest bar seen from each end so far
        water = 0
        while left <= right:
            left_max = max(left_max, height[left])
            right_max = max(right_max, height[right])
            if left_max <= right_max:
                # some bar on the right is >= left_max, so bar `left` is capped by left_max
                water += left_max - height[left]
                left += 1
            else:
                # some bar on the left is > right_max, so bar `right` is capped by right_max
                water += right_max - height[right]
                right -= 1
        return water
Time O(n)Space O(1)

One pass that settles one bar per iteration; four integers of state.

When to use: The optimal answer. Explain it through the prefix-max version: it is that formula with the arrays replaced by two running values.

Common mistakes

  • Taking the maxima strictly to the left and right of bar i. For a bar taller than both sides the formula then goes negative; include the bar itself in both maxima (or clamp with max(0, ...)).
  • In the two-pointer version, settling the side with the larger max. That bar's lower wall may be on the side you have not scanned yet, so its water level is still unknown.
  • Comparing height[left] with height[right] but then adding water with the wrong side's maximum. Whichever rule you pick, the bar you settle must use its own side's running maximum.
  • Expecting the first or last bar to hold water: there is no wall beyond them, and the formula already gives 0.
  • The O(n²) brute force runs about 4·10⁸ inner steps at n = 2·10⁴, far too slow in Python.

Python notes

  • itertools.accumulate(height, max) yields the running maximum, which is exactly the left_max array; list(accumulate(reversed(height), max))[::-1] gives right_max.
  • left_max = right_max = 0 binds both names to the same integer. Integers are immutable, so updating one later does not change the other (unlike a = b = []).
  • Once the arrays exist, sum(min(a, b) - h for a, b, h in zip(left_max, right_max, height)) is the whole answer.

Follow-up questions

What an interviewer might ask next, and what changes.

  • Container With Most Water LC 11 — only two lines form one container and the bars in between do not matter; same 'move the lower side' instinct, but you maximise one area instead of summing columns.
  • Trapping Rain Water II LC 407 — a 2-D height map: the lowest wall is no longer one of two pointers but the lowest cell on the whole boundary, so a min-heap replaces the pointers and water floods inward from the edges.
  • Largest Rectangle in Histogram LC 84 — another bars problem where each bar's answer depends on its nearest shorter neighbours; there the monotonic stack is the main tool.
  • Solve it with a monotonic stack — keep indices of bars with decreasing heights. When a taller bar arrives, pop the bottom of the dip and add the horizontal layer of water between the new bar and the bar below it on the stack. Still O(n), but water is counted in layers instead of columns.
  • Return the water above every bar — the prefix-max approach yields each water[i] directly; the two-pointer walk produces the same values, just in an outside-in order.

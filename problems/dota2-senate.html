Dota2 Senate Medium 250

LeetCode #649 ↗
Status

Two parties take turns banning each other's senators in rounds; find which party is left standing when everyone plays their best.

Problem

A senate has two parties, Radiant ('R') and Dire ('D'). Every senator belongs to one of them. The string senate lists the senators in the order they act.

The senate plays in rounds. In a round, every senator who still has the right to vote takes a turn, in order, and does one of two things:

  • Ban one senator of the other party. A banned senator loses all their rights: they skip the rest of this round and every later round.
  • Announce victory, but only if every senator who still has rights belongs to their own party.

When the last senator of a round has acted, the next round starts with the remaining senators in the same order. Every senator plays the best way for their own party.

Return the party that wins, as the string "Radiant" or "Dire".

Examples

Example 1
Input: senate = "RDRDD"
Output: "Radiant"
Round 1: R0 bans D1, R2 bans D3, and D4 bans R0 (the next Radiant after position 4 wraps around to position 0). Round 2: the only Radiant left, R2, acts first and bans D4. No Dire senator remains.
Example 2
Input: senate = "DDRRR"
Output: "Dire"
Round 1: D0 bans R2, D1 bans R3, and R4 bans D0. Round 2: D1 acts first and bans R4. Three Radiant senators lose to two Dire senators, because Dire acts first.
Example 3
Input: senate = "DRD"
Output: "Dire"
D0 acts first and bans R1, the only Radiant senator. Dire wins in the very first round.

Constraints

  • 1 <= len(senate) <= 10^4
  • senate[i] is 'R' or 'D'

Think first try before reading the solution

A senator can ban any opponent, but only one choice is smart. Which opposing senator hurts you most if you leave them alone?
The most dangerous opponent is the one who acts next: they will ban one of your teammates before anyone else from their party gets a turn. So every senator should ban the next opposing senator in the order. Which structure gives you "the next senator of each party" quickly?
Keep one queue of positions per party. Compare the two fronts: the smaller position acts first and bans the other front. The senator who acted goes to the back of their queue, as position + n: the same senator, one round later.

Key idea

Every ban removes one opponent, so the question is whom to remove. Think about who is about to cause trouble: the opposing senator who acts next will use their turn to ban one of your teammates. Banning someone who acts later leaves that dangerous senator free, so the best ban is always the next opponent in the order. (This is a greedy choice: remove the most urgent threat.)

That rule makes the game easy to run. Keep the positions of each party's senators in a queue, with the earliest position at the front. The two fronts are the next senator of each party to act. Whoever has the smaller position acts first, and that senator's ban lands on the other front, because the other front is the next opponent in the order. The banned senator leaves for good.

The senator who did the banning plays again in the next round, in the same place in the order. Adding n to their position does exactly that: it moves them behind everyone who still acts in the current round, and keeps the relative order of everyone else who gets + n. When one queue becomes empty, the other party has won. Each loop removes one senator, so there are at most n iterations.

Approaches from the obvious one to the one you want in an interview

Play the game as it is described. Keep a list of the senators who still have rights and a pointer i to whoever acts. That senator bans the next opposing senator in the list, going around the circle, which means scanning forward and removing that entry from the list. Then the pointer moves to the next senator. Stop when only one party is left in the list.

It is correct, but every ban scans the list to find the target and shifts the list when removing it, so a ban costs O(n) and there are up to n bans: O(n²) overall.

Dry run

  1. senate = "RDRDD": senators = [R, D, R, D, D], i = 0
  2. R0 bans the next D (index 1) → [R, R, D, D], then it is the next senator's turn
  3. R2 bans the next D → [R, R, D]; D4 bans the next R, wrapping around to R0 → [R, D]
  4. the pointer wraps: R2 bans D4 → [R], only Radiant remains → "Radiant"

Python

class Solution:
    def predictPartyVictory(self, senate: str) -> str:
        senators = list(senate)                  # senators who still have the right to vote, in order
        i = 0                                    # whose turn it is
        while "R" in senators and "D" in senators:
            mine = senators[i]
            j = (i + 1) % len(senators)          # find the next senator of the other party, going around
            while senators[j] == mine:
                j = (j + 1) % len(senators)
            senators.pop(j)                      # ban them
            if j < i:                            # the list shifted left, so our own position moved too
                i -= 1
            i = (i + 1) % len(senators)          # the next senator's turn
        return "Radiant" if "R" in senators else "Dire"

Put the positions of the Radiant senators in one queue and the positions of the Dire senators in another, earliest first. Then repeat while both queues are non-empty:

  1. Take the two fronts, r and d.
  2. The smaller one acts first and bans the other (the other front is the next opponent). The banned senator is dropped.
  3. The senator who acted returns to the back of their own queue as position + n: the same senator, one round later.

When one queue is empty, the other party wins. Each iteration removes one senator, so there are at most n iterations, each O(1).

Dry run

  1. senate = "RDRDD", n = 5: Radiant queue [0, 2], Dire queue [1, 3, 4]
  2. fronts 0 and 1 → Radiant is first, bans Dire 1; Radiant queue [2, 5], Dire queue [3, 4]
  3. fronts 2 and 3 → Radiant bans Dire 3; Radiant queue [5, 7], Dire queue [4]
  4. fronts 5 and 4 → Dire is first (4 < 5), bans Radiant 5; Dire queue [9], Radiant queue [7]
  5. fronts 7 and 9 → Radiant bans Dire 9; the Dire queue is empty → "Radiant"

Python

class Solution:
    def predictPartyVictory(self, senate: str) -> str:
        n = len(senate)
        radiant = deque(i for i, party in enumerate(senate) if party == "R")   # positions, earliest first
        dire = deque(i for i, party in enumerate(senate) if party == "D")
        while radiant and dire:
            r, d = radiant.popleft(), dire.popleft()   # the next Radiant and the next Dire to act
            if r < d:                            # Radiant acts first and bans Dire
                radiant.append(r + n)            # the winner acts again, one round later
            else:                                # Dire acts first and bans Radiant
                dire.append(d + n)
        return "Radiant" if radiant else "Dire"

Watch it run

Keep a single queue of party letters in turn order, plus two counters for bans already issued but not yet carried out. Take the next senator off the queue:

  • If their party has a pending ban, they were banned earlier in the order: drop them and use up one pending ban.
  • Otherwise they act: they ban the next opponent, so add one pending ban against the other party, and they rejoin the back of the queue for the next round.

Stop when a party has no senators left. This never searches for the target, because the first opposing senator to come off the queue after the ban is exactly the next opponent.

Dry run

  1. senate = "RDRDD": queue R D R D D, counts R 2, D 3, no pending bans
  2. R acts: pending ban on Dire 1, R goes to the back. D comes up: it has a pending ban, so it is removed (D count 2)
  3. R acts: pending ban on Dire 1, R to the back. D comes up and is removed (D count 1)
  4. D acts: pending ban on Radiant 1, D to the back. R comes up: removed (R count 1)
  5. R comes up: no pending ban, so it acts and a pending ban on Dire appears. D comes up: removed (D count 0) → "Radiant"

Python

class Solution:
    def predictPartyVictory(self, senate: str) -> str:
        queue = deque(senate)                    # everyone in turn order
        radiant, dire = senate.count("R"), senate.count("D")
        radiant_banned = dire_banned = 0         # bans issued against each party but not applied yet
        while radiant and dire:
            party = queue.popleft()
            if party == "R":
                if radiant_banned:               # an earlier Dire senator already banned this one
                    radiant_banned -= 1
                    radiant -= 1
                else:
                    dire_banned += 1             # ban the next Dire senator that comes up
                    queue.append("R")            # and play again next round
            else:
                if dire_banned:
                    dire_banned -= 1
                    dire -= 1
                else:
                    radiant_banned += 1
                    queue.append("D")
        return "Radiant" if radiant else "Dire"
Time O(n)Space O(n)

Each acting senator creates one pending ban that removes exactly one senator later, so the loop runs at most about 2n times.

When to use: When you prefer to count bans instead of keeping positions. It needs only one queue.

Common mistakes

  • Banning an opponent other than the next one. In RDRDD, if R0 bans the last D instead of D1, then D1 bans R2 and D3 bans R0, so Dire wins; the correct play makes Radiant win.
  • Assuming the bigger party wins. In RRDDD there are three Dire senators and only two Radiant, yet Radiant wins: R0 and R1 act first and ban two of the three Dire senators before they get a turn.
  • Pushing the winner back with its old position instead of position + n. For RDDD the old position stays smaller than every remaining Dire position, so Radiant keeps winning and the function answers "Radiant", but the real answer is "Dire".
  • Forgetting to put the winner back at all. A senator who bans someone still acts in later rounds, so dropping both fronts ends the game with too few senators.
  • Comparing the senators' letters or counts instead of their positions. Only the position tells you who acts first.

Python notes

  • collections.deque (already imported) gives O(1) popleft() and append(). A list's pop(0) is O(n).
  • A deque is falsy when empty, so while radiant and dire: stops as soon as one party has nobody left, and "Radiant" if radiant else "Dire" reads the result.
  • deque(i for i, party in enumerate(senate) if party == "R") builds a queue straight from a generator.
  • Python integers do not overflow, so positions like position + n repeated many times are safe.

  • Find the Winner of the Circular Game LC 1823 — players leave a circle one at a time. A queue that rotates the survivors solves it, and so does the Josephus recurrence.
  • Number of Students Unable to Eat Lunch LC 1700 — a queue simulation where people who do not match the next item go to the back of the line.
  • What if there are three parties? — a senator now has two kinds of opponents, so "the next opponent" is ambiguous and you must decide which one to ban. The greedy argument has to be redone.
  • Return the number of rounds the game lasts — the position + n trick already counts rounds: the round of a senator is their position divided by n, rounded down. Track the position of the winning senator.

How to run a system design interview

A system design interview is a 45-minute conversation you are expected to drive. This chapter gives you the script (seven phases, with minutes), the questions to ask, a calculator for the estimation phase, a way to build the high-level diagram one justified component at a time, a method for choosing deep dives and stating trade-offs, and the phrases and red flags that separate strong answers from weak ones.

After this chapter you can
  • Follow a seven-phase, 45-minute script and say what should be on the whiteboard at each checkpoint
  • Turn a vague prompt into scoped functional and non-functional requirements with a short clarifying-questions list
  • Estimate QPS, peak QPS, storage and bandwidth from a handful of inputs, and say which design decision each number drives
  • Build a high-level diagram by adding each component only when a requirement forces it, then trace a read and a write through it
  • Pick deep dives deliberately and state every choice as a trade-off with a signal that would change your mind

What the interviewer is grading

A system design interview is a conversation about an open-ended problem such as "design a photo-sharing app". There is no single correct answer. The interviewer is collecting evidence on a few signals:

  • Scoping: do you turn a vague prompt into a bounded problem before you draw anything?
  • Structure: do you move through the problem in an order that keeps you (and the interviewer) from getting lost?
  • Breadth: do you know the standard building blocks (load balancers, caches, queues, databases, object storage) and when each earns its place?
  • Depth: can you go one level below the boxes in one or two areas and reason about failure, scale and cost?
  • Trade-offs: do you say what each choice gives up, and what would make you choose differently?
  • Communication: can the interviewer follow your thinking, and how do you react to hints and pushback?

At SDE-2 level you are expected to drive: propose the structure, make decisions, defend them, and ask the interviewer for direction only at real forks. Waiting to be led through each step reads as junior-level behaviour, however good your component knowledge is.

What the interviewer is really asking

The question under every question is: can this person take a vague problem, bound it, and make defensible decisions using numbers and trade-offs? Each phase below exists to leave visible evidence of exactly that on the whiteboard.

Requirements: turn a vague prompt into a problem

▶ Video lesson: Requirements: two lists before any box 3:56 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Spend the first five or six minutes on requirements and write them where the interviewer can see them. Two lists:

  • Functional requirements say what the system does. Keep to 3-5 core use cases ("upload a photo", "follow a user", "see a feed of followed users' photos") and say out loud what you are leaving out ("no stories, DMs, ads or search"). They determine the API and the data model.
  • Non-functional requirements say how well it does it: scale, latency, availability, consistency, durability, cost, security. They determine the architecture. A chat app for 1,000 users and one for 500 million users have the same features and completely different designs.

Clarifying questions: ask the ones that change the design

How to ask

Interrogation

Reciting ten questions and waiting for each answer burns five minutes, hands the problem back to the interviewer, and shows you cannot make a reasonable assumption on your own.

Propose and confirm

"I'll assume 50M daily users, mostly reads (about 20:1), feed latency under 300 ms at p99, feeds can be a few seconds stale, and photos must never be lost. Is there anything there you'd change?" One minute, and the interviewer corrects only what matters.

A model opening statement (about 45 seconds)

'Before I draw anything, I'd like to pin down scope and scale. I'll assume the core flows are uploading a photo, following people and viewing a feed; I'll leave out stories, DMs, ads and search. For scale I'll assume 50M daily users doing about 20 actions a day, mostly reads, so roughly 12k requests a second on average and 35k at peak. Feeds can be a few seconds stale, but uploaded photos must never be lost, and I'll target a 300 ms p99 on the feed. Does that match what you had in mind? Then I'll spend a few minutes on estimates and the API, draw the data flow, and leave time to go deep on feed generation, which I think is the interesting part.'

Estimates in five minutes

Estimates exist to choose between designs, not to be right. A number is worth computing only if it can change a decision: one database or many, cache or no cache, CDN or no CDN. Round aggressively (86,400 seconds in a day is about 100,000), keep units on everything, and finish each figure with its consequence.

Five inputs give you most of what you need:

  • Daily active users (DAU) and actions per user per day give average QPS: DAU × actions ÷ 86,400.
  • Peak QPS is the average times a peak factor, usually 2-5× for consumer products. Size for the peak.
  • The read:write ratio splits QPS into reads (cache and replica decisions) and writes (database and queue decisions).
  • Bytes per write × writes per day × retention × copies gives storage.
  • QPS × payload size gives bandwidth. Network capacity is quoted in bits, so multiply bytes by 8.

Estimate load, storage and bandwidth

Defaults describe the photo-sharing app from the model opening. Storage and bandwidth use decimal units (1 KB = 1,000 bytes), as you would in an interview. Change any input and watch which outputs move.

Say the 'so what' after every number

Read the defaults as a story. 50M users × 20 actions is a billion actions a day: about 11.6k per second on average and 35k at peak. Only 5% are writes, roughly 580 per second, which one database primary handles comfortably. But 33k reads per second at peak is more than one database serves, so the number, not habit, justifies a cache. Storage is about 18 TB a year; five years with three copies is about 270 TB, more than one node holds, so the data must be partitioned. Each sentence ends in a design decision. The next chapter, Back-of-envelope estimation, covers the reference numbers behind this.

API and data model

Write one line per core use case. Use REST-style resources unless the problem is clearly RPC or streaming. For each endpoint give the method, path, key parameters and the shape of the response, and add three details that signal experience:

  • Pagination for every list. Prefer a cursor (the last item seen) over an offset once data is large: it stays stable while new items arrive and costs the same at any depth.
  • Idempotency for writes a client may retry. A retried upload must not create two photos: use a client-generated ID or an Idempotency-Key header, or make the operation a PUT.
  • Who is calling comes from the auth token, never from a request field the caller could forge.

Four endpoints cover the core flows. Resist adding more until the interviewer asks.

For the data model, start from the queries, not the entities. List the reads and writes the API implies, then choose keys and stores that make each one a single lookup. Here: "a user's feed, newest first" is a range read on one partition key; "a photo by id" is a point lookup; "who do I follow" and "who follows me" are the two directions of one relation, so index both.

The high-level diagram, one justified box at a time

▶ Video lesson: Build the diagram, one justified box at a time 4:10 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A diagram is a list of decisions. Start with the smallest thing that could work (a client and a server) and add a component only when you can name the requirement or number that forces it. Say it as you draw: "one app server cannot give us failover, so a load balancer and at least two instances." That narration is the evidence the interviewer is grading. Run the two scenarios below and notice that the same method produces different diagrams for different requirements.

Build the diagram, justify every box

Each step adds one component and shows, under the box, the reason it exists. The read-heavy scenario ends with a cache and a CDN. The write-heavy scenario never gets either, because nothing forces them.

Once the picture is complete, trace one request through it aloud: a read and a write. It takes a minute and finds the gaps (an arrow with no owner, a store nobody writes to) before the interviewer does.

sequenceDiagram
  participant C as Client
  participant L as Load balancer
  participant A as App server
  participant R as Redis
  participant D as Database
  C->>L: GET /v1/feed?limit=20
  L->>A: forward to any healthy instance
  A->>R: GET feed:42
  R-->>A: miss
  A->>D: recent photos of followed users
  D-->>A: 20 rows
  A->>R: SET feed:42 with 60 s TTL
  A-->>L: 200 JSON
  L-->>C: 200 JSON
The feed read path on a cache miss. On a hit, the sequence stops after the Redis reply.

Then the write path: client → load balancer → app → (bytes to object storage, metadata to the database) → a message on the queue → workers that add the photo to each follower's cached feed. Saying "that last step is asynchronous, so the uploader does not wait for 500 followers' feeds to update" is exactly the kind of sentence that earns a follow-up question you want.

Choosing deep dives

After a complete, shallow design the interviewer usually steers ("tell me more about how the feed is generated"). If they do not, you choose, and the choice is graded. Decide in this order:

  1. Did the interviewer point at something? Go there. A hint is the cheapest signal you will get.
  2. What breaks first at your numbers? The component with the biggest number next to it: the hot partition, the 33k reads/s on one database, the fan-out of one post to a million followers.
  3. What is unique to this problem? Skip the generic parts (load balancer, auth). Ranking, fan-out, ID generation, transcoding, ordering, matching: the parts another system would not have.
  4. Where is your own depth? If two options tie, pick the one you can go deepest on.
flowchart TD
  Start[Complete, shallow design] --> Hint{Interviewer<br/>pointed somewhere?}
  Hint -->|yes| Go1[Go there first]
  Hint -->|no| Num{Any component over<br/>its limit at your numbers?}
  Num -->|yes| Go2[Deep dive on that bottleneck]
  Num -->|no| Uniq{A part unique<br/>to this problem?}
  Uniq -->|yes| Go3[Deep dive on that part]
  Uniq -->|no| Go4[Pick what you know best]
Walk the tree top to bottom; stop at the first 'yes'.

Every deep dive has the same four beats. Hit all four and it sounds like engineering rather than recitation:

  • Options: two or three realistic ways to do it.
  • Choice and why: tie the pick to a requirement or a number.
  • Failure modes: what happens when it breaks, is slow, or gets 10× the load.
  • Numbers: does it fit? (Fan-out on write for 200 followers is 200 inserts per post; for a celebrity with 20M followers it is not.)

Typical deep dives for classic prompts

News feed or timeline

Fan-out on write (precompute each follower's feed when a post arrives) makes reads cheap but multiplies writes by the follower count; fan-out on read does the opposite. Real systems mix them: precompute for ordinary users, merge celebrity posts at read time. Also ranking and the feed cache. See Case study: news feed.

URL shortener

ID generation (a counter handed out in ranges, a hash with collision handling, or pre-generated keys), 301 versus 302 redirects and what they do to caching and analytics, and a read path that is almost entirely cache hits. See Case study: URL shortener.

Chat

Holding millions of long-lived WebSocket connections, ordering within a conversation, delivery guarantees (at-least-once with client-side de-duplication), presence and offline sync. See Case study: chat application.

Video streaming

The upload and transcoding pipeline as a queue of independent jobs, adaptive-bitrate packaging, CDN placement and cost. See Case study: video streaming.

Rate limiter

The algorithm (token bucket versus sliding window), where counters live (local memory versus shared Redis), accuracy versus cost, and what happens when the counter store is down. See Reliability patterns.

Typeahead and search suggestions

Prefix lookup (a trie, or precomputed top-k lists per prefix), how often rankings are rebuilt, and caching the hottest prefixes.

Ride matching and nearby search

Geospatial indexing (geohash, quadtree, S2 cells), the write rate of location updates, and sharding by region.

Stating trade-offs

Every choice gives something and takes something, and the interviewer cannot hear a trade-off you did not say. Use a fixed sentence shape so you never leave a part out:

I'd pick A over B because [requirement or number]. It costs us [what we give up]. If [signal] changes, I'd move to B.

Assertion versus trade-off

Assertion

"I'll use NoSQL because it scales."

No requirement, no cost, no exit. It invites "why not Postgres?" and there is no answer to give.

Trade-off

"Feed reads are lookups by user id, about 33k a second at peak, with no joins. A partitioned key-value store serves that with predictable latency. We give up ad-hoc queries and multi-row transactions, so reporting goes to a separate warehouse. If product later needs rich queries over feed items, I'd revisit with a relational read model."

Red flags and self-review

Red flags interviewers write down (and the fix)

Drawing before asking

You solve a problem nobody posed. Fix: five minutes of scope and non-functional requirements, written down, before any box.

Numbers with no units or no 'so what'

'About 100k' of what, per second or per day? A figure that changes no decision is noise. Fix: units on everything, and finish with the consequence.

Technology name-dropping

'Kafka, Redis, Cassandra' with no reason attached. Fix: every technology comes with the requirement that forces it and what it costs.

Designing for a scale the numbers do not justify

Sharding a database that sees 50 writes a second. Fix: do the estimate first, start simple, and add complexity at the threshold where the number demands it.

A single point of failure nobody mentioned

One load balancer, one primary, one queue broker. Fix: for each box ask 'what if this dies?' and answer it, or say why it's acceptable.

Monologue

Ten minutes of talking without a pause. Fix: check in at phase boundaries ('does this match what you had in mind?').

Defensiveness

Arguing for the first design when the interviewer adds a new constraint. Fix: treat pushback as a new requirement and adapt the design out loud.

No failure modes

Everything works in the happy path. Fix: for each deep dive, say what happens when it breaks, slows down or gets 10× the traffic.

No wrap-up

The interview just stops. Fix: reserve five minutes for the summary, the risks and what you would monitor.

Self-review after a practice run

How to practise

Practise by taking one prompt a day with a 45-minute timer and a voice recorder. Replay the recording against the checklist above: the gaps you hear (long silences, unexplained boxes, missing numbers) are the ones the interviewer hears too.

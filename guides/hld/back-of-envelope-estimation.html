Back-of-envelope estimation

Estimation is how you decide, in five minutes, whether a design needs one database or fifty. This chapter gives you the numbers worth memorising (powers of two, the latency hierarchy, the nines of availability), the conversions that turn daily users into QPS, storage and bandwidth, three fully worked estimates you can adapt, and a way to say all of it out loud so the interviewer hears judgement rather than arithmetic.

After this chapter you can
  • Convert daily users and per-user actions into average and peak QPS using 86,400 s/day ≈ 10^5, and do it in your head
  • Recall powers of two and the latency hierarchy to within an order of magnitude, and use them to reject a slow design
  • Turn an availability target (the nines) into allowed downtime, and compute the availability of serial and redundant components
  • Estimate storage and bandwidth for a service, including replication, retention and peak factors
  • Present an estimate out loud: assumptions, rounded arithmetic, a sanity check, and the design decision the number drives

Why estimate, and how precise to be

▶ Video lesson: From daily users to QPS and storage 4:05 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

An estimate answers one question: does this design have to be different at this scale? One database or a sharded fleet, a cache or no cache, direct serving or a CDN, synchronous writes or a queue. Interviewers do not check your arithmetic. They check that you reach the right order of magnitude, notice which number matters, and let it change the design.

Aim to be within a factor of 2-3 and never off by 10. A number is worth computing only if a decision flips on it: if 5k and 50k QPS both lead to "one database plus a cache", do not spend a minute choosing between them.

Every estimate follows the same pipeline. Step through it once, then run it on real inputs with the calculators in the worked examples.

flowchart TD
  DAU[Daily active users] --> ACT[Actions per day]
  ACT --> QPS[Average QPS]
  QPS --> PEAK[Peak QPS]
  PEAK --> RW[Read QPS and write QPS]
  RW --> BW[Bandwidth]
  RW --> STO[Storage per day]
  STO --> TOT[Total storage]
The same eight boxes sit behind every capacity estimate. Only the inputs change.

What a strong estimate sounds like

The interviewer wants to hear the chain: assumption, rounded arithmetic, result with units, design consequence. Producing 11,574 instead of 'about 12k' earns nothing. Saying 'about 12k a second on average, 35k at peak, so one database cannot serve the reads' earns the point.

Numbers worth memorising: sizes and time

Four small tables cover almost every estimate you will do. Learn the first two cold and skim the others. Storage vendors and interviews use powers of ten (1 KB = 1,000 bytes) while memory uses powers of two (1 KiB = 1,024). Practise with a calculator you control

DAY = 86_400                      # seconds; use 1e5 when estimating in your head

def qps(per_day: float) -> float:
    return per_day / DAY

def human_bytes(n: float) -> str:
    for unit in ("B", "KB", "MB", "GB", "TB", "PB"):
        if n < 1000:
            return f"{n:.3g} {unit}"
        n /= 1000
    return f"{n:.3g} EB"

writes_per_day = 50_000_000
print(qps(writes_per_day))                           # about 579 writes per second
print(human_bytes(writes_per_day * 1_000 * 365))     # about 18 TB a year at 1 KB per write
  • Per day to per second: divide by 10^5 and add 15%. Or: 'X million a day is 12 × X per second'.
  • Multiply with exponents: 5 × 10^7 users × 2 × 10^1 actions = 10 × 10^8 = 10^9.
  • Round early: 86,400 → 10^5, 365 → 400 or 3 × 10^2, 1,024 → 1,000. Errors cancel more often than they add.
  • Sanity-check by another route: 1 TB a day is about 12 MB per second (10^12 ÷ 10^5), so roughly 100 Mbit/s.
  • Bits and bytes: 1 Gbit/s = 125 MB/s. Multiply bytes per second by 8 before comparing with a network link.

Latency numbers

▶ Video lesson: Latency numbers: add up a request 4:11 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Latency numbers decide what a design can possibly do. You do not need exact values; you need the order of magnitude of each level and the ratios between levels. The right-hand column rescales the table so that an L1 cache hit takes one second, which makes the ratios feel real.

Operation≈ TimeNanosecondsIf an L1 hit took 1 second
L1 cache reference~1 ns11 second
L2 cache reference~5 ns55 seconds
Main memory (RAM) reference~100 ns100about 2 minutes
SSD random read (4 KB)~100 µs100,000about 1 day
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD~1 ms1,000,000about 12 days
Round trip inside one data centre~0.5 ms500,000about 6 days
Disk (HDD) seek~10 ms10,000,000about 4 months
Read 1 MB sequentially from HDD~10 ms10,000,000about 4 months
Round trip across continents~150 ms150,000,000about 5 years
Order-of-magnitude figures that drift with hardware and year. Use them for ratios, not for benchmarks.

What the table tells you:

  • Memory versus SSD is about 1,000×. Whether the working set fits in RAM is the first question about any data store, and the reason caches exist.
  • A network hop costs about 0.5 ms inside a data centre, or roughly 5,000 memory references. Count the hops on a request path: 20 sequential calls is 10 ms of pure waiting before any work is done. Parallelise or batch them.
  • Sequential beats random. Reading 1 MB sequentially from a spinning disk takes about as long as a single seek. That is why logs, B-trees and LSM-trees are built around sequential I/O.
  • Crossing continents costs 300× a hop inside the data centre. This is why synchronous cross-region calls stay off the hot path, and why CDNs and regional deployments exist.

Walk up the latency ladder

Step through the levels. Bar height is log10 of the time in nanoseconds, with the real value above each bar and dashed lines at 1 µs, 1 ms and 1 s. Switch scenarios to see why the table needs a log scale.

Use the numbers by adding up a request. The diagram below walks one cache-miss read from a phone and keeps a running total of the time spent inside our servers.

Server-side work totals about 8.5 ms; the phone's network adds about 40 ms. Know which part you can change.

Latency answers are sums, not adjectives

When asked 'how fast would this be?', add up the hops like this. It shows you know which numbers matter: the answer is rarely 'make Redis faster'. State the user-visible target (say p99 under 300 ms), the budget per hop, and which hop dominates.

Availability and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) in which the system works. It is quoted in "nines", and you should convert it to downtime so it feels real. Each extra nine means ten times less allowed downtime and often much more than ten times the effort.

AvailabilityPer yearPer month (30 days)Per week
99% (two nines)3.65 days7.2 hours1.68 hours
99.9% (three nines)8.76 hours43.2 minutes10.1 minutes
99.95%4.38 hours21.6 minutes5.04 minutes
99.99% (four nines)52.6 minutes4.32 minutes1.01 minutes
99.999% (five nines)5.26 minutes25.9 seconds6.05 seconds
Memorise one row: three nines is about 9 hours a year, 43 minutes a month. The rest scale by factors of ten.

Two rules turn component numbers into a system number:

  • In series (every request needs all of them), availabilities multiply. Three components at 99.9% give 0.999³ ≈ 99.7%, which is about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 9.
  • In parallel (any one is enough), unavailabilities multiply. Two independent replicas at 99% give 1 − 0.01² = 99.99%. This assumes failures are independent and failover is instant. A shared rack, a shared deploy or a shared bug breaks both assumptions, which is why real redundancy spans zones and why bad releases cause correlated outages.
flowchart LR
  Users[Users] --> LB[Load balancer<br/>99.99%]
  LB --> A1[App A<br/>99.9%]
  LB --> A2[App B<br/>99.9%]
  A1 --> DB[(Database<br/>99.95%)]
  A2 --> DB
The weakest element that sits in series with everything else caps the whole system.

From nines to downtime, and chains of components

Set a target and see the allowed downtime. Then set how many components sit in series, or how many replicas run in parallel, and see what the chain delivers. Parallel results assume independent failures and instant failover.

Four terms come up whenever availability does:

  • SLI (service level indicator): the measurement, such as the fraction of requests that return without a server error in under 300 ms.
  • SLO (objective): the internal target for that indicator, such as 99.9% over 30 days.
  • SLA (agreement): the contract with penalties, usually looser than the SLO.
  • Error budget: 1 − SLO. At 99.9% over 30 days it is 43.2 minutes of failure to spend on releases and incidents.

How to use this in an interview

Say what 'down' means for your system (errors? latency above a threshold? a single feature?) and whether you measure time or requests. Then name the target and its consequence: at three nines you need redundancy at every tier and no single point of failure; each further nine adds zones, regions, automated failover and operational discipline, and costs disproportionately more.

Worked estimate 1: a Twitter-like timeline

The assumptions below are illustrative, not any company's real figures. Say them out loud before computing:

  • 150 million daily active users, each opening their timeline about 10 times a day.
  • An average user posts 0.5 times a day (most people post nothing, a few post constantly).
  • Each user has about 200 followers on average.
  • A post is about 500 bytes of text and metadata; 10% of posts carry a photo of about 300 KB.
  • We keep the latest 800 post IDs of every active user's timeline in memory, 16 bytes per entry.

The interesting number is not read QPS. It is what happens to writes when each post is copied into every follower's timeline.

Timeline service: QPS, fan-out, storage and cache size

Change followers per user or the share of posts with media and see which outputs react. Storage uses decimal units.

flowchart LR
  Post["New posts<br/>868 per second"] -->|"x 200 followers"| Fan[Fan-out workers]
  Fan -->|"174k inserts per second"| TL[(Follower timelines<br/>in memory)]
  Read["Timeline reads<br/>17k per second"] --> TL
868 posts a second become 174 thousand timeline inserts a second. The write path, not the read path, is the hard part.

What the numbers decide

Four decisions fall out of these numbers. (1) 52k reads/s at peak means timelines are served from memory, not computed per request. (2) 2.6k posts/s is small, but 520k timeline inserts/s is not, so inserts go through a queue and workers, partitioned by user ID. (3) Text is about 14 TB a year and fits a partitioned store, while media is about 0.8 PB a year and belongs in object storage behind a CDN. (4) An account with 20 million followers turns one post into 20 million inserts, so very popular accounts are handled differently (their posts are merged in at read time). See Case study: news feed.

Worked estimate 2: a URL shortener for ten years

A long-retention estimate is mostly about storage and key space. Assumptions (illustrative):

  • 500 million new short URLs a month.
  • 100 redirects for every URL created (read:write = 100:1).
  • Every URL is kept for 10 years.
  • A record is about 500 bytes: the long URL (a hundred or two bytes on average, occasionally much longer), the short key, creation time, owner and expiry.
  • Three copies of the data, and a peak of 3× the average.

URL shortener: QPS, ten-year storage, key length and cache size

The key-length output answers 'how many characters does the short code need?'. The cache output is an upper bound: it counts repeat redirects of the same URL as if each were a different key.

Reading the results:

  • Writes are tiny: about 190 creates a second. One database node handles that; the interesting part of the write path is generating unique keys, not throughput.
  • Reads are the load: about 19k redirects a second on average and 58k at peak. That is far beyond one database, so redirects must be served from a cache with the database behind it.
  • Storage is modest: 30 TB of data, 90 TB with three copies. Spread across a handful of nodes, capacity is not the constraint.
  • Key length comes from the count, not from taste. Sixty billion URLs need 7 base-62 characters: 62^6 is about 57 billion (just too small) and 62^7 is about 3.5 trillion, which leaves roughly 60× headroom.
  • Cache size is around 170 GB for the hottest fifth of a day's redirects, which fits a few cache nodes. Because repeated redirects of the same URL count several times in that figure, the real set of distinct hot keys is smaller.

Name the input the answer is sensitive to

Notice what dominated each decision: the 100:1 ratio forced the cache; the retention period and URL count set the key length; the write rate forced nothing. Saying that out loud shows you know which input the design is sensitive to. More in Case study: URL shortener.

Worked estimate 3: video uploads

Video is where bandwidth and storage dominate everything else. Assumptions (illustrative, not any company's figures):

  • 500,000 videos uploaded a day, 5 minutes long on average.
  • The original is about 60 MB per minute (roughly 8 Mbit/s).
  • We keep the original plus a ladder of renditions from 240p to 1080p, about 2.5× the original's size in total.
  • Transcoding makes 5 renditions per video, and one CPU core encodes 2 minutes of video per minute for one rendition.
  • Viewing: 500 million views a day, 4 minutes watched per view, at an average of 2.5 Mbit/s.

Video platform: upload bandwidth, storage, transcoding and egress

Bandwidth is in bits per second, storage in decimal units. The last output is the ratio that explains why video platforms are built around a CDN.

flowchart LR
  Phone[Uploader] -->|"14 Gbit/s avg"| Ingest[Upload service]
  Ingest --> Raw[(Object store<br/>originals)]
  Raw --> Q{{Transcode queue}}
  Q --> TC[Transcoder fleet]
  TC --> Out[(Object store<br/>renditions)]
  Out --> CDN[CDN]
  CDN -->|"3.5 Tbit/s avg"| Viewers[Viewers]
Each stage carries a number from the calculator. The two bandwidth figures are 250× apart.

What the numbers decide

(1) Egress is 250× ingress, so a CDN is mandatory, not an optimisation. (2) About 137 PB a year means object storage with tiering and an explicit retention policy. (3) Transcoding is the compute hot spot: a queue plus autoscaled workers. (4) Uploads are large and bursty, so use resumable multipart uploads direct to storage. See Case study: video streaming and Object storage (S3) & CDNs.

From numbers to design decisions

An estimate earns its place when it points at a component. Take the photo-sharing app from How to run a system design interview: about 35k requests a second at peak, 95% of them reads. Saying it out loud

Rounding rules that keep the arithmetic fast and the answer honest:

  • Round to one significant figure while you calculate and keep powers of ten visible: 5 × 10^7 users, not 49,730,000.
  • Use 10^5 seconds a day (86,400 if you want to look careful), 2.5 × 10^6 a month and 3 × 10^7 a year.
  • Convert to per-second early, and back to per-day or per-year only for storage.
  • Keep units on every figure. "100k" is noise; "100k requests per second" is a claim.
  • Round in the safe direction: up for load and storage, and down for latency budgets you want to meet.
  • Check by a second route. 580 writes a second × 1 KB is about 0.6 MB a second; times 86,400 should land near 50 GB a day, which matches 50 million writes × 1 KB.
  • Name the dominant assumption. "Everything here hinges on the 20:1 read ratio; at 200:1 the cache matters even more."

A model estimate, said aloud

'I'll assume 50 million daily users doing 20 actions each, so about a billion actions a day. Dividing by roughly a hundred thousand seconds gives about 12 thousand a second on average and call it 35 thousand at peak, 95% of them reads. One database will not serve 33 thousand reads a second, so a cache goes in front. For storage, 50 million writes a day at 1 KB is 50 GB a day, about 18 TB a year; with three copies and five years that is around 270 TB, so the data will be partitioned.' Four beats: assumption, rounded arithmetic, result with units, the decision it drives.

Hand-waving versus a structured estimate

Hand-waving

"It's a big system, probably millions of QPS, so we'll need lots of servers and a huge database."

No inputs, no units, no consequence. It cannot be corrected, and it may be off by 100×.

Structured

"1 billion actions a day is about 12k a second, 35k at peak. 95% are reads, so 33k reads a second: that needs a cache. Writes are under 2k a second: one primary is fine."

Four sentences, every figure with a unit, and each one ends in a decision.

Common mistakes and the fix

Mistakes

  • Using total or monthly users where daily actives are needed
  • Sizing for the average and forgetting the peak
  • Counting one copy of the data, with no retention and no indexes
  • Comparing MB/s with a link quoted in Gbit/s
  • Dividing by 1,000 or 10^6 instead of about 10^5 seconds a day
  • Reporting 11,574.07 requests a second
  • Computing numbers that change no decision
  • Never stating the assumptions

Instead

  • State a DAU assumption (20-50% of monthly actives is common for engaged apps)
  • Apply a peak factor of 2-5 and say it
  • Multiply by retention and copies, and leave room for indexes
  • Multiply bytes by 8 before comparing with network capacity
  • Use 10^5, or 'a million a day is a dozen a second'
  • Say "about 12k" and move on
  • Stop as soon as the design decision is clear
  • Say each assumption aloud so the interviewer can correct it

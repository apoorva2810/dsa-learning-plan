Case study: news feed

Design the home feed of a Twitter- or Instagram-style app: when someone posts, their followers see it within seconds, and loading the feed takes a few hundred milliseconds. You will size fan-out from users, posts and followers, watch fan-out on write and fan-out on read handle an ordinary account and a celebrity, store each feed as a capped sorted set, paginate with cursors, rank within a latency budget, make sure authors see their own posts at once, and finish with a five-sentence answer.

After this chapter you can
  • Run the interview script on a news feed: requirements, estimates, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, trade-offs
  • Estimate feed loads, posts and the write amplification of fan-out on write, and compare it with the reads of fan-out on read
  • Explain the celebrity problem and apply the hybrid rule: push for ordinary accounts, pull for accounts with millions of followers
  • Store a feed as a capped sorted set of post ids, hydrate ids into posts, and paginate with an opaque cursor
  • Add ranking without breaking the latency budget, serve media through a CDN, and guarantee authors see their own posts immediately

Requirements and scope

A news feed is the screen you see when you open Twitter, Instagram or LinkedIn: recent posts from the accounts you follow, newest or most interesting first. The product looks simple. The engineering problem is amplification: one post must reach every follower's feed, and follower counts range from three to a hundred million. Everything in the design follows from where you pay for that amplification: when the post is written, or when the feed is read.

Fix the scope first. Say the checklist out loud and write down the numbers you assume.

Requirements to confirm with the interviewer

The interviewer is waiting for one realisation: follower counts are wildly skewed, so the design that works for an account with 200 followers breaks for one with 100 million. Ask early how big the largest accounts are, how fresh the feed must be, whether it is ranked or chronological, and the ratio of feed loads to posts. Then say where you will pay for fan-out, and why.

Estimates that shape the design

Every follow edge has one follower and one followee, so the average user follows as many accounts as the average account has followers. That makes the two strategies easy to compare: pushing costs followers writes per post, pulling costs following reads per feed load. The defaults are illustrative.

Feed loads, fan-out and cache size

The two 'operations per second' rows compare the strategies directly. The celebrity row is one post from the biggest account, pushed to every follower.

Read the outputs as decisions:

  • About 23,000 feed loads a second, 70,000 at peak. The feed load is the hot path, so it must be served from memory, not computed from a database on each request.
  • About 1,200 posts a second is small. The post store is not where the design gets hard.
  • Push costs about 250,000 cache operations a second; pull costs about 4.6 million, roughly 18 times more, and pull's cost lands on the latency-critical read path. Because people load feeds far more often than they post, fan-out on write is the default.
  • One post from a 100-million-follower account takes about 100 seconds to push and occupies the whole fan-out tier while it runs. That single number is the celebrity problem, and it forces the hybrid.
  • About 6.4 TB of feed cache for active users: a sharded cache cluster keyed by user id, with a hard cap on feed length. Pages need about 1.4 million post lookups a second at peak, so post bodies get their own cache.
  • About 6 TB of images a day belongs in object storage behind a CDN, never on the feed path.

The feed returns hydrated posts plus an opaque cursor. Follow is a PUT, so repeating it is harmless.

Three details are worth saying. The post id is a time-sortable 64-bit id in the style of Twitter's Snowflake: 41 bits of milliseconds, 10 bits of machine id, 12 bits of sequence. Ids generated on different machines sort by creation time without coordination. The cursor is opaque (base64 of the last item's score and id here), so the server can change its format without breaking clients. And follow is a PUT on a resource, so a retried request changes nothing.

The social graph is stored twice: by follower ("whom do I follow", read when building a feed) and by followee ("who follows me", read by fan-out), each sharded by its first column. The feed itself is not a table at all: it is a sorted set per user in the cache, holding post ids, not post bodies.

This is pure fan-out on write, and it is correct for nearly every account. It breaks for a few hundred accounts out of hundreds of millions: the ones with millions of followers. The first deep dive shows exactly how, and the fix adds one arrow to the read path.

Deep dive 1: fan-out on write, fan-out on read, and celebrities

▶ Video lesson: Push, pull and the celebrity problem 3:57 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Fan-out on write (push) precomputes every feed: when a post is created, its id is inserted into each follower's feed. Reads become trivial. Fan-out on read (pull) stores each post only in its author's timeline and assembles a feed when someone opens the app, by merging the timelines of everyone they follow. Writes become trivial.

In the push path, the fan-out queue is a log partitioned by author id, so one author's posts fan out in order, and many workers share the load. Delivery is at-least-once, which is safe here because inserting the same post id into a sorted set twice leaves one entry (Message queues & streaming (Kafka) covers partitions, consumer groups and delivery guarantees). Follow one post through the push path first.

Fan-out on write: an ordinary account, a celebrity, and the hybrid rule

The counter on the workers is feed inserts so far; the counter on the queue is other posts waiting. Start with an ordinary account, then push a post from an account with 100 million followers, then apply the hybrid rule to it.

The hybrid rule

Push for ordinary accounts, pull for celebrities, and merge the two at read time. The rule is per author, and it is the design almost every large feed ends up with. A useful way to justify the threshold: for one follower and one author, push costs a write each time the author posts, and pull costs a read each time the follower loads the feed. Push is cheaper whenever the author posts less often than the follower reads, which holds for most pairs. The follower-count threshold adds a second reason: a single 100-million-write burst delays every other post.

flowchart TD
  NP[New post] --> Q1{Author has 1M or more<br/>followers?}
  Q1 -- yes --> TLO[Append to author timeline only]
  Q1 -- no --> FAN[Fan out to each follower]
  FAN --> Q2{Follower seen in<br/>last 30 days?}
  Q2 -- yes --> ZA[ZADD to follower feed]
  Q2 -- no --> SK[Skip: rebuild on next visit]
  TLO --> MR[Merged into feeds at read time]
Push by default, pull for the few accounts whose follower count makes pushing slow, and never write to feeds nobody will read.
stateDiagram-v2

  Push --> Pull: followers reach 1M

The mode is a flag on the account. Two thresholds instead of one stop an account near the line from flipping back and forth.

Deep dive 2: the feed cache, hydration and pagination

▶ Video lesson: Reading the feed: one sorted set, a merge and a cursor 4:26 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A sorted set per user

Each feed is a Redis sorted set feed:{user_id}: members are post ids, scores are creation times in milliseconds. Insertion is O(log n), reading the newest 20 is O(log n + 20), and the set keeps itself ordered even if fan-out delivers posts out of order. Three rules keep it healthy (Caching covers the cache itself):

  • Cap it. After each insert, trim to the newest 800 entries. Memory stays bounded; anyone scrolling further back gets an on-demand rebuild from author timelines.
  • Store ids, not posts. An id is a few bytes, and edits and deletes need no fan-out because the post body is read at display time.
  • Do not use a 64-bit id as the score. Scores are 64-bit floating-point numbers, exact only for integers up to 2^53. A Snowflake id is larger than that, so two ids can collapse to one score. Use the millisecond timestamp (well inside 2^53) as the score and the id as the member; equal scores are ordered by member.

The hybrid read path: one precomputed list, a few celebrity timelines, one batched hydration.

Reading the feed: pull, hybrid, and the next page

The counters on the stores are reads made for this one page. Compare pure fan-out on read with the hybrid for the same 6 followed accounts, then scroll to page 2 after a new post has arrived.

Push, trim and read a page with a cursor (redis-py)

import base64
import json

import redis

r = redis.Redis()
FEED_LEN = 800


def push_to_feeds(post_id: str, created_ms: int, follower_ids: list[str]) -> None:
    """Fan-out on write for one page of followers: one pipelined round trip."""
    pipe = r.pipeline(transaction=False)
    for uid in follower_ids:
        key = f"feed:{uid}"
        pipe.zadd(key, {post_id: created_ms})            # idempotent: a redelivered event re-adds the same member
        pipe.zremrangebyrank(key, 0, -(FEED_LEN + 1))    # keep only the newest 800
    pipe.execute()


def encode_cursor(score: int, post_id: str) -> str:
    return base64.urlsafe_b64encode(json.dumps([score, post_id]).encode()).decode().rstrip("=")


def decode_cursor(cursor: str) -> tuple[int, str]:
    padded = cursor + "=" * (-len(cursor) % 4)
    score, post_id = json.loads(base64.urlsafe_b64decode(padded))
    return int(score), post_id


def read_page(user_id: str, limit: int = 20, cursor: str | None = None) -> tuple[list[str], str | None]:
    key = f"feed:{user_id}"
    if cursor is None:
        rows = r.zrevrangebyscore(key, "+inf", "-inf", start=0, num=limit, withscores=True)
    else:
        score, last_id = decode_cursor(cursor)
        # include the cursor's own score (two posts can share a millisecond), then drop what was already shown;
        # equal scores come back in descending member order
        rows = r.zrevrangebyscore(key, score, "-inf", start=0, num=limit + 10, withscores=True)
        rows = [(m, s) for m, s in rows if s < score or m.decode() < last_id][:limit]
    ids = [m.decode() for m, _ in rows]
    next_cursor = encode_cursor(int(rows[-1][1]), ids[-1]) if len(rows) == limit else None
    return ids, next_cursor
Score = milliseconds, member = id. The cursor names a point in the set, so inserts at the top never shift a page.

Offset or cursor?

Offset: ?page=2 means items 20 to 39

Simple, and wrong for a feed. New posts arrive at the top between two requests, so everything shifts down and page 2 repeats the end of page 1; a deletion makes it skip a post. Deep offsets also cost more, because the store must count past every earlier item.

Cursor: 'older than this item'

The response carries an opaque cursor encoding the last item's score and id. The next page asks for items strictly older than that point, which is O(log n + page) in a sorted set. New posts cannot shift it, and the client asks for 'newer than my first item' when the user pulls to refresh.

The three strategies in code

Skip celebrities and inactive followers; page through follower ids so a large list is never held in memory.

CELEBRITY_THRESHOLD = 1_000_000
ACTIVE_MS = 30 * 86_400_000


def handle_post_created(event: dict, users, graph, feeds) -> None:
    author = users.get(event["author_id"])
    if author.follower_count >= CELEBRITY_THRESHOLD:
        return                                              # pulled at read time instead
    cutoff = event["created_ms"] - ACTIVE_MS
    for page in graph.followers(author.user_id, page_size=5_000):      # [(user_id, last_seen_ms)]
        active = [uid for uid, last_seen_ms in page if last_seen_ms >= cutoff]
        feeds.push_many(event["post_id"], event["created_ms"], active)  # pipelined ZADD + trim

Each author's timeline is already sorted newest first, so building a feed is a k-way merge. This is Merge k Sorted Lists with real data.

import heapq
from itertools import islice


def build_feed(user_id: str, graph, timelines, limit: int = 20) -> list[tuple[int, str]]:
    lists = [timelines.recent(author, n=limit) for author in graph.following(user_id)]   # [(ms, post_id)] newest first
    merged = heapq.merge(*lists, key=lambda item: item[0], reverse=True)
    return list(islice(merged, limit))

One precomputed list plus a handful of celebrity timelines, merged the same way and de-duplicated, because an account that recently switched mode can appear in both.

import heapq
from itertools import islice


def read_feed(user_id: str, feeds, graph, timelines, limit: int = 20) -> list[tuple[int, str]]:
    pushed = feeds.newest(user_id, n=200)                                   # one ZREVRANGE: [(ms, post_id)]
    pulled = [timelines.recent(c, n=limit) for c in graph.celebrities_followed(user_id)]
    seen: set[str] = set()
    page = []
    for ms, post_id in heapq.merge(pushed, *pulled, key=lambda item: item[0], reverse=True):
        if post_id not in seen:
            seen.add(post_id)
            page.append((ms, post_id))
        if len(page) == limit:
            break
    return page

Deep dive 3: ranking and media

Chronological or ranked

A chronological feed is the merge you just watched: newest first, no model, fully predictable, and the easiest to cache and paginate. A ranked feed orders candidates by predicted interest. It usually raises engagement, but it adds a scoring step to every load and makes the order change between visits. Many apps rank by default and offer a 'latest' view.

Ranking is one more stage in the same pipeline, and the latency budget decides how big it can be. If a page must load in 200 ms at p99, scoring may get around 50 ms: enough for a small model over a few hundred candidates, not a large one over thousands. Expensive work (author affinity, post embeddings, spam scores) is precomputed offline, and the online step combines precomputed features.

flowchart LR
  FCX[(Pushed ids)] --> CAN[Candidates<br/>about 500]
  CTL[(Celebrity timelines)] --> CAN
  RCM[Recommended posts] --> CAN
  CAN --> FEAT[Fetch features]
  FEAT --> SCR[Score with a model]
  SCR --> RR[Re-rank rules]
  RR --> SNAP[(Ranked snapshot<br/>per session)]
  SNAP --> PG[Page of 20]
Ranking consumes the same candidates the chronological feed would show, plus recommendations. The snapshot is what makes cursors work on a ranked feed.

Inside a stored snapshot an offset is safe, because the snapshot never changes; the cursor is the snapshot id plus a position. Without a snapshot, the ranked order would shift between requests and pages would repeat or skip posts.

Media goes around the feed, not through it

Images and videos are uploaded before the post, straight to object storage with a presigned URL. A worker resizes each image into a few widths (and transcodes video, as in Case study: video streaming), and the post stores only media ids. The feed response contains CDN URLs; the client downloads the bytes from the nearest edge (Object storage (S3) & CDNs). The feed service never touches a byte of media, which keeps a feed page a few kilobytes of JSON.

flowchart LR
  APP[Author app] -->|"1. ask for upload URL"| MS[Media service]
  APP -->|"2. PUT the bytes"| OBJ[(Object storage)]
  OBJ -->|"3. upload event"| RSZ[Resize workers]
  RSZ -->|"4. 320, 720, 1080 px"| OBJ
  VWR[Follower app] -->|"5. GET image URL"| CDN[CDN edge]
  CDN -->|"miss only"| OBJ
The post holds media ids; the feed holds post ids. Bytes go in through a presigned URL and out through the CDN.

Deep dive 4: consistency and edge cases

A feed is eventually consistent by design: followers see a post when fan-out reaches them, a few seconds later. That is fine for followers and unacceptable for one person, the author. Each event below has a specific rule.

A post is editedthe new textnothing to do: feeds hold ids, hydration reads the current version
You unfollowno new posts from themstop future pushes, filter by the current follow set at read time, clean up in the background
You followtheir recent posts soonbackfill their last few posts into your feed asynchronously

The feed cache can always be rebuilt from the post store and the graph. That is what makes it a cache.

If asked 'what consistency do you offer?', answer per person: read-your-writes for the author (their own post is written to their feed synchronously), eventual consistency for followers (seconds of fan-out lag), and stable pagination (cursors, so scrolling never repeats or skips a post). Then say what you would measure: fan-out lag at p99, from post creation to the post appearing in follower feeds.

What interviewers ask next

Where exactly should the celebrity threshold be?

It is a cost and latency knob, not a constant. For one follower and one author, push costs a write per post and pull costs a read per feed load, so push wins when the author posts less often than the follower reads (the result of the 'Feeding Frenzy' paper in the references). The follower-count threshold caps the size of a single fan-out burst. Tune both from fan-out lag and cache load, with hysteresis between them.

What about a user who follows 5,000 accounts?

Push handles them well: their feed is precomputed, and the cost sits with the authors. The read gets expensive only if they follow many celebrities. Cap the number of celebrity timelines merged per load, or precompute a per-user 'celebrity digest' every minute.

A celebrity post goes viral. What gets hot?

timeline:@star and the post's body in the post cache are read by every follower's feed load. Replicate those keys across shards and keep them in each feed-service instance's memory for a second or two. Like counts come from a counter service with batched increments, not from the post row.

How do you store the social graph?

Two adjacency tables, followers-of and following, each sharded by its first column, plus a separate follower count. Feeds only need one hop, so a graph database is unnecessary. Celebrity follower lists are paged, never loaded whole.

How do like and reply counts stay fresh in the feed?

They are not stored in feeds. Hydration reads them from a counter cache that aggregators update in batches, the same pattern as view counts in Case study: video streaming. A count that is a few seconds old is fine.

How would you add ads or recommended posts?

As another candidate source in the ranking pipeline. Re-rank rules place them at fixed positions (for example every fifth slot) and enforce frequency caps.

How does this work across regions?

Each region keeps feed caches for the users homed there. Posts replicate between regions asynchronously, and fan-out runs in every region that has followers. The author's own post is written in their home region first, which is what keeps read-your-writes for them.

What do you monitor?

Feed load latency at p50 and p99, fan-out lag at p99 (post created to post in follower feeds), fan-out queue depth, post-cache hit ratio, feed-cache memory per shard, and the share of loads that had to rebuild a feed.

A five-sentence model answer

'Feeds are read about twenty times as often as people post, so I would precompute them: a post is written once to a sharded post store, and fan-out workers push its id into every active follower's feed, a Redis sorted set capped at 800 entries, so loading the feed is one cache read plus a batched lookup of post bodies. At 200 million daily users that is about 1,200 posts and 23,000 feed loads a second, and pushing costs about 230,000 feed inserts a second, far less than the 4.6 million reads a second that pulling would cost. The exception is accounts with millions of followers, where one post would take about 100 seconds to push and stall everyone else's fan-out, so those posts stay in the author's timeline and are merged into feeds at read time, the hybrid most large feeds use. Pages use an opaque cursor holding the last score and id so new posts never shift a page, ranking is one more stage over a few hundred candidates within the latency budget, and media is served from a CDN by URL. Authors get read-your-writes because their post is written to their own feed synchronously, followers see it within seconds, and I would watch feed-load p99, fan-out lag and queue depth.'

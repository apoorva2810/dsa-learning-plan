Reliability patterns

Distributed systems fail partially: one dependency slows down, a reply is lost, a burst arrives at once. This chapter turns each of those failures into a pattern you can draw and defend: timeouts with deadlines, retries with exponential backoff and jitter, idempotency keys, circuit breakers, rate limiters, bulkheads, load shedding, graceful degradation, health checks, and SLOs with error budgets. Every system design deep dive eventually asks 'what happens when this is slow or down?', and a strong answer names the pattern, its parameters and the failure it prevents.

After this chapter you can
  • Set timeouts from latency percentiles and propagate a deadline across service hops
  • Retry only safe, transient failures, with capped exponential backoff, full jitter and a retry budget
  • Make a payment safe to retry with an idempotency key, including the concurrent-duplicate case
  • Explain the circuit breaker's three states and choose its thresholds and fallback
  • Compare token bucket, fixed window and sliding window limiters, and say where the counters live
  • Turn an SLO into an error budget and allowed downtime, and use the burn rate to decide when to page

Partial failure and where the patterns sit

A single machine usually fails all at once. A distributed system fails partially: the payments API answers in 9 seconds instead of 90 ms, one replica in twelve returns errors, a network hop drops one reply in a thousand. Partial failures are the dangerous ones. A slow dependency does not just fail its own requests: every caller waiting on it holds a thread, a connection and some memory, and when those run out the caller fails too, and then its callers. That chain is a cascading failure, and many large outages are one.

The patterns fall into three groups:

  • Protect yourself from your dependencies: timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads and fallbacks.
  • Protect yourself from your callers: rate limiting and load shedding.
  • Know where you stand: health checks, and SLOs with error budgets that say how much failure is acceptable.

Idempotency keys sit underneath all of them: they are what makes a retry safe in the first place.

When an interviewer asks 'what if the payments service is down?' or 'what if traffic triples?', they are testing whether you think about partial failure. Answer with a mechanism and a number: 'calls to payments get a 2 s timeout and a circuit breaker that opens at 50% errors; the app retries twice with jittered backoff behind an idempotency key; while the breaker is open, the order is queued for later', not 'we add retries'.

Timeouts, retries, backoff and jitter

▶ Video lesson: Timeouts, deadlines and retries that do not storm 4:43 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Timeouts and deadlines

Every network call needs a timeout, because without one a single hung dependency can hold a thread forever. Library defaults are rarely right: Python's requests waits indefinitely unless you pass timeout=, and many clients default to 30 s or more.

  • Set the timeout from the dependency's measured latency: a little above its p99.9 (p99.9 of 300 ms suggests a 400-500 ms timeout). Too short fails requests that would have succeeded; too long holds resources for answers nobody is waiting for.
  • Use a short connect timeout (a few hundred milliseconds) and a separate request timeout.
  • Give the whole user request a deadline and pass the remaining time to every downstream call. No service should keep working on a request its caller has already abandoned.

The budget shrinks at every hop, so every layer gives up at about the same moment instead of working for a user who left.

Retries

Many failures are transient: a lost packet, a leader election, one overloaded replica. A retry turns them into successes. A retry is also extra load that arrives exactly when the system is least able to take it, so retry with discipline:

  • Retry only what is safe and transient. Safe means reads, idempotent writes, or writes with an idempotency key (next section). Transient means timeouts, connection resets, 502/503/504, and 429 after waiting as long as Retry-After says. A 400, 401, 403, 404 or 422 fails the same way every time.
  • Cap the attempts (two or three retries) and stop when the deadline is near.
  • Retry at one layer. If A calls B, B calls C, and each makes 3 attempts, C can receive 3 × 3 = 9 calls per request to A, and a fourth layer makes it 27. Retry near the edge and let inner layers fail fast.
  • Keep a retry budget. Allow retries up to about 10% of a client's requests; beyond that, fail. During an outage this caps the extra load at about 1.1× instead of 3× or 4×.
  • Back off exponentially, with jitter. Before retry n, wait up to base × 2^n, capped (100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms, up to a few seconds), so a struggling server gets more room with each failure. Then randomise the wait. With full jitter the client sleeps a random time between 0 and that value, which spreads out clients that failed together.
flowchart TD
  E[Call failed] --> S{Safe to repeat?<br/>read, idempotent or keyed}
  S -- no --> F[Fail or use fallback]
  S -- yes --> T{Transient error?<br/>timeout, 503, 429}
  T -- no --> F
  T -- yes --> B{Attempts, deadline<br/>and budget left?}

Three questions before every retry. A 'no' to any of them means fail now, or serve a fallback.

A retry storm, without and with jitter

Six clients fail together during a short outage, then retry with the same capped exponential backoff. Watch the bars against the capacity line: without jitter the retries arrive in waves the recovered server cannot absorb.

A retry decorator: capped exponential backoff, full jitter, and a deadline

import functools
import random
import time


class TransientError(Exception):
    """Raise for 502/503/504, and for 429 after honouring Retry-After."""


RETRYABLE = (TimeoutError, ConnectionError, TransientError)


def retry(max_attempts: int = 3, base: float = 0.1, cap: float = 2.0, deadline: float | None = None):
    def decorate(fn):
        @functools.wraps(fn)
        def wrapper(*args, **kwargs):
            give_up_at = time.monotonic() + deadline if deadline else float("inf")
            for attempt in range(max_attempts):
                try:
                    return fn(*args, **kwargs)
                except RETRYABLE:
                    if attempt == max_attempts - 1:
                        raise                                           # out of attempts
                    sleep = random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt))   # full jitter
                    if time.monotonic() + sleep >= give_up_at:
                        raise                                           # no time left for another try
                    time.sleep(sleep)
        return wrapper
    return decorate


@retry(max_attempts=3, base=0.2, deadline=2.0)
def fetch_profile(user_id: int) -> dict:
    return http_get(f"https://profiles.internal/users/{user_id}", timeout=0.5)   # per-attempt timeout
Only listed exception types are retried, so a 400 or a bug fails immediately. Production versions add a retry budget and metrics.

A retry of a request that is not idempotent is how customers get charged twice. Before adding a retry anywhere, ask: if the first attempt actually succeeded and only the reply was lost, what does the second attempt do?

Idempotency keys

▶ Video lesson: Idempotency keys: charge once, even when you retry 4:07 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A timeout tells the client one thing: I do not know what happened. The server may never have seen the request, or it may have charged the card and lost the reply. Retrying a read is harmless; retrying POST /payments unprotected can charge twice.

An idempotency key makes a non-idempotent operation safe to repeat. The client generates a unique key (a UUID) for each logical operation, sends it in a header such as Idempotency-Key, and reuses the same key for every retry of that operation. The server remembers each key with a fingerprint of the request and the response it gave, and answers a repeat with the stored response instead of doing the work again. The client chooses the key because only the client knows that two requests are the same attempt.

sequenceDiagram
  participant C as Checkout app
  participant P as Payments API
  participant K as Key store
  participant N as Card network
  C->>P: POST /payments, Idempotency-Key k7
  P->>K: claim k7 as in progress
  P->>N: charge 40 EUR, key k7
  N-->>P: approved
  P->>K: save k7 as done with the 201
  P--xC: 201 Created, lost on the way
  C->>P: retry POST, same key k7
  P->>K: claim k7
  K-->>P: k7 is done, here is the 201
  P-->>C: 201 Created, the stored copy
The second POST never reaches the card network. The user is charged once and the client cannot tell the replay from the original.
When the key is…The server…Because
newclaims it, does the work, stores the responsethis is the first attempt
done, same fingerprintreplays the stored status and bodyit is a retry of a request that already finished (successfully or with a permanent error)
in progressanswers 409 Conflict, or waits briefly and checks againa concurrent duplicate, such as a double tap, must not run the work a second time
done, different fingerprintanswers 422: the key was reused for another requesta client bug; replaying would answer a different question
expiredtreats it as newkeys live for a bounded window, so retries must happen inside it
The five cases an idempotency store must handle. The in-progress row is the one most designs forget.

Two details decide whether the scheme really works:

  • Atomicity. If the work is a local database write, insert the key in the same transaction as the effect, so either both exist or neither does. If the work is an external call, a crash can land between the effect and the 'done' record: pass the same key to the external system so repeating the call is harmless, and give in progress entries a lease so a stuck one can be retried later.
  • Scope. Store keys per (account, key), so one customer can never receive another customer's stored response.

The same idea appears inside systems without a header: a message id checked by an idempotent consumer (Message queues & streaming (Kafka)), or a client-generated message id that de-duplicates chat sends (Case study: chat application). The HTTP view of safe and idempotent methods is in HTTP & REST APIs.

An idempotent payment endpoint

import hashlib
import json

CLAIM_SQL = (
    "INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (account_id, key, fingerprint, state, created_at) "
    "VALUES (%(account)s, %(key)s, %(fp)s, 'in_progress', now()) "
    "ON CONFLICT (account_id, key) DO NOTHING"
)


def create_payment(db, psp, account: str, key: str, body: dict) -> tuple[int, dict]:
    fp = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(body, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
    if db.execute(CLAIM_SQL, {"account": account, "key": key, "fp": fp}).rowcount == 1:
        result = psp.charge(body["amount"], body["card_token"], idempotency_key=key)   # pass the key downstream
        status = 201 if result["approved"] else 402
        db.execute("UPDATE idempotency_keys SET state = 'done', status = %s, response = %s "
                   "WHERE account_id = %s AND key = %s", (status, json.dumps(result), account, key))
        return status, result
    row = db.fetch_one("SELECT fingerprint, state, status, response FROM idempotency_keys "
                       "WHERE account_id = %s AND key = %s", (account, key))
    if row["fingerprint"] != fp:
        return 422, {"error": "idempotency key reused with a different request"}
    if row["state"] == "in_progress":
        return 409, {"error": "a request with this key is still being processed"}
    return row["status"], json.loads(row["response"])      # replay the stored answer
The conditional INSERT is the lock: exactly one request gets rowcount 1 and does the work. A real version also expires stale in-progress rows.

Circuit breakers

▶ Video lesson: Circuit breakers: fail fast, recover gently 4:13 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Retries help with blips. They hurt when a dependency is properly down: every call still waits the full timeout, and every retry adds load to a service that is trying to recover. A circuit breaker sits in front of a dependency and watches the results of recent calls. When too many fail it opens, and calls fail immediately with an error or a fallback, without touching the dependency. After a cool-down it lets a few trial calls through to find out whether the dependency has recovered.

stateDiagram-v2
  state "Half-open" as HalfOpen
  [*] --> Closed
  Closed --> Open: failure rate over threshold
  Open --> HalfOpen: cool-down elapsed
  HalfOpen --> Closed: trial calls succeed
  HalfOpen --> Open: a trial call fails
Three states. Only Closed sends real traffic; Half-open sends a trickle to test the water.

A breaker in front of a failing payments API

One request per second; the API times out on every call for eight seconds, then recovers. Compare the time callers spend waiting, and the calls the API receives while it is down.

Choices to state when you propose one:

  • One breaker per dependency (often per endpoint or per host), so one broken service does not block calls to healthy ones.
  • Trip on a failure rate over a window with a minimum volume, and count timeouts and slow calls, not only errors. Do not count 4xx responses: a user's typo is not an outage.
  • Decide the fallback in advance: a cached or default value, a degraded page, a queued request ('we will confirm by email'), or a clear error. A breaker without a fallback still helps because it fails fast, but users notice.
  • Combine it with retries correctly: each attempt is a call the breaker counts, and once it is open the retry loop stops.
  • Libraries such as Resilience4j (Java), Polly (.NET) and pybreaker (Python) implement it. Envoy offers related controls outside the code: connection and request limits, which it calls circuit breakers, and outlier detection, which ejects failing hosts.

A tiny circuit breaker

import time


class CircuitOpenError(Exception):
    pass


class CircuitBreaker:
    """Closed -> Open after `threshold` consecutive failures; Open -> Half-open after `cool_down` seconds."""

    def __init__(self, threshold: int = 5, cool_down: float = 30.0):
        self.threshold = threshold
        self.cool_down = cool_down
        self.failures = 0
        self.state = "closed"
        self.opened_at = 0.0

    def call(self, fn, *args, **kwargs):
        if self.state == "open":
            if time.monotonic() - self.opened_at < self.cool_down:
                raise CircuitOpenError("failing fast")      # the caller uses its fallback
            self.state = "half-open"                        # let this call through as a trial
        try:
            result = fn(*args, **kwargs)
        except Exception:
            self._on_failure()
            raise
        self._on_success()
        return result

    def _on_success(self) -> None:
        self.failures = 0
        self.state = "closed"

    def _on_failure(self) -> None:
        self.failures += 1
        if self.state == "half-open" or self.failures >= self.threshold:
            self.state = "open"
            self.opened_at = time.monotonic()


payments = CircuitBreaker(threshold=5, cool_down=30)


def charge(order):
    try:
        return payments.call(payments_api.charge, order, timeout=2.0)
    except CircuitOpenError:
        return queue_for_later(order)      # fallback: accept the order now, charge when the API is back
A single-threaded sketch. Production breakers add a lock, a failure-rate window instead of a consecutive count, a limit on concurrent half-open trials, and they ignore client errors.

Rate limiting

A rate limiter caps how many requests a client may make in a period and rejects the rest with 429 Too Many Requests and a Retry-After header. It protects a service from overload and abuse (one buggy script should not take an API down for everyone) and enforces fairness and paid quotas. Three decisions define one: the key (API key, user id, IP, or a combination), the limit (100 requests per minute per key, say, plus a burst allowance) and the algorithm.

Token bucket is the usual default for APIs. The sliding window counter is the usual fix when a fixed window's boundary burst matters.

Rate limiters in Python

No timer is needed: the bucket refills lazily, by the elapsed time, whenever a request arrives.

import time


class TokenBucket:
    def __init__(self, capacity: float, refill_per_sec: float):
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.rate = refill_per_sec
        self.tokens = capacity
        self.updated = time.monotonic()

    def allow(self, cost: float = 1.0) -> bool:
        now = time.monotonic()
        self.tokens = min(self.capacity, self.tokens + (now - self.updated) * self.rate)
        self.updated = now
        if self.tokens >= cost:
            self.tokens -= cost
            return True
        return False                      # the caller answers 429 with Retry-After


buckets: dict[str, TokenBucket] = {}


def allow_request(api_key: str) -> bool:
    bucket = buckets.setdefault(api_key, TokenBucket(capacity=20, refill_per_sec=5))
    return bucket.allow()

Exact, and the deque only ever holds the timestamps still inside the window.

import time
from collections import deque


class SlidingWindowLog:
    def __init__(self, limit: int, window_sec: float):
        self.limit = limit
        self.window = window_sec
        self.log: deque[float] = deque()     # timestamps of requests let through, oldest first

    def allow(self) -> bool:
        now = time.monotonic()
        while self.log and self.log[0] <= now - self.window:
            self.log.popleft()                # forget requests that slid out of the window
        if len(self.log) < self.limit:
            self.log.append(now)
            return True
        return False

Two integers per key. If 30 s into a one-minute window the previous minute had 80 requests and this one has 30, the estimate is 30 + 80 × 0.5 = 70.

import time


class SlidingWindowCounter:
    def __init__(self, limit: int, window_sec: float):
        self.limit = limit
        self.window = window_sec
        self.index = 0            # which fixed window `current` belongs to
        self.current = 0
        self.previous = 0

    def allow(self) -> bool:
        now = time.monotonic()
        index = int(now // self.window)
        if index != self.index:
            self.previous = self.current if index == self.index + 1 else 0   # older windows count for nothing
            self.current, self.index = 0, index
        elapsed = (now % self.window) / self.window       # how far into the current window, 0 to 1
        if self.current + self.previous * (1 - elapsed) < self.limit:
            self.current += 1
            return True
        return False

Every gateway instance shares one bucket per key. The Lua script runs atomically inside Redis, so two instances can never both spend the last token.

import time

import redis

r = redis.Redis()

TOKEN_BUCKET_LUA = '''
local capacity, rate, now = tonumber(ARGV[1]), tonumber(ARGV[2]), tonumber(ARGV[3])
local tokens = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 'tokens') or capacity)
local updated = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 'updated') or now)
tokens = math.min(capacity, tokens + (now - updated) * rate)
local allowed = 0
if tokens >= 1 then
  tokens = tokens - 1
  allowed = 1
end
redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', tokens, 'updated', now)
redis.call('EXPIRE', KEYS[1], math.ceil(capacity / rate) * 2)
return allowed
'''
bucket = r.register_script(TOKEN_BUCKET_LUA)


def allow_request(api_key: str, capacity: int = 20, rate: float = 5.0) -> bool:
    return bucket(keys=[f"rl:{api_key}"], args=[capacity, rate, time.time()]) == 1

Where the limiter runs

  • At the edge, in the API gateway or a sidecar, before any expensive work (Load balancers, reverse proxies & API gateways).
  • Counters in one place. With 20 gateway instances, purely local counters let each instance allow the full limit, 20 times too much, unless you divide the limit by the instance count. A shared Redis gives one counter per key for about a millisecond per check. The check-and-update must be atomic (INCR, or a Lua script), or two instances can both read 99 and both allow the hundredth request.
  • Decide what happens when Redis is down: fail open (allow, and rely on load shedding) for most APIs; fail closed for abuse-sensitive endpoints such as login or sending SMS.
  • Tell the client: 429 with Retry-After, plus headers with the limit and what remains, so well-behaved clients slow down instead of hammering.

Bulkheads, load shedding and graceful degradation

Bulkheads

A ship's hull is split into watertight compartments, so a breach floods one compartment instead of sinking the ship. A bulkhead in software gives each dependency (or each tenant, or each class of traffic) its own bounded pool of threads, connections or queue slots. When one dependency hangs, it can exhaust only its own pool.

flowchart LR
  REQ[Incoming requests] --> SVC[Checkout service]
  SVC --> P1[Payments pool<br/>20 threads]
  SVC --> P2[Recs pool<br/>5 threads]
  SVC --> P3[Inventory pool<br/>10 threads]
  P1 --> PAY[Payments API]
  P2 --> REC[Recommendations]
  P3 --> INV[(Inventory DB)]
Three small pools instead of one big one. A hung dependency fills its own pool and nothing else.

Load shedding

Every server has a throughput ceiling. Past it, queues grow, every request waits longer, and once the queueing time exceeds clients' timeouts the server spends its effort on answers nobody will read: useful throughput collapses while the CPU sits at 100%. Load shedding refuses the excess early and cheaply, so the requests it does accept finish in time.

  • Watch a signal that reflects saturation: requests in flight, queue length, or time spent waiting in the queue.
  • Above the limit, reject at the door with 503 (or 429 for a client over its quota) before doing any work. A rejection that costs 1 ms is far cheaper than a timeout that costs 2 s.
  • Shed by priority: keep checkout and login; drop prefetches, analytics beacons and batch jobs first.
  • Clients cooperate by backing off on 503, which is one more reason for jittered backoff.

Graceful degradation

Decide in advance which dependencies are critical (the request cannot succeed without them) and which are optional (the answer is worse but still useful). Give each optional one a time budget and a fallback:

DependencyWhen it is slow or downThe user sees
Recommendationsskip after 100 ms; breaker opensthe product page without the carousel
Reviewsserve the last cached summarya slightly stale star rating
Inventoryshow 'usually ships in 2 days'; confirm at checkouta softer promise
Paymentsaccept the order and charge later, or fail clearlya 'payment pending' email

Kill switches (feature flags that turn off an expensive feature in seconds) and a read-only mode belong in the same toolbox.

Waiting for everything vs budgeting each dependency

Wait for every dependency

The product page calls recommendations, reviews and inventory and waits for all three. When recommendations takes 8 s, the page takes 8 s, and the users who give up never buy. One slow team makes every page slow.

Budget each optional dependency

The page waits at most 100 ms for recommendations and 150 ms for reviews, then renders with what it has. Inventory is critical, so it gets a real timeout and a clear error. A slow optional service costs a feature, not the page.

Health checks

A load balancer or orchestrator needs to know which instances should receive traffic. Health checks answer that, and the main design decision is what each check is allowed to depend on.

  • Liveness: is this process stuck? A cheap endpoint that touches no dependencies. After a few consecutive failures the orchestrator restarts the instance. Never make liveness depend on a database, or a database blip restarts the whole fleet at once.
  • Readiness: can this instance serve requests right now? It fails during startup (caches warming, config loading), while the instance drains before a deploy, and when the instance itself is broken (disk full, worker pool exhausted). A failing readiness check takes the instance out of the load balancer without restarting it.
  • Dependency (deep) checks: are my database and downstream services reachable? Useful for dashboards and alerts, dangerous as readiness. When a shared dependency fails, every instance fails its check at the same moment, and the load balancer removes them all, turning a partial outage into a total one.

Active checks every 5 to 10 s with a threshold of 2 or 3 failures mean a dead instance can keep receiving traffic for 10 to 30 s; passive checks, which eject an instance after a few failed live requests, react faster. Load balancers, reverse proxies & API gateways covers the balancer's side: thresholds, ejection and connection draining.

  LB[Load balancer] -- readiness --> APP
  APP --> DB[(Database)]
  APP --> PAY[Payments API]
  MON[Monitoring] -- deep check --> APP
Three probes with three different consequences: restart, remove from rotation, or alert a human.

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

Reliability needs a number, or every discussion becomes 'it felt slow'. Three terms:

  • An SLI (service level indicator) measures what users experience, usually as a ratio of good events to valid events: the share of checkout requests that return a non-5xx response within 800 ms, measured at the load balancer.
  • An SLO (service level objective) is the target for an SLI over a window: 99.9% of checkout requests are good, over a rolling 30 days.
  • An SLA (service level agreement) is a contract with consequences, such as service credits. It is set looser than the internal SLO, so you hear about problems before you owe anyone money.

The error budget is what the SLO allows to go wrong: 1 − SLO. At 99.9% over 30 days that is 0.1% of requests, or about 43 minutes of total outage. The budget turns an argument into a rule: while budget remains, ship and take risks; when it is spent, freeze risky changes and work on reliability. 100% is the wrong target: users cannot tell 99.999% from 100% through their own flaky networks, and each extra nine costs far more than the last.

From an SLO to a budget, downtime and a burn rate

The burn rate is the current error rate divided by the budget. At 1, the budget runs out exactly at the end of the window; at 14.4, an hour uses 2% of a 30-day budget, which is a common paging threshold.

Alerting on the budget, not on every error

Page when the budget is burning too fast, not when a single error appears. A common policy from Google's SRE workbook pages when the burn rate over the last hour exceeds 14.4 and is still above it over the last 5 minutes (so the page stops once the problem does), pages at a burn rate of 6 over 6 hours, and opens a ticket for slower burns over days.

Availability also multiplies. A request that needs three dependencies, each available 99.9% of the time, succeeds about 0.999³ ≈ 99.7% of the time if their failures are independent. A service cannot promise more than its critical dependencies offer without redundancy, caching or fallbacks in between.

Chaos testing

A fallback works only if someone has seen it work. Chaos engineering injects failures on purpose, under control: kill an instance, add 500 ms of latency to one dependency, black-hole a zone, fill a disk. Each experiment states a hypothesis in SLI terms ('checkout success stays above 99.9% while recommendations is down'), starts small (staging, then a sliver of production traffic) and has an abort switch tied to the error budget. Netflix's Chaos Monkey, which terminates production instances at random, made the idea famous; scheduled game days rehearse the same failures with the people who would be paged.

Pick SLIs a user would recognise: 'checkout succeeded within a second', not 'CPU below 70%'. CPU, memory and queue depth are causes to investigate, and they belong on dashboards, not in the SLO.

Putting it together in the interview

Resilience checklist for a design discussion

What interviewers ask next

Why not retry until it works?

Retries multiply load exactly when the system is weakest: 3 attempts at each of 3 layers is 27 calls at the bottom. Unbounded retries also outlive the user's patience. Cap attempts, retry at one layer, keep a retry budget of about 10%, and make sure the operation is idempotent first.

How do you choose a timeout?

From the dependency's latency distribution: a little above its p99.9, and never more than the caller's remaining deadline. For tail latency on reads, a hedged request (send a second copy to another replica if the first has not answered by about the p95, use whichever answers first) cuts the tail for a few percent of extra load.

Circuit breaker or retries?

Both, for different failures. Retries absorb blips that last milliseconds; the breaker handles failures that last seconds or minutes, and stops the retries from hammering a dead dependency. Retries go inside the breaker, so each attempt counts and an open breaker ends the loop.

Bulkhead or circuit breaker: what is the difference?

A bulkhead limits how much of you a dependency can occupy (threads, connections), whether it fails or not. A breaker decides whether to call it at all, based on recent failures. A slow-but-working dependency trips the bulkhead first; a failing one trips the breaker.

Where does the rate limiter live, and what if Redis is down?

At the gateway, keyed by API key or IP, with counters in a shared Redis updated atomically. If Redis is down, fail open for ordinary APIs and protect the service with load shedding; fail closed for abuse-sensitive endpoints such as login. A small local limiter per instance is a good backstop.

How do you rate-limit across several regions?

Exact global counters need a cross-region round trip per request, which nobody wants. Give each region a share of the limit (in proportion to its traffic) and reconcile the counts asynchronously, accepting a small overshoot.

How do you make a payment happen exactly once?

You cannot get exactly-once delivery; you get at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing. The client sends an idempotency key, the service stores it atomically with the outcome, passes it to the payment provider, and a reconciliation job compares your records with the provider's for anything left in progress.

What SLO would you set?

Start from what users need and what dependencies can deliver: 99.9% of checkouts succeeding within a second is a common starting point, measured at the load balancer over 30 days. Then say what happens when the budget runs out: a release freeze and reliability work.

A model answer

'Every call from checkout has a timeout taken from that dependency's p99.9, and the request carries a one-second deadline that each hop passes on. Clients retry only transient errors on idempotent calls, at most twice, with exponential backoff, full jitter and a retry budget, and payment requests carry an idempotency key that the payments service stores with its response, so a retry can never charge twice. Each dependency sits behind a circuit breaker and its own small connection pool, so a slow recommendations service costs us the carousel and nothing else. The gateway rate-limits each API key with a token bucket in Redis and sheds low-priority traffic when we are saturated. Readiness checks look only at the instance itself, and we page on the burn rate of a 99.9% checkout SLO.'

Case study: video streaming

Design a YouTube-style platform where creators upload videos and viewers stream them on any network. You will size storage and egress from uploads per day and the bitrate ladder, build a resumable upload path and a queue-driven transcoding pipeline, explain adaptive bitrate streaming with segments and manifests, deliver through a CDN with an origin shield, count views without a hot row, and finish with a five-sentence answer.

After this chapter you can
  • Run the interview script on a video platform: requirements, estimates, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, trade-offs
  • Estimate yearly storage and viewing egress from uploads per day, video length and the bitrate ladder, and say why egress forces a CDN
  • Design the upload and transcoding path: presigned resumable uploads, a job queue, leased idempotent workers, chunk-parallel encoding
  • Explain adaptive bitrate streaming (HLS and DASH segments and manifests) and how a player picks a rendition from throughput and buffer
  • Serve segments through a CDN with an origin shield, keep metadata apart from blobs, and count views with batched approximate counters
  • Name the cost levers (CDN hit ratio, per-title ladders, codecs, storage tiers, lazy encoding) and what each one trades

Requirements and scope

A video platform has two halves that share almost nothing except storage. Creators upload a few hundred thousand files a day, each hundreds of megabytes to several gigabytes, often over a phone connection that drops. Viewers watch hundreds of millions of times a day, on networks whose speed changes from one second to the next. The design is mostly about bytes: moving large files in, turning each into many smaller files, and pushing those out through a CDN at terabits per second. Rows in a database are the easy part.

Fix the scope in the first five minutes. Say the checklist out loud and write the numbers you assume next to it.

Requirements to confirm with the interviewer

The interviewer wants to see you split the problem into an upload path and a watch path, because they differ in scale (about a thousand views per upload), in latency budget (minutes versus milliseconds) and in what an outage costs. Five questions settle most of the scope: on-demand or live, maximum file size, which devices, how fresh view counts must be, and how many uploads and views a day. State your assumptions and move on.

Estimates that shape the design

Two assumptions drive almost every number. The bitrate ladder (how many renditions you keep, at what bitrates) decides storage, and watch time × streamed bitrate decides egress. The defaults below are illustrative, not any company's figures. A bitrate in Mbit/s divided by 8 is megabytes per second, so one minute at 8 Mbit/s is 60 MB.

Uploads, storage, transcoding and egress

Storage is in decimal units (1 TB = 10^12 bytes). The ladder is described by its number of renditions and their average bitrate: five rungs averaging 2 Mbit/s is the 10 Mbit/s ladder in the table further down.

Read the outputs as decisions:

  • About 14 Gbit/s of uploads arrive on average. Proxying that through application servers buys nothing, so clients upload straight to object storage with presigned URLs and the API only signs requests.
  • About 340 TB a day, roughly 120 PB a year, and it only grows. The ladder is more than half of it (10 of every 18 Mbit/s here), so the ladder itself is a cost lever, not a fixed fact.
  • About 8,700 cores encode around the clock, more after a burst. Work like that sits behind a queue with an autoscaled fleet of workers. Slower presets and newer codecs multiply this figure.
  • About 3.5 Tbit/s on average and 10 Tbit/s at peak goes out to viewers, 250 times the upload rate. Only a CDN can serve that. Even with 95% of bytes served at the edge, the origin would still see about 500 Gbit/s at peak, which is why a shield layer sits in front of it.
  • Metadata is small: 500,000 new video rows a day fit a conventional database. Blobs and bandwidth are the hard part.

API and data model

The API never carries video bytes. It creates records, signs URLs and returns metadata. On the way in, bytes go from the creator's app straight to object storage; on the way out, from the CDN straight to the player.

Details worth saying out loud. The create call takes an Idempotency-Key, so a client that retries after a timeout does not create two videos. The upload is multipart: each 16 MiB part is its own request with its own retry, which is what makes a 2 GB upload over a train's Wi-Fi possible (the mechanics are in Object storage (S3) & CDNs). complete answers 202 Accepted because nothing is watchable until transcoding finishes. The manifest URL is signed with an expiry, so it cannot be embedded on another site forever. View events have their own endpoint and are counted asynchronously.

The data splits by size and access pattern. Metadata (titles, owners, status, rendition lists, counters) is small, structured and queried, so it goes in a database sharded by video_id. Blobs (originals, segments, manifests, thumbnails) are large, immutable and read front to back, so they go in object storage under deterministic keys such as renditions/v_8Kq2Zx/720p/seg_00042.m4s. A row points at its blobs by prefix; no one ever queries a blob.

The two paths are separate deployments on purpose. Turning one original into a ladder

Transcoding decodes the original and re-encodes it at several resolutions and bitrates: the bitrate ladder. Each rendition is then packaged, meaning cut into short segments plus a manifest that lists them. Every segment starts with a keyframe (a frame that decodes without reference to earlier frames), and segment boundaries fall at the same timestamps in every rendition. That alignment is what lets a player switch rendition at any segment boundary.

An illustrative ladder: about 10 Mbit/s across five rungs, the calculator's default. Real services tune the ladder per title.

The work is parallel in two directions. Renditions are independent of each other, and a long video can be split into chunks at keyframes, each chunk encoded on a different machine and the results concatenated. A two-hour film that would take hours on one machine is ready in minutes when hundreds of chunk jobs run at once.

A leased, idempotent transcode worker

import time

LEASE_S = 120
MAX_ATTEMPTS = 3


def run_worker(queue, store, meta, encoder) -> None:
    while True:
        msg = queue.receive(visibility_timeout=LEASE_S)       # hidden from other workers while leased
        if msg is None:
            time.sleep(1)
            continue
        job = msg.body                                        # {"video_id": "v_8Kq2Zx", "rendition": "720p", "chunk": 7}
        key = f"renditions/{job['video_id']}/{job['rendition']}/chunk_{job['chunk']:05d}.mp4"
        if meta.chunk_done(job):                              # an earlier delivery already finished it
            queue.delete(msg)
            continue
        if msg.receive_count > MAX_ATTEMPTS:                  # poison file: stop retrying, alert a human
            queue.send_to_dead_letter(msg)
            queue.delete(msg)
            continue
        source = store.signed_get_url(f"originals/{job['video_id']}")
        for _progress in encoder.encode(source, job):         # yields every few seconds
            queue.extend_lease(msg, LEASE_S)                  # heartbeat: keep the job while working
        store.put_file(key, encoder.output_path(job))         # same key on every attempt: a retry overwrites
        meta.mark_chunk_done(job)                             # conditional write: done stays done
        queue.delete(msg)                                     # ack only after the output is durable
At-least-once delivery plus idempotent work. A duplicate delivery either finds the chunk done or rewrites the same key with the same bytes.

Deep dive 2: adaptive bitrate streaming

▶ Video lesson: Adaptive bitrate: the player picks a rung per segment 4:16 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A single 1080p file needs 4.5 Mbit/s sustained for the whole film. A phone on a train gets 20 Mbit/s one minute and 1 Mbit/s in the next tunnel, so a single file either stalls or looks worse than it needs to everywhere. Adaptive bitrate streaming (ABR) solves this on the client. Every rendition is cut into aligned segments of 2 to 6 seconds, a manifest lists the renditions and their segments, and the player picks a rendition for each segment from what the network has been delivering. The servers only serve files.

Two formats dominate. HLS (from Apple, with .m3u8 playlists) and MPEG-DASH (an ISO standard, with an XML .mpd manifest). Both can point at the same fragmented-MP4 segments (the CMAF format), so a single set of segments serves both and storage is not doubled. In HLS the top-level file is the master playlist (the current spec calls it the multivariant playlist); each rendition has its own media playlist.

The simulation runs a simplified player. Its rule: estimate throughput as the lower of the last measurement and the average of the last three, spend at most 80% of it, never climb with less than 4 s buffered, and abandon a download that would finish after the buffer runs dry. Real players (hls.js, dash.js, ExoPlayer, AVPlayer) use richer versions of the same signals: throughput history and buffer level.

The player's decision in Python

LADDER = [("240p", 0.4), ("360p", 0.8), ("480p", 1.5), ("720p", 2.8), ("1080p", 4.5)]   # Mbit/s
SAFETY = 0.8              # leave headroom: throughput estimates are noisy
UP_MIN_BUFFER_S = 4.0     # do not climb on a thin buffer


def estimate(throughputs: list[float]) -> float:
    """Pessimistic: react to a drop at once, to a recovery gradually."""
    last3 = throughputs[-3:]
    return min(throughputs[-1], sum(last3) / len(last3))


def choose_rung(throughputs: list[float], buffer_s: float, current: int) -> int:
    if not throughputs:
        return 2                                      # no data yet: start in the middle
    budget = SAFETY * estimate(throughputs)
    best = max((i for i, (_, mbps) in enumerate(LADDER) if mbps <= budget), default=0)
    if best > current and buffer_s < UP_MIN_BUFFER_S:
        return current                                # hold until the buffer can absorb a mistake
    return best


def should_abandon(elapsed_s: float, got_mbit: float, size_mbit: float, buffer_s: float) -> bool:
    """Give up on a segment that would arrive after the buffer runs dry."""
    if elapsed_s < 1.0 or got_mbit <= 0:
        return False
    rate = got_mbit / elapsed_s
    return (size_mbit - got_mbit) / rate > buffer_s
Drop fast, climb slowly, and never let a single slow download freeze the picture.

If asked 'what happens when the bandwidth drops?', walk the loop: the next download is slow, the estimate falls, and the player fetches the following segments from a lower rung while it lives off its buffer. If one download is so slow that it would outlast the buffer, the player abandons it and re-requests that segment lower. Climbing back is deliberately slower than dropping, so the picture does not flap. None of this involves the server, which is why it scales.

Deep dive 3: delivery through the CDN

Segments are the easiest objects a CDN ever sees: immutable, identical for every viewer, a few hundred kilobytes to a few megabytes each, and requested in a predictable order. Popularity is heavily skewed: a small share of videos gets most of the views, so the head of the catalogue lives in every edge while the long tail misses. The edge, shield and invalidation mechanics are in Object storage (S3) & CDNs; video adds three twists.

  • Origin shield. Hundreds of edges missing on the same new video would each fetch it from the bucket. A shield, one regional cache that every edge asks first, turns those misses into one origin read. At 10 Tbit/s even a 5% miss rate is hundreds of Gbit/s, so the shield is not optional.
  • Request collapsing. When a premiere starts, thousands of viewers ask for segment 0 within the same second. The edge sends one request upstream and holds the rest until it returns; without that, a cold segment causes a stampede (the same failure as in Caching).
  • Immutable names. A segment's URL never changes its content, so it can be served with Cache-Control: max-age=31536000, immutable. A re-encode writes new names. Nothing ever needs invalidating.

Signed URLs can quietly destroy your hit ratio. If every viewer's segment URL carries their own ?exp=...&sig=... and the CDN includes the query string in its cache key, every request names a different object and misses. Validate the signature at the edge and leave it out of the cache key, or use a signed cookie so the URLs are identical for everyone.

At the very top end, platforms also put cache servers inside internet providers' networks (Netflix Open Connect, Google Global Cache), so the most popular titles never cross the backbone at all. Mention it as the next step after a commercial CDN, not as the first design.

Deep dive 4: metadata, view counts and recommendations

Metadata and blobs live apart

The watch page needs title, channel, status, duration, rendition list, view count and like count: a few kilobytes, read on every page view, edited rarely. That is a cache-aside read in front of a database sharded by video_id (Caching). The bytes are the opposite: huge, immutable, read sequentially, never queried. Mixing them fails both ways. Blobs in a database bloat its backups and replicas; metadata kept only in object storage cannot be queried or updated transactionally.

Counting views without a hot row

UPDATE videos SET views = views + 1 on every view works until a video goes viral. 500 million views a day is about 6,000 a second on average, and one popular video can take thousands a second on its own, all on one row. Treat counting as a stream instead (Message queues & streaming (Kafka)):

flowchart LR
  PL[Players] --> IN[View ingest API]
  IN --> LOG{{Event log<br/>by video_id}}
  LOG --> AGG[Aggregators]
  AGG --> CNT[(Counter store)]
  CNT --> CC[(Cache)]
  CC --> WP[Watch page]
  LOG --> DL[(Data lake)]
Millions of events become a few hundred batched writes. The displayed count lags by a minute; exact counts come from the lake.

A deduplicating, batching view aggregator

import random
import time
from collections import Counter


class ViewAggregator:
    """Count each (session, video) once, accumulate per video, flush batched increments."""

    def __init__(self, counter_store, flush_every_s: float = 5.0, shards: int = 16):
        self.store = counter_store
        self.flush_every_s = flush_every_s
        self.shards = shards
        self.pending: Counter = Counter()
        self.seen: set[tuple[str, str]] = set()           # in production: a TTL cache or Bloom filter per day
        self.last_flush = time.monotonic()

    def on_event(self, event: dict) -> None:
        key = (event["session_id"], event["video_id"])
        if key in self.seen or event.get("is_bot"):
            return                                         # replay, refresh or bot: not a new view
        self.seen.add(key)
        self.pending[event["video_id"]] += 1
        if time.monotonic() - self.last_flush >= self.flush_every_s:
            self.flush()

    def flush(self) -> None:
        for video_id, n in self.pending.items():
            shard = random.randrange(self.shards)          # spread a hot video's writes over rows
            self.store.increment(video_id, shard, n)       # UPDATE ... SET views = views + :n
        self.pending.clear()
        self.last_flush = time.monotonic()
The log is partitioned by video_id, so one aggregator sees every event for a video and in-memory dedupe is correct. Commit the log offset after the flush.

Delivery is at-least-once, so a crash between a flush and the offset commit counts one batch twice. That is fine for a number on a watch page and wrong for payouts, which is why payouts come from the lake. Unique viewers use a HyperLogLog sketch: about 12 KB per video estimates millions of distinct ids to within about 1%.

Recommendations, at whiteboard depth

Keep this to two stages unless the interviewer asks for more. Candidate generation narrows millions of videos to a few hundred per viewer from cheap signals: subscriptions, videos often watched after the viewer's recent ones, and nearest neighbours in an embedding space. Ranking scores those few hundred with a model trained to predict watch time or satisfaction, then a final pass removes duplicates, enforces variety and drops what the viewer has seen. Home pages are mostly precomputed per viewer in batch and adjusted from the last few watch events.

Cost levers, trade-offs and follow-ups

At this scale the bill is mostly egress and storage, then compute. Each lever trades something:

  • CDN hit ratio and egress price. Every point of hit ratio is tens of Gbit/s the origin does not serve. Long TTLs on immutable names, a shield, and negotiated or multi-CDN pricing matter more than anything inside your own servers.
  • Fewer bits for the same picture. Per-title encoding picks the ladder from each video's complexity: a cartoon looks fine at a fraction of the bitrate an action film needs. Newer codecs (HEVC, VP9, AV1) save very roughly 30 to 50% of bits over H.264 at similar quality, but encode several times slower and not every device decodes them, so spend that CPU on the videos that get watched.
  • Encode lazily. Most uploads get few views. Encode the low rungs at upload and the expensive ones (1080p and up, new codecs) when a video crosses a view threshold.
  • Storage tiers. Lifecycle rules move originals and rarely watched renditions to infrequent-access or archive tiers. Keep the original: it lets you re-encode with a better codec later.
  • Cheaper compute. Transcode jobs are idempotent and retryable, so they can run on spot or preemptible capacity at a large discount.

What interviewers ask next

How does an interrupted upload resume?

The client kept the upload_id. It calls ListParts to see which parts object storage already holds, uploads only the missing ones, then completes. A lifecycle rule aborts multipart uploads left incomplete for a week, because stored parts are billed even though no object exists.

How quickly can a new video be watched?

Split the original into chunks so encoding runs in parallel, put the cheap rungs ahead of the expensive ones (a priority queue, as in the simulation), and publish as soon as 360p and the moderation verdict are ready. For a five-minute video that is a minute or two; the higher rungs follow.

A worker dies halfway through a job. What happens?

It stops renewing its lease, so the message becomes visible again and another worker takes it: at-least-once delivery. Outputs go to deterministic keys and status writes are conditional, so the redo overwrites rather than duplicates. After three failures the job goes to a dead-letter queue for a human.

A famous creator premieres a video to millions at once. What breaks?

The first segments are cold at every edge at the same moment. Request collapsing at the edge and the shield turn millions of requests into a few origin reads; pre-warming the first segments to the edges before the premiere removes even those. The metadata row is served from cache, and the view counter is sharded.

How is live streaming different?

Ingest arrives as a continuous stream (RTMP, SRT or WebRTC), so transcoding is real time and cannot be retried later. Segments are short (2 s, or partial segments in low-latency HLS), the media playlist keeps growing and is refetched every few seconds with a short TTL, and the player keeps a small buffer, which makes stalls more likely.

How do you stop people downloading or hot-linking videos?

Signed URLs or cookies with short expiries stop casual hot-linking. Real protection for paid content is DRM (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady): segments are encrypted and the player fetches a decryption key from a license server that checks the viewer's entitlement. Geo-restriction is enforced at the edge.

How does seeking and scrubbing stay fast?

Segments start on keyframes, so a seek fetches one segment from the new position. Thumbnails while scrubbing come from an I-frame playlist or from sprite sheets of small images generated during processing.

What do you monitor?

Startup time, rebuffering ratio and average bitrate as players report them; CDN hit ratio and origin egress; transcode queue depth and the age of its oldest message; job failure and dead-letter rates; upload success rate by network type.

A five-sentence model answer

'I would split the system into an upload path and a watch path. Creators upload resumable multipart files straight to object storage with presigned URLs; the upload event becomes jobs on a queue, consumed by an autoscaled fleet of leased, idempotent workers that encode a five-rung ladder in parallel chunks and package it as CMAF segments with HLS and DASH manifests. At 500,000 uploads a day that is about 340 TB of new storage a day, and 500 million views a day is about 3.5 Tbit/s of egress, 250 times the upload rate, so segments are served by a CDN with an origin shield and year-long immutable TTLs, while the player picks a rendition for every segment from measured throughput and its buffer. Metadata lives in a sharded database behind a cache, separate from the blobs, and views flow through an event log into deduplicated, batched counters that lag by a minute but never create a hot row. Egress and storage drive the cost, so I would raise the CDN hit ratio, encode per title, add AV1 only for popular videos and tier old renditions, and I would watch startup time, rebuffering ratio, CDN hit ratio and the age of the oldest transcode job.'

Consistency models & the CAP theorem

Copies of data disagree for a while, and a consistency model is the contract that says what a read may return meanwhile. This chapter runs linearizable, sequential, causal and eventual consistency on one timeline, states the CAP theorem precisely and shows what CP and AP stores do during a partition, adds PACELC's everyday latency cost, makes quorums concrete with N, R and W, covers how replicas repair and how concurrent writes are resolved, and places real systems on the map, so that 'is your system CP or AP?' gets a precise, per-operation answer.

After this chapter you can
  • Define linearizable, sequential, causal and eventual consistency, and name the anomalies each allows
  • State the CAP theorem precisely and describe what CP and AP systems do during a partition
  • Use PACELC to explain the latency cost of consistency when there is no partition
  • Choose N, R and W for a leaderless store and explain what R + W > N does and does not guarantee
  • Explain read repair, hinted handoff, Merkle-tree anti-entropy, last write wins and version vectors
  • Answer 'is your system CP or AP?' per operation, with real systems as examples

What 'consistent' means once there are copies

▶ Video lesson: Consistency models: what a read may return 4:26 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Once data has copies, "what does a read return?" no longer has an obvious answer: during replication, the copies disagree. A consistency model is the contract between a data store and its clients that says which values a read may return, given the writes before and during it. Stronger models allow fewer surprises and need more coordination. Four cover what interviews ask:

  • Linearizable (strong consistency): the system behaves as if there were one copy, and every operation takes effect at a single instant between its start and its end. Once any read returns a new value, every read that starts later returns it too, or something newer. This is the C in CAP.
  • Sequential: all clients see all operations in one order, and each client's own operations keep the order it issued them, but that order may lag real time. A read may return an old value after someone else saw the new one; nobody sees updates in a different order, and nobody goes backwards.
  • Causal: operations that are causally related are seen in that order by everyone. If Bob replied after reading Alice's message, nobody sees the reply without the message. Concurrent, unrelated writes may be seen in different orders by different clients.
  • Eventual: if writes stop, all replicas eventually hold the same value. Until then, a read may return any recent value: stale, new, then stale again.

Between causal and eventual sit the session guarantees from Scaling databases: replication, sharding, partitioning (read-your-writes, monotonic reads): cheap promises about one client's own view that remove most of the anomalies users notice. Do not confuse any of these with the C in ACID, which means "transactions keep the declared constraints".

flowchart TD
  LIN[Linearizable<br/>one copy, real time] --> SEQ[Sequential<br/>one order, may lag]
  SEQ --> CAU[Causal<br/>causes before effects]
  CAU --> SES[Session guarantees<br/>read your own writes]
  SES --> EV[Eventual<br/>converges once writes stop]
Each arrow points to a weaker model: it allows every history the one above allows, and more. Down the chain, reads get cheaper and faster, and users see more surprises.

The CAP theorem, stated precisely

▶ Video lesson: CAP, stated precisely 4:24 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

CAP is quoted more often than it is read. Eric Brewer stated it as a conjecture in 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002 with precise definitions:

  • Consistency means linearizability: every read sees the latest completed write, as if there were one copy.
  • Availability means every request received by a non-failing node gets a response that is not an error. No "try again later", no waiting forever.
  • Partition tolerance means the system keeps its promises even when the network loses or delays messages between nodes for arbitrarily long.

The theorem: while the network is partitioned, a system cannot be both consistent and available. The proof fits in a sentence: cut the link between two replicas and write to one side; a read on the other side either answers with the old value (available, not consistent) or waits for a message that cannot arrive (consistent, not available).

Two consequences interviewers probe:

  1. You do not get to drop P. Networks partition: a switch fails, a fibre is cut, a firewall rule is wrong, a long garbage-collection pause makes a node unreachable. A distributed system must decide what to do when that happens. "CA" describes only a system that is not distributed at all.
  2. The choice is made during a partition, and per operation. With no partition a system can be consistent and available at once. And one system can make different choices for different data: refuse bookings of the last seat on the minority side, but keep accepting likes.
flowchart TD
  REQ[Request arrives<br/>at a replica] --> P{Can it reach the<br/>nodes it needs?}
  P -->|yes| BOTH[Serve normally<br/>consistent and available]
  P -->|no, partition| CH{Keep which?}
  CH -->|consistency| CP[CP<br/>refuse or time out]
  CH -->|availability| AP[AP<br/>answer from local copy]
  CP --> CPC[Errors on the<br/>minority side]
  AP --> APC[Stale reads, conflicts<br/>reconciled after healing]
CAP only bites in the lower half. The upper path, no partition, is where PACELC's latency trade-off lives.

What CAP does not say

Three CAP myths. 'Pick two of three': partitions are not optional, so the real choice is C or A while one is happening. 'CP means unavailable': only requests that need the unreachable side fail; a 5-node consensus group keeps serving with 2 nodes gone. 'AP means inconsistent': only during the partition and the repair after it; with a healthy network an AP store usually converges within milliseconds.

PACELC: the trade-off you pay every day

CAP describes the rare moment of a partition. Daniel Abadi pointed out (first in a 2010 blog post, then in a 2012 paper) that the trade-off a replicated system pays all the time is different: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. That is PACELC.

The "else" half is about replication when the network is healthy. A linearizable write must reach a majority of replicas before it is acknowledged, and a linearizable read must confirm with the leader or a quorum: at least one round trip per operation. Inside a data centre that is well under a millisecond; across regions it is tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Answering from the local replica and replicating later is fast, and may be stale.

flowchart LR
  Q{Network<br/>partition?} -->|yes| PA[PA<br/>available, may be stale]
  Q -->|yes| PC[PC<br/>minority side refuses]
  Q -->|no, else| EL[EL<br/>answer locally, fast]
  Q -->|no, else| EC[EC<br/>wait for replicas]
Every replicated store makes two choices: one for the rare partition, one for every ordinary request.

What the 'else' costs: a consistent write across three regions

With replicas in three regions and the leader in one of them, a linearizable write waits for a majority: itself plus the nearer of the other two regions. Round trips between regions are typically tens to a couple of hundred milliseconds (order of magnitude).

Quorums: tuning N, R and W

▶ Video lesson: Quorums: tuning N, R and W 4:21 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Leaderless stores (Dynamo-style: Cassandra, ScyllaDB, Riak) turn the consistency trade-off into three numbers:

  • N: how many replicas hold each key (the replication factor), typically 3.
  • W: how many replicas must acknowledge a write before it counts as successful.
  • R: how many replicas must answer a read; the reader keeps the value with the newest version.

If R + W > N, every read set shares at least one replica with every successful write set, so a read sees the latest successful write. N = 3, W = 2, R = 2 is the usual "quorum" setting (Cassandra's QUORUM is a majority, ⌊N/2⌋ + 1). A write needs W replicas reachable, so it survives N − W failures; a read survives N − R. The extremes:

  • W = 1, R = 1: fastest and most available, any single replica will do, and reads can be stale.
  • W = N, R = 1: fast, fresh reads, but a single dead replica blocks every write.
  • W = 1, R = N: fast writes; reads wait for the slowest replica and fail if any is down.

R + W > N is not linearizability

Quorum overlap guarantees that a read includes the latest successful write, and still leaves gaps. A sloppy quorum (writes accepted by stand-in nodes outside the key's usual replicas during a failure, delivered later by hinted handoff) breaks the overlap. Concurrent writes resolved by last-write-wins can lose one. A write that failed overall but landed on some replicas can show up in later reads. And a read racing a write can see the new value and then the old one, unless read repair finishes before the read returns. For operations that need true linearizability, use a consensus-based store, or Cassandra's lightweight transactions (Paxos-based compare-and-set) for the few writes that need it.

Repairing replicas: read repair, hinted handoff, anti-entropy

Replicas in a leaderless store drift apart: a node was down for an hour, a write reached only W of N, a message was dropped. Three mechanisms pull them back together, each catching what the one before misses:

  • Read repair: when a read sees replicas disagree, the coordinator writes the newest version back to the stale ones. Free for data that is read often; no help for data nobody reads.
  • Hinted handoff: if a replica is down during a write, another node stores the write with a hint naming the intended owner, and delivers it when the owner comes back. Cassandra keeps hints for a limited window, 3 hours by default; a node that was down longer needs a full repair.
  • Anti-entropy: a background process compares whole replicas and copies whatever differs. Comparing every row would be far too slow, so replicas build Merkle trees: each leaf is a hash of one range of keys, and each parent is a hash of its children. Matching hashes prove a whole range identical; only differing branches are explored. Cassandra's nodetool repair and Dynamo work this way.

Concurrent writes: last write wins, version vectors, CRDTs

When two clients write the same key through different replicas at about the same time, neither write happened before the other. They are concurrent, and something must decide the outcome.

  • Last write wins (LWW): each write carries a timestamp; the highest wins and the others are discarded. Cassandra resolves every column this way. It is simple and always converges, but it silently drops one of two concurrent writes, and with skewed clocks a write that really came later can lose to one stamped by a fast clock. Use it where losing a concurrent write is harmless (a profile photo URL, a last-seen time) or where keys are written once.
  • Version vectors (often called vector clocks): each value carries one counter per replica that coordinated a write. Comparing two vectors tells you whether one write saw the other (its vector is at least as large in every entry) or neither did (concurrent). Concurrent values are kept as siblings and handed to the application to merge. Dynamo's shopping cart did this, merging carts by taking the union of their items; Riak exposes siblings to clients.
  • CRDTs (conflict-free replicated data types): data types with a merge built in that gives the same result in any order, such as counters, sets that track adds and removes, maps and collaborative text. Riak's data types and Redis Enterprise's active-active databases use them, as do collaborative editors.

Version vectors detect that two writes were concurrent instead of guessing a winner; the application merges.

Resolving concurrent writes in Python

Detect concurrency, keep siblings, let the application merge.

Clock = dict[str, int]          # replica id -> how many writes that replica has coordinated


def compare(a: Clock, b: Clock) -> str:
    keys = a.keys() | b.keys()
    a_le_b = all(a.get(k, 0) <= b.get(k, 0) for k in keys)
    b_le_a = all(b.get(k, 0) <= a.get(k, 0) for k in keys)
    if a_le_b and b_le_a:
        return "equal"
    if a_le_b:
        return "a before b"       # b has seen everything a has, so b replaces a
    if b_le_a:
        return "b before a"
    return "concurrent"           # neither saw the other: keep both as siblings


def merge(a: Clock, b: Clock) -> Clock:
    return {k: max(a.get(k, 0), b.get(k, 0)) for k in a.keys() | b.keys()}


def bump(clock: Clock, replica: str) -> Clock:
    return {**clock, replica: clock.get(replica, 0) + 1}


ann = bump({}, "X")                         # milk, written through replica X
ben = bump({}, "Y")                         # eggs, written through replica Y
print(compare(ann, ben))                    # concurrent: the store keeps both carts
merged = bump(merge(ann, ben), "X")         # the app merges the carts and writes through X
print(compare(ann, merged), "|", compare(ben, merged))   # a before b | a before b

Always converges, by throwing a concurrent write away.

import time


def lww_put(replica: dict, key: str, value, node: str) -> None:
    stamp = (time.time(), node)               # the node id breaks timestamp ties the same way everywhere
    current = replica.get(key)
    if current is None or stamp > current[0]:
        replica[key] = (stamp, value)          # the older write is dropped, and nobody is told


def lww_merge(a: dict, b: dict) -> dict:
    # anti-entropy between two replicas: for each key, the higher stamp wins
    return {k: max(x for x in (a.get(k), b.get(k)) if x is not None) for k in a.keys() | b.keys()}


east, west = {}, {}
lww_put(east, "cart:7", ["milk"], "east")
lww_put(west, "cart:7", ["eggs"], "west")      # concurrent with the write above
print(lww_merge(east, west)["cart:7"][1])      # ['eggs']: the milk is gone

Choose consistency per piece of data, not per system. Most data in most systems ends at the bottom.

How to answer 'is your system CP or AP?'

A strong answer: 'It depends on the data, so I'd answer per operation. Bookings and payments are CP: they go to a consensus-backed store, and during a partition the minority side returns errors rather than risk selling a seat twice. The feed, likes and view counts are AP: any replica answers, writes merge later, and a few seconds of staleness is invisible to users. With no partition the trade-off is latency, so I keep cross-region synchronous writes for the CP data only, and give user profiles read-your-writes on top of eventual consistency.'

Follow-ups and how to handle them

'Why not choose CA?'

Because partitions are not optional in a distributed system. 'CA' describes a single node, or a system that silently breaks both guarantees when the network fails. Say what each side does during a partition instead.

'Your CP system lost a node. Is it down?'

No. A 5-node consensus group tolerates 2 failures; only requests that need a majority they cannot reach fail. CP costs availability only on the minority side of a partition.

'How does your AP system resolve conflicts?'

Name the rule and what happens to the losing write: last write wins (simple, silently lossy), version vectors with an application merge (siblings), or CRDTs (automatic merge for counters, sets and similar types). Then say why the data tolerates it.

'Can a global system be strongly consistent and fast?'

Not for writes: a linearizable write must reach a majority of replicas, and the speed of light sets the round trip between regions. Spanner pays Paxos round trips plus a short commit wait. You can make reads fast with leader leases, follower reads at a slightly older timestamp, or caches, by accepting bounded staleness.

'What consistency do you need for X?'

Work from the harm: what does a stale read or a lost write cost the user or the business? Money, inventory and uniqueness need linearizability; a user's own profile needs read-your-writes; social counters need only eventual consistency.

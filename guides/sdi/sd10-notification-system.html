Design a notification system

A notification system looks like a thin wrapper around four vendor APIs until you ask what happens at ten times the usual volume, when a provider is down, or when a worker dies halfway through a send. This chapter follows the interview in four steps: scope and estimate, the pipeline of stateless API, a queue per channel, worker pools and providers, deep dives on retries, duplicates, user settings and rate limits, and the failure modes you close with.

After this chapter you can
  • Estimate daily volume, peak rate, provider calls in flight, log size and SMS spend, and say which channel dominates traffic and which dominates cost
  • Explain what iOS push, Android push, SMS and email each need: an address, a provider, a credential and a way to hear back
  • Draw the pipeline (stateless API, a queue per channel, worker pools, providers) and say what each part removes from the single-server design
  • Choose between at-most-once and at-least-once, and combine retries with backoff, a dead-letter queue and event-id dedup to get effectively-once
  • Place the gate checks (dedup, opt-out, quiet hours, rate limits, templates) in a sensible order and name what to monitor

Step 1 · Understand the problem and establish scope

A notification system delivers short messages to people on four channels: iOS push, Android push, SMS and email. Calling a provider's API is the easy part. The design problem is doing it tens of millions of times a day through third parties that slow down and fail, without losing a message, sending one twice, or contacting someone who opted out.

Agree on scope before drawing. These are the answers this chapter assumes:

  • Channels: iOS push, Android push, SMS, email. In-app inboxes and web push are out of scope.
  • Callers: internal services (checkout, billing, security) and a campaign scheduler. End users never call the system, so every caller can be authenticated.
  • Traffic shape: mostly one message to one user ("your order shipped"), plus campaigns to millions of users at once.
  • Latency: soft real time. A login code leaves within seconds. A promotion may wait minutes when the system is busy, but is not dropped.
  • Consent: users switch channels and categories on or off. Security and legally required messages ignore the switch. Marketing does not.
  • Guarantee: every accepted notification reaches a provider at least once. A rare duplicate is tolerable and a silent loss is not.

Questions worth asking in the first two minutes

  • Which channels, and is it one-way? (SMS users can reply, and STOP must be honoured.)
  • Who triggers a notification: internal services only, or users and bulk campaigns too?
  • How fast must each kind arrive? Does a login code share a deadline with a weekly digest?
  • How many per day, and how spiky? What does the campaign peak look like?
  • Can users opt out, and is any message exempt?
  • Which is worse, a duplicate or a lost message?

The last question fixes the delivery guarantee, and interviewers listen for it.

Traffic, connections, storage and money

The defaults describe an imagined mid-size consumer app. Change a daily volume or the peak factor and watch which channel dominates traffic and which dominates the bill. Calls in flight uses Little's law (arrival rate × time per call). The prices are illustrative: real SMS prices differ by country and route. Background on the method: Back-of-envelope estimation.

Step 2 · Four channels, four providers

Nobody pushes to a phone or texts a number directly. Each channel goes through a provider that owns the last mile, and the four providers differ in what address they need, how we authenticate, and what they tell us afterwards. Step through each path and watch for two things: what we have to store about the user, and what comes back. The contact tables, the retry rules and the opt-out list in the rest of the chapter exist to handle those two.

Gathering contact data

The addresses come from three moments:

  • Sign-up and profile edits. The user types an email address and a phone number. Store them only after verification (a code sent to the address or number), so typos do not cost SMS money and strangers do not get messages.
  • App launch and login. The app asks the OS for its push token and sends it to us with the user id every time it starts. Tokens change on reinstall and restore, so the upload is an upsert keyed by device, and last_seen_at lets a cleanup job drop devices that went quiet.
  • Provider feedback. Hard bounces, STOP replies and dead-token errors flip a status column, so the next send skips that address.

A user therefore owns many rows: several devices, maybe two emails, a phone number, and a set of preference rows.

Step 3 · Deep dive: never lose it, never send it twice

▶ Video lesson: Reliability: retries, duplicates and the event id 5:39 · 12 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Providers time out, workers die in the middle of a send, and networks lose the answer to a request that succeeded. A queue-based design can keep one of two promises, and the difference is where the worker acknowledges a message:

  • At-most-once: acknowledge first, then send. A crash after the acknowledgement loses the notification. There are no duplicates, ever.
  • At-least-once: send first, then acknowledge. A crash before the acknowledgement makes the queue hand the message to another worker. Nothing is lost, and the user may be notified twice.

A third promise, exactly-once, would need the worker to learn whether a send happened even when the answer is lost, and no design can guarantee that over a network that drops answers. The practical target is at-least-once plus a check that makes the repeat harmless: effectively once.

Where the acknowledgement goes

Acknowledge, then send

At-most-once. The queue forgets the message before the provider has it, so a worker crash is a silent loss.

def run_once(queue, provider):
    msg = queue.receive()
    queue.ack(msg)             # the queue forgets the message here
    provider.send(msg)         # a crash on this line loses the notification

Send, record, then acknowledge

At-least-once. A crash anywhere before the last line causes a redelivery, never a loss. The sent record makes the repeat harmless.

def run_once(queue, provider, sent):
    msg = queue.receive()
    if not sent.exists(msg.event_id):
        provider.send(msg)
        sent.set(msg.event_id)
    queue.ack(msg)             # a crash before this line means a redelivery, never a loss

Retry with backoff, and when not to retry

A worker calls a flaky provider. Watch what the delay queue does between attempts, then switch to a long outage and to a dead token. Delays here are seconds to keep the picture small. Production backoff runs from seconds to minutes. More retry patterns are in Reliability patterns.

A crash after the send: duplicates and dedup by event id

The queue hands a message to a worker for a lease of 5 seconds. If the worker has not acknowledged by then, the queue offers the message to someone else. Run the three scenarios and count the buzzes on the user's phone.

A timeout is an unknown, not a failure

If a provider call times out, the worker cannot tell whether the provider accepted it. Retrying risks a duplicate and giving up risks a loss, and a notification system picks the duplicate. What we control is the part around the call: acknowledge only after the send, remember the event id once the provider has said yes, and use the provider's own collapse key where it has one (apns-collapse-id on iOS makes two pushes with the same id replace each other on the phone). SMS and email have no equivalent, so a duplicate there stays a duplicate.

The worker loop

import random
import time

MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5
PERMANENT = {400, 404, 410}               # retrying cannot help: a bad payload or a dead token


def backoff(attempt: int, base: float = 2.0, cap: float = 300.0) -> float:
    """Full jitter: wait a random time up to base * 2 ** (attempt - 1), capped."""
    return random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** (attempt - 1)))


def handle(msg, queue, provider, sent, log, registry):
    key = f"{msg.event_id}:{msg.channel}"
    if sent.exists(key):                           # a redelivery of something we already sent
        queue.ack(msg)
        return
    if time.time() > msg.expires_at:               # a login code that is 10 minutes late is worse than none
        log.update(msg.id, "expired")
        queue.ack(msg)
        return

    try:
        status, retry_after = provider.send(msg)   # the network call
    except TimeoutError:
        status, retry_after = 503, None            # unknown outcome: treat it as retryable

    if status < 300:
        sent.set(key, ttl=86_400)                  # remember the event id for a day
        log.update(msg.id, "sent")
        queue.ack(msg)
    elif status in PERMANENT:
        log.update(msg.id, f"failed {status}")
        if status in (404, 410):                   # APNs 410 or FCM UNREGISTERED: the token is dead
            registry.remove_token(msg.device_id)
        queue.ack(msg)                             # never retry
    elif msg.attempt >= MAX_ATTEMPTS:
        queue.dead_letter(msg)                     # park it where a human will see it
        log.update(msg.id, "dead-lettered")
    else:
        delay = retry_after or backoff(msg.attempt)   # obey Retry-After when the provider sends one
        queue.retry_later(msg, delay)                 # a delay queue: no sleeping inside the worker
        log.update(msg.id, f"retry {msg.attempt + 1}")
Three outcomes are kept apart on purpose: success (record, then acknowledge), a permanent error (log it, clean up, acknowledge, never retry) and a transient error (retry later with jitter, or park it). Queue leases work as in Amazon SQS visibility timeouts.

Step 3 · Deep dive: settings, templates and rate limits

Three decisions sit between "a service asked for a notification" and "it is on a queue": does this person want it, what does it say, and has this person had enough today. All three run in the API, so a message that will never be sent costs almost nothing.

  • Settings are one row per user, channel and category: on or off, plus quiet hours and a time zone. Security alerts and legal notices are marked non-optional, so a settings bug cannot silence them. Reads go through a cache (Caching) and a change writes through to it, so an unsubscribe takes effect in seconds instead of at the next expiry.
  • Templates keep wording out of the callers. The caller sends a template id and parameters, and the service renders the variant for the channel and the user's locale.
  • Rate limits stop one person from being buried by a buggy producer or an eager campaign, and stop a runaway SMS loop from burning the budget.

    ("order_shipped", "sms", "en"): "Hi {first_name}, order {order_id} has shipped.",
    ("order_shipped", "email", "en"): "<p>Hi {first_name}, order {order_id} is on the way.</p>",

def render(template, channel, locale, params):
    body = TEMPLATES.get((template, channel, locale)) or TEMPLATES[(template, channel, "en")]
    return body.format(**params)

Check preferences again at send time for anything that waits

A promotion built at 08:00 for a 09:00 send, or a message stuck in a backed-up queue for twenty minutes, can outlive the user's choice. For marketing and scheduled messages, re-read the cached preference in the worker just before the provider call and drop the message if it is now off. Skip the second check for login codes and alerts: they cannot wait, and the extra lookup is one more dependency on the hot path. Signal: if a message can sit in a queue longer than a user takes to change their mind, check twice.

Step 3 · Deep dive: security, monitoring and tracking

What a provider outage does to one channel's queue

Queue depth is the number to watch, but age is the better alarm: a deep queue at high throughput is healthy, while one old message means someone is waiting. Try an outage on a single channel. If worker capacity is at or below the arrival rate, the backlog never drains, which is the signal to add workers before the outage ends.

Security, monitoring and tracking (click each)

Who may call the API (security)

Every producer service gets its own credential, bound to a quota and a list of allowed categories. Requests are signed, or sent over mutually authenticated TLS, and the API rejects anything else before it reads the body. An open notification endpoint lets anyone send spam, from your own domain, to your own users.

Provider credentials are a separate matter. The APNs signing key, the FCM service account and the SMS and email API keys live in a secrets manager, are readable by the worker pools only, and rotate on a schedule. The API tier never sees them.

What goes into a payload (security)

A push banner can appear on a locked screen, an SMS crosses carrier networks as plain text, and email passes through many servers. Keep full card numbers, passwords and long-lived links out of all three. One-time codes and links that expire are fine.

Verify provider webhooks by signature, or an attacker can mark real users as bounced or unsubscribed. Keep personal data out of logs, and restrict who can read the notification log.

What to watch (monitoring)
  • Age of the oldest message per queue. Alarm against each channel's deadline: seconds for a login code, minutes for email.
  • Provider responses by status code. A jump in 5xx or 429 is an outage or throttling. A jump in dead-token errors points to a bug in token registration or a mass uninstall.
  • Dead-letter queue size. Every message there failed all its attempts. Alarm on any growth.
  • Time to the provider (p50 and p99). Slow calls hold workers and silently shrink capacity.
  • Bounce and complaint rates for email. Providers throttle or block senders above their published thresholds.
  • SMS spend against budget. A producer bug can burn a month of budget in an hour.
Event tracking and analytics

A notification moves through states: queued, sent (a provider accepted it), delivered (a receipt says it arrived, where receipts exist), opened or clicked (the app or a tracked link reports back), unsubscribed. Our own log supplies the first two. Provider webhooks supply delivery receipts, bounces and complaints. The app's SDK reports opens, and tracked links report clicks.

Write every state change as an event to a stream and let an analytics store read from it: dashboards of delivery and open rates by template and send time, and feedback into the rate limiter ("stop pushing people who never open"). Read open rates with care. 'Sent' only means a provider accepted the request, and mail apps that prefetch images can inflate email opens.

Step 4 · Wrap up

Failure modes and what the design does about them

Notice how often the answer is the same three parts: a queue to wait in, an event id to make a repeat safe, and a log to see what happened.

Follow-up questions interviewers ask next

How do you send one message to 20 million users?

The campaign scheduler resolves the audience in pages, turns each page into a batch job of about a thousand recipients, and feeds those jobs through the same API and queues. Rate limit the scheduler so a campaign cannot outrun worker or provider capacity, and spread sends across time zones. A campaign is many ordinary notifications with a lower priority.

How do you keep login codes from waiting behind a promotion?

Give each channel two or three queues by priority (urgent, normal, bulk) and let workers take from the urgent queue first, or reserve a share of workers for it. Bulk can be slow. It cannot block the urgent kind.

How do you schedule a notification for later?

Store send_at with the log row. A scheduler (a time-bucketed table or a delay queue) releases due rows into the normal queues. Preferences are checked again at release time, because the user may have changed their mind since.

How do you survive a provider outage for good?

Put two providers for a channel behind the worker's provider interface, watch the error rate with a circuit breaker, and move a share of traffic to the backup when the first one degrades. Keep the backup warm with a trickle of real traffic so its reputation and limits are ready.

What changes when you add web push or WhatsApp?

A new queue, a new worker pool, a provider adapter, a contact type and a new value in the preference table. The API, the log and the gate do not change. That is what the channel-per-queue design is for.

The 60-second summary

I would put one authenticated API in front, so producers only say 'event e-17, user 42, template order_shipped'. The API dedups by event id, checks the user's preferences, quiet hours and rate limits, renders the template, writes a row to the notification log, and only then enqueues. There is one durable queue per channel with its own worker pool, so an SMS gateway outage backs up only SMS and each pool scales on its own. Workers are the only code that talks to APNs, FCM, the SMS gateway and the email service. They acknowledge after the send, retry transient errors with exponential backoff and jitter, never retry permanent ones like a dead token, and park the rest in a dead-letter queue. That is at-least-once. The event-id check makes it effectively once, and I accept a rare duplicate over a loss. I would alarm on the age of the oldest message per queue and feed provider receipts back into the log and analytics.

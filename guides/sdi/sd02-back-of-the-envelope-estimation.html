Back-of-the-envelope estimation

An estimate is how you decide, in two minutes, whether a design needs one server or fifty. This chapter drills the numbers to know cold (powers of two, the latency ladder, the nines of availability) with calculators you can change, then walks the chain from daily users to QPS, peak QPS and storage on a microblogging service, and shows how to say it aloud so the interviewer hears judgement rather than arithmetic.

After this chapter you can
  • Convert between powers of two and decimal units, and say how many bits or bytes it takes to name a given number of things
  • Place the key latency numbers (memory, SSD, a network hop, a disk seek, an ocean) on a log scale and say what each one rules out
  • Turn an availability target into downtime per day and per year, and compute the availability of tiers in series and in parallel
  • Chain daily users into average QPS, peak QPS, storage over five years and egress, with units, in about two minutes
  • Present an estimate aloud: label each assumption, round early, name the input the answer is sensitive to, and finish on the design decision

What an estimate is for

An estimate answers one question: does this design have to look different at this scale? One server or a fleet, one database or shards, images served directly or through a CDN. You are not predicting the future. You are finding the one or two numbers that make the architecture change, and finding them in two minutes.

Interviewers read three things off an estimate:

  • Do you know the conversion factors without looking them up (seconds in a day, bytes in a gigabyte)?
  • Do you state your assumptions and units, so they can correct a number without redoing your reasoning?
  • Does the number change the design, or is it decoration?

This chapter is about the numbers and the habits. The hld track has a second set of worked examples (a photo feed, a URL shortener, video) in Back-of-envelope estimation; here the worked estimate is a microblogging service with different assumptions.

What the interviewer is really asking

The interviewer is not checking your multiplication. They are watching whether you pick round numbers, label them, keep units, and stop the moment a decision is clear. A confident 'about 14 thousand reads a second at peak, so we need a cache' beats 13,888.9 every time.

Powers of two

▶ Video lesson: The numbers you must know cold 4:15 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Memory sizes, disk blocks and address spaces are all powers of two, so estimates start there. One fact does most of the work: every 10 extra bits multiplies the value by about 1,000 (exactly 1,024). So 2^10 is about a thousand, 2^20 about a million, 2^30 about a billion and 2^40 about a trillion.

Treat 1 KB as 1,000 bytes unless the exact figure matters. The error is 2.4% at the kilobyte rung and grows by roughly 2.5 points per rung (4.9% at MB, 7.4% at GB, 10% at TB), which is small next to the assumptions underneath. If you need to be exact, say KiB and mean 1,024.

Learn the 10-bit rungs by heart (10, 20, 30, 40, 50) and the two fixed widths that keep appearing (32 and 64).

The reverse question comes up constantly: how many bits does it take to name N things? The answer is the ceiling of log2 of N. Eight billion people need 33 bits. 32 bits give only 4.3 billion values, which is why 4-byte ids run out and 8-byte ids are the default. Try your own numbers in the calculator.

Powers of two, bits and bytes

The exponent p gives 2^p exactly, as a byte count in binary and decimal units, and the error you make by reading it as 10 to the power 0.3 p. The count n gives the bits and whole bytes needed to name n things, and what an id column of that width costs.

Latency numbers

You do not need exact latencies. You need the order of magnitude of each rung and the ratios between rungs, because a design is a decision about which rungs a request touches. Three ratios are worth carrying around:

  • Memory, SSD and disk are each roughly 100 to 1,000 times slower than the one before, for a random access.
  • One hop across the data centre costs about as much as five SSD reads, so a request path with many sequential service calls spends most of its time on hops.
  • Distance has a floor. A round trip between continents cannot be tuned below tens of milliseconds; the only fix is to move the data.

Walk up the latency ladder

Each bar is one operation on a log scale, so equal steps up mean 1,000 times more. Step through the ten operations, then switch to the second scenario, which asks the opposite question: how many of each can one thread do in a second?

The ladder has two uses. The first is a latency budget. Take the response time you promise (say 300 ms at the 99th percentile), subtract what the user's own network costs (50 to 100 ms on mobile), and spend the rest on hops. Twenty sequential calls inside a data centre cost about 10 ms. The same twenty made in parallel cost about 0.5 ms. One extra cross-continent round trip costs 150 ms. Say the budget, not an adjective like "fast".

The second is capacity: rates become device counts when you divide. If one spinning disk does about 100 random reads a second, 10,000 reads a second needs around 100 disks, which is why such workloads moved to SSDs and memory. If a link carries about 100 MB a second, a 100 GB copy takes about 17 minutes however fast the sender is. The speeds in the calculator are inputs, not facts: change them to match the hardware you assume.

How long does it take to move this much data?

Sequential speeds in decimal units (1 GB = 1,000 MB). The last two outputs answer: how many devices reading in parallel finish the job in the target time?

Availability and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) in which a service does its job. Targets are quoted in nines, and the skill is converting a target into a downtime budget you can feel. Each extra nine allows ten times less downtime and usually costs more than ten times the effort.

Say what "down" means before you quote a number: errors, latency above a threshold, or one feature? Say also whether you count time or requests. A service that fails one request in a thousand all year is at 99.9% by requests and was never "down".

Each nine cuts the budget by ten

Bar height is log10 of the seconds of downtime a year allows, so each nine lands one gridline lower. The notes say what each level forces you to build.

Real services are chains, and two rules turn component numbers into a system number:

  • In series (every request needs every tier), availabilities multiply. Three tiers at 99.9% deliver 0.999³ ≈ 99.7%, about 26 hours of downtime a year instead of 9.
  • In parallel (any one replica is enough), the unavailabilities multiply. Two independent replicas at 99.9% are both down with probability 0.001² = one in a million, so the tier is 99.9999%.

Nines, downtime budgets and chains of tiers

Set a target to see its downtime budget. Set the number of tiers in series and the availability of each to see what the chain delivers and what every tier would have to reach to hit the target. Replicas assume independent failures and instant failover.

Why two replicas rarely give the textbook number

Shared fate

Two app servers on one rack, configured from the same file and updated by the same deploy step. A bad config push or a bug in the release takes both down at once. The parallel formula promised 99.9999%; you get about the availability of one server plus however fast you can roll back.

Independent replicas

Replicas in different zones, deployed one at a time with a health check between them, and no shared dependency on the request path. One failing is invisible to users, and the formula starts to apply. The remaining risk is whatever is still shared: DNS, the config service, the release itself.

From daily users to storage: the chain

Every capacity estimate is the same chain of conversions: users → actions → QPS → peak QPS → storage and bandwidth. Here it is on a microblogging service with short posts, a timeline of recent posts and a few images. All numbers are assumptions chosen for this example; in an interview you say each one aloud, which is the point.

  • 50 million monthly active users, 40% of whom open the app on a given day.
  • A daily user loads the timeline 20 times (refreshes and scrolls) and posts 0.6 times on average. Most people read and a few post a lot.
  • A post record (text up to 280 characters, ids, timestamp, counters) is about 300 bytes.
  • 12% of posts carry one image, stored at about 400 KB after resizing.
  • Everything is kept for 5 years, in 3 copies.
  • Peak traffic is 3 times the daily average.
  • A timeline load returns about 250 KB: the text of 20 posts plus a few image thumbnails.

The estimate, with every assumption editable

The defaults reproduce the diagram. Change one input and watch which outputs move: the share active daily scales everything; the image share and size scale storage; the peak factor scales only the peak figures. Storage uses decimal units.

The peak factor is the least obvious input. It is the ratio between the busiest period and the daily average, and it depends on the product: roughly 1.5 to 2 for a business tool used in office hours, 2 to 4 for a consumer app with an evening habit, and 10 or more for ticket sales and live events. Ask which one you are designing. The simulation shows where a factor of 3 comes from and what it costs to size for the average instead.

Round, label, say it aloud

Four habits make an estimate sound like judgement instead of arithmetic:

  • Round before you calculate. 86,400 seconds is 10^5, 365 days is 4 × 10^2, 1,024 is 1,000. The errors are small (15%, 10%, 2%) and mostly cancel. A decision that flips on a 15% error was not robust anyway.
  • Write a unit on every number. "14k" is noise; "14k reads per second" is a claim. Most wrong answers come from mixed units: bits and bytes, MB and MiB, per day and per second.
  • Say each assumption as you make it, with its value ("assume 40% are active daily"). The interviewer can then change one number without re-reading your reasoning.
  • Stop at the decision. If 14k and 40k reads per second both lead to "cache plus replicas", the next digit is wasted time. Then name the input the answer is most sensitive to.

Two shortcuts save most of the arithmetic. A million requests a day is about 12 a second (divide by 10^5, add 15%), so 400 million a day is about 4,600 a second. And to multiply, count zeros: 20 million × 20 is (2 × 10^7) × (2 × 10^1) = 4 × 10^8.

The same estimate, said aloud in about 40 seconds

'I'll assume 50 million monthly users, 40% active daily, so 20 million a day, each loading the timeline 20 times: 400 million loads, about 4,600 a second on average and 14,000 at peak. Posting is 0.6 a day each, so 12 million posts, 139 a second and about 400 at peak, which one primary handles. A post is 300 bytes, so text is 3.6 GB a day, but 12% carry a 400 KB image: 576 GB a day, about 3 petabytes over five years with three copies. So reads need a cache and replicas, images need object storage, and 28 gigabits a second at peak needs a CDN.'

Commonly asked estimates

Six questions you will be asked, with the one-paragraph answer

How many servers do I need?

Peak requests per second divided by what one server handles, then headroom and redundancy. Here 14k ÷ 1k per server is 14 servers; plan for 20 or more, so that a deploy in progress or a lost zone does not overload the rest. The number that deserves a sentence is the per-server rate: say "assuming 1k requests per second per server, which I would load-test".

How much storage do I need?

Writes per day × bytes per write × 365 × years × copies, plus 20 to 50% for indexes and metadata. Split blobs from records: here the records are about 20 TB and the images 3.2 PB, so they live in different systems.

How much bandwidth do I need?

Peak QPS × bytes per response × 8 bits. Here 14k × 250 KB is 3.5 GB per second, or 28 Gbit/s. Compute ingress (writes) and egress (reads) separately; reads usually dominate. A 10 Gbit/s network card carries about 1.25 GB per second, so 28 Gbit/s is at least three machines' worth of network before they do any other work.

How much cache memory do I need?

Find the hot set. The text of the last 3 days is 3 × 3.6 GB = 10.8 GB, which fits in RAM on one machine. A day of 100 KB thumbnails (1.44 million images) is about 144 GB, a handful of cache nodes. If you do not know the skew, assume the common 80/20 pattern: 20% of the items take 80% of the reads, so cache about 20% of a day's distinct reads.

How long must an id or a short key be?

N items and an alphabet of b symbols need ceil(log base b of N) symbols. Posts: 12 million × 365 × 5 is about 22 billion. In binary that is 35 bits, so an 8-byte integer is plenty. As text in base 62 (a to z, A to Z, 0 to 9) it is 6 characters, because 62^6 is about 57 billion.

How do I get from monthly to daily users?

Ask. If the interviewer will not say, state a ratio: engaged consumer apps often see 20 to 50% of monthly users on a given day, tools used weekly far less. The rest of the estimate scales linearly with it, so it is a lever to name, not a risk to hide.

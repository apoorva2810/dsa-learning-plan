Design a news feed system

A news feed shows each user the recent posts of the people they follow. The difficulty is amplification: one post must reach five feeds or eight million. This chapter follows the interview in four steps: scope and a back-of-the-envelope estimate, the two flows (publishing and retrieval) as stepped diagrams, then deep dives on fan-out on write against fan-out on read, the hybrid that stops one celebrity from clogging the queue, the fan-out service, and the cache tiers that make a page cost about nine reads. Every mechanism runs in a simulation, and two calculators let you change the numbers.

After this chapter you can
  • Estimate feed opens, posts, the cache operations of push and of pull, feed cache size and media egress from a handful of assumptions
  • Explain fan-out on write and fan-out on read with operation counts, for an ordinary account and for one with millions of followers
  • State the hybrid rule: where the threshold comes from, what the read path merges, and how two thresholds stop flip-flopping
  • Describe the fan-out service step by step and say why feed lists hold ids, tolerate duplicate inserts and can be thrown away
  • Trace one feed page through the cache tiers, count its reads, and explain cursor pagination and the rebuild by pull

Step 1 · Understand the problem and establish scope

A news feed is the first screen of a social app: recent posts from the people and pages you are connected to, newest first. The product is easy to describe. The design is hard because of amplification: one post has to appear in the feed of every connection of its author, and that number runs from 5 to 8 million.

Agree on scope before drawing. These are the answers this chapter assumes:

  • Clients: mobile and web, through the same API.
  • Features: publish a post (text and one optional photo or short video), read a feed, scroll back through older posts. Likes, comments, search and notifications are out of scope.
  • Connections: ordinary users have mutual friends, 180 on average. Public figures are followed one way, and the biggest has 8 million followers. The feed treats both the same: an edge from an author to a reader.
  • Order: newest first. Ranking by relevance is a wrap-up topic.
  • Scale: 35 million daily active users, each opening the feed about 6 times a day and publishing about one post every four days.
  • Latency: the first page of a feed in under 300 ms at the 99th percentile, measured at the web tier.
  • Freshness: friends see a new post within about 10 seconds. Eventual consistency is fine.
  • Durability: a published post is never lost. A feed list may be lost, because it can be rebuilt.

Questions worth asking in the first two minutes

  • Is it a mobile app, a web app, or both? (It shapes how pagination and refresh work.)
  • What can a user post: text only, or photos and video? (Media decides whether a CDN is in the first sketch.)
  • Is the feed in time order or ranked? (Ranking adds a service and a latency budget.)
  • Are connections mutual friendships, one-way follows, or both? What is the biggest audience an account can have?
  • How many daily users are there, how often do they open the feed, and how often do they post?
  • How fast must a page load, and how soon must a friend see a new post?

The fourth question is the one interviewers listen for. Audience sizes are wildly skewed, and the answer decides between pushing posts to readers and pulling them.

Traffic, fan-out work, cache size and media

The defaults are this chapter's assumptions. Change the posts per day, the opens per day or the number of friends and watch the two cache operations rows: reads outnumber posts, and that ratio drives the whole design. Method: 2 · Back-of-the-envelope estimation.

Read the outputs as decisions:

  • About 2,400 feed opens a second, 7,300 at the peak. Reading is the hot path, so a page has to be assembled from memory, never from a database query per open.
  • About 100 new posts a second. Writing posts is small. The post store is not where this design gets hard.
  • Push costs about 20,700 cache operations a second. Pull costs about 438,000, roughly 21 times as many. Opens outnumber posts 24 to 1, so paying the 180-fold multiplier on every open costs far more than paying it on every post.
  • About 87,500 post lookups a second at the peak to fill pages of 12. Post bodies need their own cache in front of the post store.
  • 720 GB for one copy of the feed lists (60 million lists of 500 ids at 24 bytes), so a sharded in-memory cluster, not one box. The 24 bytes is an assumption: a general-purpose sorted set costs several times more per entry, so measure before quoting it.
  • 40 seconds to push one post of the 8-million-follower account, with the whole fan-out tier busy. That single number is the celebrity problem.
  • 525 GB of photos a day, and about 42 Gbit/s at the peak for first pages alone. Media goes to object storage and a CDN, never through the feed path.

Step 2 · Propose a high-level design

The system does two jobs that share almost nothing except data. Publishing receives a post and makes it reach many feeds. Retrieval answers a reader who asks for a page. Draw them separately and keep each one short. Step through the diagram in the order you would sketch it on a whiteboard: every box is there because a number in the estimate asks for it. Publish, read a page, follow and unfollow. The feed returns hydrated posts and a cursor, never raw ids.

Three details are worth saying aloud:

  • Post ids are time-sortable 64-bit ids, generated without coordination (7 · Design a unique ID generator in distributed systems). Sorting by id sorts by time, so an id is also a position in a feed. That is why the cursor is just the id of the last post shown.
  • The idempotency key on POST /v1/posts lets a client retry after a lost response without publishing twice. Follow is a PUT on a resource, so repeating it changes nothing.
  • The response carries posts, not ids. Turning ids into posts is the server's job, so the client makes one request per page.

Step 3 · Deep dive: fan-out on write or fan-out on read

▶ Video lesson: Fan-out on write, fan-out on read, and the hybrid 5:55 · 9 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

There are two places to pay for the amplification.

Fan-out on write (push) pays when the post is created. The system copies the post id into the feed list of every recipient, so each list is ready before anyone asks and reading is one lookup.

Fan-out on read (pull) pays when the feed is opened. A post is stored once, in its author's timeline. The feed service finds whom the reader follows, reads each author's recent posts and merges them. Publishing is one write.

You have already seen a push for Mina: 5 friends and 7 operations. The next simulation pushes a post from @juno, with 8 million followers. The one after it pulls, first for an ordinary open and then for @juno's post. Watch the counters on the boxes and the clock.

Push or pull for one author: which side pays?

The defaults describe @juno. Then try an ordinary account: 180 followers, 0.25 posts a day, and 0.58 of the followers active on a given day (35 million daily users out of 60 million monthly). The follower count cancels out of the first comparison, so that one depends only on how often the author posts against how often followers open the app. The follower count matters for the last two rows, which say how long one post occupies the fan-out tier.

Where the work lands

Fan-out on write (push)

  • A post costs one write plus one insert per recipient: 7 operations for Mina's 5 friends, about 8 million for @juno.
  • An open costs one list read, then 4 batched reads to turn ids into a page.
  • Friends see the post when a worker reaches their list: about 250 ms for Mina, 40 s for the last of @juno's followers.
  • Wasted work: inserts into lists of people who never open the app that day.
  • Breaks on authors with millions of followers. One post occupies the fan-out tier and every ordinary post waits behind it.

Fan-out on read (pull)

  • A post costs one write, whoever the author is.
  • An open costs 1 graph read and 180 timeline reads, then the same 4 reads to turn ids into a page. The page waits for the slowest read: if 1 read in 100 takes 20 ms, 84% of opens meet one.
  • Friends see the post the moment they ask. There is no propagation delay.
  • Wasted work: none at write time, but the merge is redone on every open.
  • Breaks on every open of every user, and worst for users who follow many accounts.

The signal for choosing

Two tests, and they are separate. Cost: compare how often the author posts with how often each follower opens the app. The follower count cancels out of that comparison. Time: divide the audience by the fan-out rate and compare it with the freshness goal. An ordinary account passes both tests and is pushed. An account whose audience takes longer to write than the freshness goal fails the time test, whatever the operation counts say, and is pulled.

Step 3 · Deep dive: the hybrid for celebrities

Almost every large feed ends with the same answer: push for ordinary accounts, pull for the few accounts that are too big to push, and merge the two when a feed is read. The choice is a flag on the author, not a per-post decision. The research behind it is the Feeding Frenzy paper in the references: decide per producer whether to prepare its events in advance or fetch them at query time.

  • The threshold comes from time, not taste. The fan-out tier writes 200,000 entries a second (an assumption). Keep one post below half of the 10-second freshness goal and the limit is 5 s × 200,000 = 1,000,000 followers. At 8 million followers, a post would take 40 seconds.
  • The flag has two thresholds. Promote to pull at 1,000,000 followers and demote only below 800,000, so an account near the line does not flip back and forth.
  • Ordinary accounts skip some friends. Fan-out leaves out friends who muted the author and friends who have been inactive for 30 days, because nobody reads those lists. An inactive friend who returns gets a list rebuilt by pull on the first open.
  • Reads merge two sources. The reader's own list holds everything that was pushed. The feed service also reads the timeline of each public figure the reader follows, usually a handful, and merges the sorted lists by time. The merge is cheap because its width is the number of public figures followed, not the 180 accounts of a pure pull.

Step 3 · Deep dive: the fan-out service

The fan-out service is where pushing is made cheap and safe, so it earns the most detail in an interview. Its input is a post_created event: the post id, the author id and the timestamp. It never needs the post body. It does five things in order, and each one has a reason.

flowchart LR
  EV[post_created event] --> FO[Fan-out service]
  FO -->|1 friend ids| GDB[(Graph DB)]
  FO -->|2 who should get it| UC[(User cache)]
  FO -->|3 post id and recipients| MQ{{Message queue}}
  MQ -->|4 batch of up to 500| FW[Fan-out workers]
  FW -->|5 insert and trim| FC[(News feed cache)]
The service itself does steps 1 to 3 and returns. Steps 4 and 5 belong to the workers and can fall behind without hurting it.

What a feed list holds

The whole post in every list

1 KB per entry × 500 entries × 60 million lists is 30 TB, about 40 times the 720 GB of ids. An edit or a delete must find and rewrite the post in every list that holds it: 180 for an ordinary author, millions for a public figure. Like counts change constantly, so every like would rewrite copies too.

Only the post id

24 bytes per entry, 720 GB for all lists. An edit changes the post store and the post cache entry, once. A delete marks the post deleted, and the page drops it at read time. The price is a hydration step on every open: one batched read for the bodies, one for the authors and one for the counters, about 9 reads in all (shown below).

Three properties keep the lists correct without any coordination between workers:

  • Inserts are idempotent. A list is a sorted set scored by the time-sortable post id. Inserting an id that is already there changes nothing, and inserting out of order lands in the right place. A crashed worker's message is redelivered and the retry is harmless.
  • Lists are bounded. Each insert trims the list to 500 ids, so memory per user is fixed. A reader who scrolls past the end falls back to pulling older posts from the post store.
  • Lists are disposable. They are derived from the follow graph and the post store, so a lost or dropped list is rebuilt by pull the next time its owner opens the app.

Edits, deletes and unfollows never touch a list. They are applied at read time, because a list holds only ids.

Step 3 · Deep dive: retrieving a feed and the cache tiers

▶ Video lesson: Retrieving a feed: nine reads through the cache tiers 4:35 · 8 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Reading is where the user waits, so this is where the cache tiers earn their keep. The feed service computes nothing expensive. It does lookups by key, in three stages, and every stage is a batched read. The simulation builds page 1 for Sana, a reader whose friends were pushed and who follows two public pages, then repeats the request for a reader whose list was lost.

One feed page through the cache tiers

Six tiers hold six kinds of key, so each gets its own size, TTL and eviction. Only the feed tier is a materialised view rather than a copy: it can be rebuilt from the other stores, so losing it costs time and never data. Background on the patterns: Caching.

Pagination: a cursor, not an offset

The response ends with a cursor: the id of the last post shown. The next request asks for posts strictly older than that id, and because ids sort by time, every page starts from a point in the list instead of counting from the top.

An offset counts into a list whose top keeps changing. If three new posts arrive while a reader is on page 1, "items 12 to 23" now begins with the last three posts that reader has already seen, so they appear twice. A deleted post makes an offset skip one instead. Refreshing is the same query with the bound on the other side: ask for posts newer than the first one on screen.

Assembling one page

import heapq

PAGE = 12
CANDIDATES = 60            # read more ids than a page needs, so hidden posts still leave a full page


def load_feed(user_id: int, cursor: int | None = None, limit: int = PAGE) -> dict:
    """One page of a feed. The cursor is the id of the last post shown, and ids sort by time."""
    below = cursor if cursor else MAX_POST_ID

    # stage 1: independent reads (issued together in real code)
    pushed = feed_cache.newest_ids(user_id, below=below, count=CANDIDATES)
    follows = graph_cache.get(user_id)                  # public accounts followed, hidden post ids
    pulled = [timeline_cache.newest_ids(a, below=below, count=CANDIDATES) for a in follows.public]

    # every list is sorted newest first, so a k-way merge keeps the order; k counts public figures only
    ids = [i for i in dict.fromkeys(heapq.merge(pushed, *pulled, reverse=True)) if i not in follows.hidden]

    # stage 2: one batched read for the bodies of the page
    posts = [p for p in post_cache.get_many(ids[:limit]) if p and not p.deleted]

    # stage 3: authors and counters, in parallel in real code
    authors = user_cache.get_many({p.author_id for p in posts})
    counts = counter_cache.get_many([p.id for p in posts], viewer=user_id)

    next_cursor = posts[-1].id if len(posts) == limit else None
    return {"items": [render(p, authors[p.author_id], counts[p.id]) for p in posts], "next_cursor": next_cursor}
A sketch: the cache clients are placeholders. A deleted post makes a page one short, and real code tops it up with the next ids.

Step 4 · Wrap up

Follow-up questions and how to answer them

Rank the feed instead of sorting by time

Keep the chronological pipeline as the candidate generator: the merged list gives a few hundred recent candidates. A ranking service scores them with features such as the reader's affinity with the author, the post's age and its predicted engagement, and returns the top page. Give it a latency budget, for example 50 ms, and fall back to time order when it times out. Ranking changes what a read does with the candidates. It does not change how posts reach lists.

Photos and video

The client uploads the file to object storage with a pre-signed URL before it posts, so the post carries only media ids. A background pipeline makes resized copies, and video is transcoded into several bitrates. The CDN serves the bytes and the JSON carries URLs. See Object storage (S3) & CDNs and 14 · Design YouTube.

A post is edited or deleted

Because lists hold ids, an edit updates the post store and the post cache entry: one place, not 180 lists. A delete marks the post deleted. Hydration drops it from the page, and the feed service reads a few spare ids so the page stays full. The id leaves every list as the list ages and is trimmed.

A user follows, unfollows or mutes someone

The reader's graph record is read for every page, so posts by an author the reader no longer follows are filtered out at once, and the stale ids age out of the list. A new follow needs a small backfill, otherwise the new friend's recent posts are missing until they post again: insert that author's last few post ids into the follower's list or, for a public figure, simply start merging their timeline.

New posts arrive while the reader is looking

Do not push content into a feed that is on screen. The client asks cheaply whether anything newer than its first post exists (a poll, or a tiny 'new posts available' signal over a long-lived connection) and shows a banner. Pull to refresh asks for posts newer than the first one shown. Scrolling down and refreshing up are the same query with the bound on opposite sides.

Scaling the databases

Shard the post store by post id: writes spread evenly and a post lookup touches one shard. Store the follow graph twice, by follower and by followee, each sharded by its first column, because fan-out asks who follows this author while page assembly asks whom this reader follows. Add read replicas for the read-heavy tables and let the caches absorb most reads. See Scaling databases: replication, sharding, partitioning, Databases: SQL vs NoSQL and Consistency models & the CAP theorem.

More than one region

Feed lists are derived data and can be rebuilt, so they need not be replicated between regions. Replicate the post store and the graph, and let each region run fan-out for its own readers. After a failover, lists are rebuilt by pull on the next open. One rebuild is cheap (about 188 operations) but a whole region at once is not, so rate-limit rebuilds.

What to monitor

Page latency at the web tier, p50 and p99. The age of the oldest message in the fan-out queue, which is the freshness goal as a single number. The hit ratio of each cache tier. The rate of rebuilds by pull. List inserts per second and the share of them that land in inactive lists. Ranking timeouts.

The 60-second summary

There are two flows. Publishing writes the post once, then a fan-out service reads the author's friends from the graph store, drops the ones who muted the author or have been away for 30 days, and puts the post id and up to 500 recipients on a queue. Workers insert the id into each friend's cached list, which holds 500 ids and no bodies. Reading is one list read plus batched lookups for bodies, authors and counters: about nine reads and ten milliseconds, with a cursor for the next page.

Pure push breaks for accounts with millions of followers. At 200,000 inserts a second, 8 million followers take 40 seconds and everyone else's posts wait behind them. So accounts above about a million followers are not pushed. Their posts live in their own timeline and are merged into followers' pages at read time. A user whose list is gone is rebuilt by pulling once. Media goes through a CDN. With more time I would add ranking and shard the databases.

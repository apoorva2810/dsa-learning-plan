Design YouTube

Walk the interview for a video platform: pin down the scope, size uploads, storage, encoding and delivery, draw the upload and streaming flows, then go deep on the transcoding pipeline as a task graph with a scheduler and a resource manager, and on the speed, safety and cost optimisations. You finish with an error-handling table and a spoken summary of the whole design.

After this chapter you can
  • Estimate daily uploads, new storage, upload bandwidth, encoding capacity, streaming bandwidth and the CDN bill from a handful of assumptions
  • Draw the high-level design and explain the upload flow and the streaming flow, including why the API tier never carries video bytes
  • Model transcoding as a directed acyclic graph of tasks, find its critical path, and explain how parallel workers and chunking shorten the finish time
  • Describe the transcoding architecture (preprocessor, DAG scheduler, resource manager, task workers, temporary storage) and how its three queues match tasks to workers
  • Name the speed, safety and cost optimisations and when each pays: parallel chunk upload, upload centres, queues between stages, pre-signed URLs, DRM, CDN for popular videos only
  • Sort failures into recoverable and not, per component, and say what the system does in each case

Step 1 · Understand the problem and establish scope

The prompt is one line: design YouTube. Spend the first few minutes turning it into a spec you can design against, write the answers on the board, and point back to them later. These are the assumptions this chapter uses; each number feeds the estimate below.

  • What users do: creators upload videos (up to 4 GB each, a few minutes to an hour long) and add a title, description and thumbnail. Viewers watch on phones, browsers and smart TVs.
  • Left out unless asked: live streaming, search, recommendations, comments, ads, creator analytics.
  • Quality: playback starts in about 2 seconds, adapts to a weak connection instead of freezing, and works for viewers worldwide.
  • Freshness: a new upload is watchable within about 10 minutes.
  • Scale: 20 million daily active users; 1% of them upload one video a day; the average user watches 30 minutes a day.
  • Protection: videos can be public, unlisted or private, and a few partners publish paid titles that must be hard to copy.
  • Platform: managed cloud pieces (object storage, a CDN, queues) are allowed, but say what each one does for you.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer wants to see whether you separate a watch path from an upload path before drawing anything, and whether your questions change the design. Six questions earn that: Which clients and file formats must we support? How large and how long can an upload be? Is it on-demand only, or live as well? How soon must a new video be watchable? Which videos need protection? How many users, uploads and hours of viewing a day? Then say your assumptions aloud and keep going. The estimate does not need their approval.

Estimate: uploads, storage, encoding and delivery

The defaults are this chapter's assumptions, not any company's figures. Change them and read the formula beside each result. Sizes use decimal units (1 TB is 10^12 bytes), and a bitrate in Mbit/s divided by 8 is megabytes per second.

Step 2 · Propose a high-level design

▶ Video lesson: Upload and streaming: the design walk 6:34 · 8 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

What the numbers force

With the default inputs:

  • About 11 Gbit/s of uploads on average, 33 at peak. Pushing that through application servers would need a fleet whose only job is to forward bytes. Clients should write to object storage directly.
  • 240 TB of new storage a day, about 88 PB a year. The renditions are half of it, so the encoding ladder is a cost decision, not a given.
  • About 5,600 cores encoding around the clock, 17,000 at peak. That needs a scheduler and a queue, not a script on a few machines.
  • 0.83 Tbit/s streaming on average, 2.5 at peak, 75 times the upload rate. Only a CDN carries that, and its bill (about $2.7 million a month here) is where cost work pays.

The rule that shapes the drawing

Video bytes never pass through the API servers. Uploads go from the app to object storage. Playback goes from a CDN edge to the player. The API servers handle small requests: who is uploading, where to put the bytes, what to play. The deeper design of each box is in Case study: video streaming and Object storage (S3) & CDNs; here we draw the boxes in whiteboard order and then open the heavy one. Step 3 · Deep dive: the transcoding pipeline as a DAG

▶ Video lesson: The transcoding DAG 6:34 · 8 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Why every upload is transcoded

Transcoding means decoding the creator's file and encoding it again, in the formats, sizes and bitrates players need. Three reasons:

  • Size. Raw 1080p video at 30 frames per second is about 93 MB/s (0.75 Gbit/s, roughly 340 GB an hour). The same picture as streaming-quality H.264 is about 5 Mbit/s, 150 times smaller. Cameras compress already, but not enough.
  • Compatibility. Creators upload MOV, MKV or MP4 files holding H.264, HEVC or something stranger, while players accept a narrower set. A container (MP4, MOV, MKV, WebM) is the file format that holds the video and audio tracks and metadata. A codec (H.264, HEVC, VP9, AV1 for video; AAC or Opus for audio) is the algorithm that compresses one track.
  • Networks differ. A viewer on a crowded train needs about 0.5 Mbit/s; one on fibre can take 8. Each upload becomes several renditions (resolution and bitrate pairs), so the player can pick the one its connection supports.

Why the work is a graph

One upload needs many different jobs, and they depend on each other. Channels differ too: one wants a watermark, a partner wants DRM, a podcast needs only audio. So the steps are not a fixed script. Model them as a directed acyclic graph (DAG): each node is a task, each arrow means "needs the output of", and there are no cycles, so a valid order always exists. Tasks with no path between them can run at the same time.

Run the DAG on a pool of workers

The number on each node is its minutes of work. A free worker takes the ready task with the longest chain still ahead of it. Compare one worker with three, then split the 1080p encode into four parts that can run side by side. The lanes show what each worker does, minute by minute.

Workers do not beat the chain

The finish time is set by the critical path: the longest chain of tasks that must run one after another. Here that is Inspect, Split, Watermark, the 1080p encode, Package and Publish: 15 minutes. Total work is 26 minutes, so one worker needs 26, three workers need 15, and thirty workers still need 15. To go faster, shorten the chain: split the longest task into chunks. Then the next-longest chain, through the 720p encode, becomes the limit (11 minutes). The signal in practice: if workers sit idle while one long task runs, chunk that task; if every worker is busy, add workers.

A task graph in Python

from graphlib import TopologicalSorter

# task -> (minutes of work, tasks whose output it needs)
PIPELINE = {
    "inspect":   (1, []),
    "split":     (1, ["inspect"]),
    "watermark": (2, ["split"]),
    "thumbs":    (2, ["split"]),
    "audio":     (2, ["split"]),
    "enc_360":   (3, ["watermark"]),
    "enc_720":   (4, ["watermark"]),
    "enc_1080":  (8, ["watermark"]),
    "package":   (2, ["enc_360", "enc_720", "enc_1080", "audio"]),
    "publish":   (1, ["package", "thumbs"]),
}


def critical_path(tasks):
    """Length of the longest chain of dependent tasks, and the chain itself."""
    graph = {name: set(needs) for name, (_, needs) in tasks.items()}
    finish, via = {}, {}
    for name in TopologicalSorter(graph).static_order():      # a task always comes after the tasks it needs
        minutes, needs = tasks[name]
        finish[name] = minutes + max((finish[n] for n in needs), default=0)
        via[name] = max(needs, key=finish.get) if needs else None
    end = max(finish, key=finish.get)
    length, chain = finish[end], []
    while end:
        chain.append(end)
        end = via[end]
    return length, chain[::-1]


def ready(tasks, done, running=()):
    """Tasks whose inputs are all finished and that nobody has started yet."""
    return [n for n, (_, needs) in tasks.items()
            if n not in done and n not in running and all(d in done for d in needs)]


total = sum(minutes for minutes, _ in PIPELINE.values())
length, chain = critical_path(PIPELINE)
print(total, length, " -> ".join(chain))      # 26 15 inspect -> split -> watermark -> enc_1080 -> package -> publish
print(ready(PIPELINE, done={"inspect", "split", "watermark"}))     # ['thumbs', 'audio', 'enc_360', 'enc_720', 'enc_1080']
The scheduler's two questions in code: how long is the longest chain, and which tasks can start now.

Step 3 · Deep dive: the transcoding architecture

The graph is a plan. Something has to build it from the channel's settings, hand each task to a machine that can run it, carry on when machines die, and clean up afterwards. A preprocessor, a DAG scheduler, a resource manager and the workers share that work. Step through the diagram, then watch the resource manager's three queues do the matching. The signal for building all this: with one kind of worker, a plain queue is enough. Add the worker queue and capability matching once workers differ (GPU or CPU) or tasks carry priorities.

Step 3 · Deep dive: faster, safer, cheaper

▶ Video lesson: Speed, safety and cost 4:42 · 9 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Speed

Speed here means the time from the creator pressing upload to a playable video. Three changes attack it, each at a different stage.

  • Upload in parallel chunks. The app cuts the file at keyframes. A keyframe starts a group of pictures (GOP) that decodes without help from earlier frames, so each chunk stands alone. Chunks go up in parallel, a failed chunk is retried alone, an interrupted upload resumes at the first missing chunk, and processing can start on chunk 1 while chunk 4 is still travelling.
  • Upload centres near the creator. One TCP connection can have only about one window of data in flight per round trip, so its throughput is capped near the window size divided by the round-trip time. With a 256 KiB window, a 250 ms round trip to a distant region caps one connection near 8 Mbit/s. A 15 ms round trip to a nearby centre lifts the cap to about 140 Mbit/s, and then the creator's own link is the limit. Run ingestion endpoints in several regions and send each creator to the closest.
  • Queues between the stages. If the upload handler calls the splitter and the splitter calls the encoder, each waits for the next and one crash loses the job. A queue between each pair lets the stages work at their own pace and overlap in time (see the comparison after the simulation, and Message queues & streaming (Kafka) for the queue itself).

Which to reach for: chunked uploads when files are large or links are flaky; an upload centre when upload time is long for creators far from your regions (compare it by region); queues between stages almost always, because stages differ in speed and fail independently.

How the stages are connected

Stages call each other

The upload handler calls the splitter, which calls the encoder, and each waits for the answer.

  • A slow encoder keeps upload handlers busy, and a burst of uploads uses them all up.
  • A crash anywhere loses the job and the creator starts again.
  • The stages can only be scaled together, and adding a step means editing the caller.

A queue between every pair of stages

Each stage reads its input queue and writes to the next one.

  • A burst becomes a backlog, and the autoscaler adds workers where the queue grows.
  • A message stays until its consumer acknowledges it, so a crash causes a redelivery, not a loss.
  • Stages overlap, scale on their own queue depth, and a new stage (a moderation scan) is one more consumer.
  • The price: results arrive a little later than a direct call, and every consumer must tolerate duplicates.

Safety

Two different things need protecting: the platform (against anonymous uploads and forged requests) and the content (against copying).

  • Who may upload: a pre-signed URL for each upload. The API authenticates the creator, checks the quota, picks the storage key itself and signs a URL that allows one PUT to that key for a few minutes. Storage checks the signature on its own, so the app never holds storage credentials. A leaked URL is worth one upload slot until it expires. The cloud providers' versions are in Object storage (S3) & CDNs; the code below shows what a signature binds.
  • Who may watch: signed playback URLs. The manifest URL (or a signed cookie) carries an expiry and, for private videos, the viewer's identity. Edge servers validate it without calling the API. Keep the signature out of the CDN's cache key, or every viewer asks for a different object and the hit ratio collapses.
  • Who may copy: encryption and DRM. Segments are encrypted once. The player gets the key from a license server after an entitlement check and decrypts it inside a protected module of the browser or device (Widevine, FairPlay or PlayReady, reached through the browser's Encrypted Media Extensions). Every viewer receives the same ciphertext, so the CDN still caches it. DRM raises the cost of copying; it cannot stop someone filming a screen. Visible watermarks deter re-uploads, and invisible ones can trace a leak to an account.

Pick by exposure: pre-signed uploads and signed playback URLs everywhere, and DRM only where contracts demand it, because it adds a license server, key management and per-device player work.

What a pre-signed URL binds

import hashlib
import hmac
import time
from urllib.parse import parse_qs, urlencode, urlparse

SECRET = b"rotate-me-often"              # known to the API tier and the storage gateway, never to clients


def sign_upload_url(base: str, key: str, user_id: str, max_bytes: int, ttl_s: int = 900) -> str:
    expires = int(time.time()) + ttl_s
    payload = f"PUT\n{key}\n{user_id}\n{max_bytes}\n{expires}".encode()
    sig = hmac.new(SECRET, payload, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
    query = urlencode({"user": user_id, "max": max_bytes, "exp": expires, "sig": sig})
    return f"{base}/{key}?{query}"


def verify_upload(method: str, url: str, content_length: int) -> bool:
    parts = urlparse(url)
    q = {name: values[0] for name, values in parse_qs(parts.query).items()}
    key = parts.path.lstrip("/")
    payload = f"{method}\n{key}\n{q['user']}\n{q['max']}\n{q['exp']}".encode()
    expected = hmac.new(SECRET, payload, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
    return (hmac.compare_digest(expected, q["sig"])          # not forged, and no field was edited
            and time.time() < int(q["exp"])                  # not expired
            and content_length <= int(q["max"]))             # within the size ceiling


url = sign_upload_url("https://up.example.com", "originals/v_5Jx2", "u_42", 4_000_000_000)
print(verify_upload("PUT", url, 600_000_000))                                   # True
print(verify_upload("GET", url, 600_000_000))                                   # False: signed for PUT only
print(verify_upload("PUT", url.replace("v_5Jx2", "v_other"), 600_000_000))      # False: a different key
print(verify_upload("PUT", url, 5_000_000_000))                                 # False: over the size ceiling
The signature covers the method, the key, the user, the size ceiling and the expiry. Change any of them and it no longer matches. Real services use the same idea with a key derived from the account secret.

Cost

Delivery is the biggest line in the bill: about $2.7 million a month for delivery alone with Step 1's defaults. A CDN does not pay off equally for every video, because a cache fill costs money. The first viewer in a region makes the CDN pull the file from your storage, and later viewers share that copy. A popular video has thousands of viewers per fill. A long-tail video has one, so it pays the CDN's delivery price and the fill. The levers, and what each gives up:

  • Put only the popular head on the CDN. Serve the long tail from your own origin servers. The calculator below prices both groups. Cost: a second serving path to run, and slower starts for far-away viewers of tail videos.
  • Encode the tail lazily. Most uploads get a handful of views. Encode the cheap renditions at upload, and the expensive ones (1080p and up, new codecs) once views pass a threshold. Cost: early viewers on fast networks get a lower rung than they could.
  • Keep videos near their audience. A video popular in one country need not be copied to every region. Cost: slower starts when the audience moves.
  • Move cold renditions to cheaper storage. Keep the original, which allows re-encoding with a better codec. Cost: slower restores.
  • Send fewer bits for the same picture. Per-title ladders and newer codecs (HEVC, VP9, AV1) shrink every byte stored and delivered. Cost: encoding CPU and device support, so spend it on videos that get watched.
  • At very large scale, place caches inside ISP networks or build your own CDN. Cost: capital and operations.

The signal for every lever is the same: measure bytes per video, and act where the bytes are concentrated or wasted.

Error handling: recoverable and not

ComponentWhat goes wrongClassWhat the design does
Uploada part fails or the connection dropsRecoverableRetry that part with backoff. After a crash, ask storage which parts exist and send the rest.
Pre-signed URLexpires during a slow uploadRecoverableThe app asks the API for a fresh URL for the same upload session.
Inspect tasknot valid video, or over the size limitNot recoverableReject at once: status FAILED with a reason the creator can read. No retries.
API serverprocess or host diesRecoverableStateless: the load balancer sends the next request elsewhere. Create calls carry an idempotency key.
Metadata DBthe primary diesRecoverablePromote a replica. Reads keep flowing from the cache and replicas.
Metadata cachea node diesRecoverableOther nodes answer, reads fall through to the DB, and a new node joins.
Preprocessorcrashes while splittingRecoverableRun it again from the original. The DAG is rebuilt from the config, so it comes out identical.
DAG schedulerdiesRecoverableTask states live in durable storage. A standby takes over and re-releases tasks not marked done.
Resource managera queue node diesRecoverableQueues are replicated. A replica takes over, and running-queue entries are rebuilt from the leases.
Task workerdies or hangsRecoverableThe lease expires, the task returns to the task queue and another worker repeats it. Output keys are deterministic, so the redo overwrites.
Taskfails on every attempt (corrupt chunk, encoder bug)Not recoverableAfter three attempts: dead-letter queue, video marked FAILED, alert. The creator can re-upload.
CDN edgean edge is down, or a miss storm hits a new videoRecoverableTraffic moves to another edge. Request collapsing and a shield protect the origin.
Sort every failure into one of two classes. Recoverable: retry, fail over or recompute, and nobody notices. Not recoverable: retrying cannot help, so stop, say why and keep the rest of the system healthy.

  Blocked --> Ready: inputs done

Follow-up questions interviewers ask next

Which streaming protocol: HLS or DASH?

Both cut each rendition into segments of a few seconds and describe them in a manifest, over plain HTTP. HLS (Apple, RFC 8216) uses .m3u8 playlists and is native on Apple devices. MPEG-DASH (an ISO standard) uses an XML .mpd manifest and is common on Android, TVs and browsers. Packaging the same fragmented-MP4 segments for both (the CMAF format) lets you store one copy of the video and write two small manifests.

How would live streaming change the design?

Ingest becomes a continuous stream (RTMP, SRT or WebRTC), so transcoding runs in real time and a failed chunk cannot be retried later. Segments are short (2 to 4 seconds), the manifest keeps growing and is refetched every few seconds with a short TTL, and the end-to-end delay is measured in seconds. The per-upload DAG becomes an always-on pipeline per stream.

How do you catch copyrighted or abusive uploads?

Add tasks to the DAG before Publish: a fingerprint match against a reference database and a moderation classifier. A hit holds the video in a review state instead of publishing it. A takedown flips the status, and short-lived signed URLs plus a CDN purge stop the delivery.

A creator premieres a video to a million viewers at once. What breaks?

The first segments are cold at every edge at the same moment. Request collapsing at the edge and an origin shield turn millions of requests into a few origin reads, and warming the first segments before the premiere removes even those. Case study: video streaming covers this, and counting views without a hot row.

How do you cut startup time?

Start on a middle rung with a short first segment, fetch the manifest and first segment in parallel with the watch-page data, keep connections to the CDN warm, and keep the manifest small and cacheable.

How do you scale the metadata database?

Shard by video id, put a cache in front of the watch-page reads, and keep counters off the video row (batched, approximate). Blobs never enter the database; a row only holds the prefix that points at them.

What do you monitor?

Time from upload to publish (median and 95th percentile), the age of the oldest task in the task queue, the dead-letter count, CDN hit ratio and origin egress, rebuffering ratio and startup time as players report them, and cost per delivered GB.

What would you add with more time?

Forensic watermarking for paid titles, per-title encoding ladders, AV1 for the most-watched videos, a second region for the originals, several CDNs with automatic failover, and a review lane for large partner uploads.

A 60-second summary to say out loud

I would split the system into an upload path and a watch path. With 20 million daily users and 1% uploading, that is about 200,000 uploads and 240 TB of new storage a day, while viewers pull about 0.8 Tbit/s, 75 times the upload rate. So video bytes never pass through the API servers: the app uploads in parts straight to object storage with pre-signed URLs, and players stream from a CDN. Each upload becomes a DAG of tasks: inspect, split, watermark, thumbnails, audio and video encodes, package, publish. A DAG scheduler releases ready tasks into a resource manager, which pairs them with idle workers that can run them and uses leases to recover from crashes. Parallel chunk uploads, nearby upload centres and queues between stages cut the time to publish. Pre-signed URLs, signed playback URLs and DRM protect uploads and content, and only the popular head of the catalogue goes through the CDN. Recoverable failures are retried; poison tasks go to a dead-letter queue.

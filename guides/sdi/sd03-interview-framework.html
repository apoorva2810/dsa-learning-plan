A framework for system design interviews

A system design interview is a 45-minute conversation you are expected to steer. This chapter gives you a four-step framework (scope, high-level design with buy-in, deep dive, wrap-up), a time plan you can rescale to any slot, the habits that keep the interviewer with you, the red flags and the line that recovers from each, and a mock-interview timer and checklist to practise with.

After this chapter you can
  • Say what a system design interview measures and what it does not, and let that change how you behave in the first five minutes
  • Run the four steps against a 45-minute clock, name the checkpoint minute for each, and rescale the plan for a 30 or 60 minute slot
  • Ask scoping questions whose answers would change the design, and state an assumption when the answer is "you decide"
  • Build the high-level design in a draw, explain, check-in loop, then pick deep dives with the interviewer and run each in five beats
  • Close with a 60-second summary, and recognise the common red flags (jumping to a solution, over-engineering, silence) with the line that recovers from each

What the interview is really testing

A system design interview is a conversation about a problem with no single right answer: "design a ticket-sale website", "design a notification system". The interviewer is not waiting for a particular architecture. They are watching how you work on an open problem, because that is the job. Someone hands a team a vague goal, and the team has to turn it into a design everyone can build and defend.

What the notes at the end of the interview tend to cover:

SignalWhat the interviewer asks themselvesWhat it looks like in the room
Problem solvingCan this person cut an ambiguous request down to a design problem?Questions first, requirements written down, assumptions stated
Design skillDo they know the standard building blocks and when each earns its place?Every box has a reason tied to a requirement or a number
DepthCan they go one level below a box?Options compared, a pick, a number, a failure mode
Trade-offsDo they know what each choice costs?"This gives us X and costs us Y; I would switch if Z"
CollaborationWould I want to design with them?Checks in, takes a hint at once, changes course without drama
CommunicationCan I follow their thinking?Short sentences, signposting, a diagram that matches what is said
The first three signals are mostly knowledge. The last three are judgement and working with others, and they are easier to practise than candidates expect.

What it does not reward: reciting the architecture of a famous company, naming as many technologies as possible, a perfect final diagram, or a single correct number. A half-finished design with sound reasons beats a complete diagram with none.

The hld track has a seven-phase script with minutes in How to run a system design interview. This chapter is the coarser four-step version used throughout this track, plus the habits that make it work. The twelve design problems that follow in this track are practice material for it.

The question under every question

Am I watching a colleague I would trust to lead a design review? Every step in this chapter exists to leave visible evidence of that: questions asked, assumptions written down, options weighed, a clear summary.

The four steps and the clock

▶ Video lesson: The four steps 3:53 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

The framework has four steps, taken in order, each with a checkpoint at its end. The order is the point. Scope comes before boxes, so the design answers the right problem. A whole shallow design comes before any deep dive, so you never run out of time holding half a system. A summary closes the hour, so the interviewer's last impression is of structure.

Step through the flowchart. The dotted arrow matters as much as the solid ones: a deep dive often exposes a gap in the design, and going back to fix it is a good sign, not a failure.

The targets add up to 45. The deep dive gets the most time because that is where the rating is decided; the other steps get what they need to make it meaningful.

Fit the four steps to your slot

Type the slot you actually have. If it includes introductions and time for your own questions, take them off first: a 60-minute slot with 5 minutes of introductions and 10 for your questions leaves 45 minutes of design. The three checkpoint minutes (8, 20 and 40 for a clean 45) are the ones to watch on the wall clock.

Step 1 · Understand the problem and establish scope

Resist the urge to draw. A design for the wrong problem is worthless however elegant, and the first minutes are where the interviewer sees whether you can bound an open problem. Do three things:

  1. Ask questions whose answers could change the design. A good question has an answer that moves a box: the scale, how correct the data must be, how fast it must respond, who the users are.
  2. State an assumption when the answer is "you decide". Say it aloud and write it down so it can be corrected. That is a decision made visibly, not a gap.
  3. Write the result on the board: functional requirements (what the system does), non-functional requirements (how well it does it), and the one or two numbers that will drive the design. The arithmetic is in 2 · Back-of-the-envelope estimation.

The examples in this chapter use one prompt throughout: design a ticket-sale website for concerts. What makes it interesting is the moment a popular sale opens, when many thousands of people want the same few seats.

Ask aboutExample question for the ticket siteWhat the answer changes
FeaturesWhich flows matter: browsing events, choosing seats, paying, resale?What the API and the data model contain
Scale and spikesHow many people arrive in the first minute of a big sale?Queueing, caching, server count, whether a waiting room is needed

Six kinds of question. If you cannot say what a question's answer would change, do not ask it.

Opening the interview

Straight to boxes

"OK, so: a load balancer, a few web servers, a database, a cache for the seat map and a queue for the emails..."

Nothing in this answer depends on the problem. It would be the same for a blog, a bank or a chat app. The interviewer learns that you know component names, and nothing about whether you can tell which ones this system needs.

Scope first

"Before I draw anything, a few questions. Which flows matter: browsing, choosing seats, paying? When a big sale opens, how many people arrive in the first minute? Can a seat ever be sold twice? How fast must the seat map load? Is payment in scope?"

Then, without waiting to be told: "I'll assume 100,000 people in the first minute, no double sales ever, a seat map under a second, and payments handled by a provider. In scope: browse, hold seats for 10 minutes, pay, receive tickets. Out of scope: resale and recommendations." That takes one minute and gives you a spec.

Step 2 · Propose a high-level design and get buy-in

The goal of this step is a design the interviewer agrees with, not one that impresses. Work in a loop: draw one piece, say what it does and why it is there, check in. Start with the API (a few lines: method, path, key parameters), then add the boxes between the client and the data one at a time. Every box should trace back to a requirement or a number from step 1. If you cannot say why a box is there, leave it out.

Finish by tracing one write and one read through the finished picture, out loud. It is the cheapest test of a design: gaps show up as hesitations.

When a box tempts you into detail ("how would the seat hold expire?"), write it in a corner of the board as a parking-lot item and carry on. Those items are your candidates for step 3.

flowchart LR

The loop repeats for every piece. The check-in costs ten seconds and prevents ten minutes of building on a misunderstanding.

A buy-in loop in practice. The interviewer's question becomes the deep-dive topic because you parked it instead of chasing it.

Step 3 · Design deep dive

You now have a whole, shallow design. Step 3 is where the rating is earned: pick a few components and go one level below the box. Pick with the interviewer. Offer two or three candidates, say which you would start with and why, and let them redirect:

"The parts I would dig into are the seat hold and the sale-opening spike. The hold is where correctness is decided, so I would start there. Is there something else you would rather see?"

A deep dive has five beats, and a dive that skips one sounds shallow:

  1. Options. Name two or three ways to do it.
  2. Pick. Choose one and tie the reason to a requirement from step 1.
  3. Number. Put a rough figure on it: requests per second, a TTL, a size.
  4. Failure. Say how it breaks and what happens then.
  5. Trigger. Say what would make you choose differently.
flowchart TD
  Cand["Candidates: parking-lot items and boxes"] --> Hint{"Did the interviewer point at one?"}
  Hint -->|yes| Take["Take it first"]
  Hint -->|no| Break{"Which breaks first at scale or under failure?"}
  Break --> Unique{"Is it what makes this problem different?"}
  Unique -->|yes| Dive["Dive: options, pick, number, failure, trigger"]
  Unique -->|no| Skip["Mention it in one line and move on"]

The same cache, shallow and deep

Shallow

"We will put a cache in front of the database."

It names a component and stops. It does not say what is cached, for how long, or what happens when the cache is cold or gone. The interviewer has to ask all of that, and every question spends your time.

Deep

"The seat map is read by everyone and changes only when a seat is held or sold, so I would cache it per event with a 2 second TTL. Stale for 2 seconds is acceptable because the hold step re-checks the seat in the database inside a transaction. The TTL caps the database at one rebuild per event every 2 seconds however many people are watching. If the cache node dies, the first rebuild after it is the risk, so rebuilds take a lock to stop a stampede. If seats had to be live to the second I would shorten the TTL or push changes to viewers instead."

Step 4 · Wrap up

The last five minutes decide what the interviewer remembers. If they have not already moved on to their own questions, summarise, name the weak spots and say what comes next. Do not add new boxes now; spend the time on judgement.

  1. Recap in about 60 seconds. The requirements, the shape of the design, and the two decisions that matter most.
  2. Name the bottlenecks and failure modes you accepted. Interviewers trust a candidate who points out the weak spot before they do.
  3. Say how you would run it: the metrics and alerts, the rollout, how you would roll back.
  4. Say what you would do with more time, or what changes at ten times the load.

Follow-up questions to expect, and the shape of a good answer

What breaks first if traffic grows ten times?

Use your own numbers from step 1 to name the first resource to saturate, then the remedy. For the ticket site it is the hold write path, because every attempt writes a row. Partition holds by event, and put the sale-opening spike behind a waiting room.

How would you know it is broken?

Watch the four golden signals: request rate, error rate, latency percentiles and saturation, plus one business metric (holds that succeed per minute). One alert per user-visible symptom, dashboards per component. See Monitoring Distributed Systems.

What happens when a data centre goes down?

Say which components are replicated across zones, how long failover takes, and what data could be lost (replication lag). Then say what degrades gracefully, for example browsing continues read-only while holds pause.

How would you roll this out safely?

Behind a feature flag, to 1% of traffic first, with automatic rollback on error rate or latency. For a schema change, write to both versions before reading from the new one.

What would you cut for a first version?

Keep the invariant that matters (no seat sold twice) and cut everything else you can name: the waiting room, recommendations, resale. Showing which requirement is load-bearing is the answer.

Where is the single point of failure?

Walk the diagram and find any box with one instance, such as the payment callback handler or the job that expires holds. Say how you would remove it or contain it.

A 60-second wrap-up

'To summarise: a ticket-sale site for flash sales. Users browse events, hold seats for ten minutes and pay through a provider. Two decisions matter. A seat hold is a database row with an expiry, written inside a transaction so no seat is sold twice. And the seat map is cached with a two second TTL, so a hundred thousand viewers cost the database one read per event every two seconds. At ten times the load the hold write path saturates first, so I would partition holds by event. I would watch hold success rate and p99 hold latency, and with more time I would design the waiting room that smooths the opening spike.'

Pacing, communication and red flags

▶ Video lesson: Pacing and communication 4:11 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Two skills run through all four steps: watching the clock and making your thinking audible.

For the clock, use the three checkpoints from the plan: scope agreed by minute 8, the high-level design complete by minute 20, the deep dives finished by minute 40. Glance at the clock as you cross each one. Being behind is not the problem; not noticing is. If you are behind, say so, and trade the second deep dive for time to finish. Never trade the wrap-up.

For communication, the interviewer cannot see inside your head. Think aloud in short sentences, signpost ("I will cover the read path next, then storage"), and ask for a steer only at real forks. The simulation shows the same 45 minutes three ways.

Dos and don'ts

Don't

  • Start drawing before you know the scope
  • Add a component because it is popular, with no requirement behind it
  • Think in silence for minutes, or stay stuck without asking for a hint
  • Defend a choice after the interviewer has shown a flaw
  • Dive into one box while the rest of the design is blank
  • Say "it will scale" without a number
  • Ignore a hint
  • Present one option as if no other existed
  • Run out of time with no summary

Do

  • Ask, then state assumptions and write the requirements down
  • Tie every box to a requirement or a number
  • Think aloud in short sentences, and ask for a hint when stuck
  • Treat pushback as information: restate it, adjust, say what changed
  • Finish a shallow end-to-end design first, then pick the dives
  • Put a rough figure on every claim
  • Take a hint at once and say you are taking it
  • Offer two or three options and say which you prefer, and why
  • Check the clock at minutes 8, 20 and 40, and keep time for a summary
Red flagHow it shows upWhat to say to recover
Jumping to a solutionBoxes on the board in the first minute, no questions"Let me step back and pin down the scope first."
Over-engineeringQueues, sharding and multi-region for 10,000 users"At this scale one primary with a replica is enough. I would add X when Y happens."
SilenceMinutes of thinking with nothing said"I am weighing A against B. Let me talk through both."
Narrow focus30 minutes on one component, the rest of the board empty"Let me zoom out and finish the main flow, then come back to this."
DefensivenessArguing with a hint instead of using it"Good point, that changes the hold design. Let me adjust."

Every red flag is recoverable if you notice it. The recovery line is short on purpose: say it, then act on it.

Reference solution (open after your attempt)

A model run on the ticket-sale prompt, showing what is on the board at each checkpoint. The numbers are illustrative.

Minute 8, scope. Functional: browse events, view a seat map, hold seats for 10 minutes, pay through a provider, receive tickets. Out of scope: resale and recommendations. Non-functional: 100,000 people in the first minute of a big sale, no seat ever sold twice, seat map under a second, available during sales. Numbers: 100,000 users refreshing every 5 seconds is 20,000 seat-map reads per second; hold attempts are a small fraction, roughly 1,000 per second.

Minute 20, high-level design. API: GET /events/:id/seats, POST /holds, POST /orders. Boxes: clients, a load balancer, API servers, a SQL database for holds and orders, a cache for the seat map, a payment provider called from the order service, and a queue for ticket emails. A hold traced: insert a hold row inside a transaction and return its expiry. A purchase traced: check the hold, call the provider, mark the seats sold. Parking lot: hold expiry, the sale-opening spike, payment retries.

Minutes 20 to 40, two dives. (1) Holds. Options: a row lock, an optimistic version column, or a unique constraint on active holds with an expiry job. Pick the unique constraint, because the database enforces it under any concurrency. Failure: an expired hold that is never cleaned up blocks its seat, so a periodic job deletes expired holds and a hold request deletes an expired row for the same seat inside its own transaction. (2) The opening spike. Options: scale out, a waiting room, or per-user rate limiting. Pick a waiting room that admits users at the rate holds can be written, because scaling out cannot fix a contended row. Number: about 1,000 admissions per second.

Minutes 40 to 45, wrap-up. A 60-second summary, hold success rate and p99 hold latency as the metrics, the hold write path as the first thing to saturate at ten times the load, and resale and notifications as next steps.

A practice plan that works

Doing problems is not the same as getting better at them. This chapter turns the 150 into a schedule: how to use the Attempted, Solved and Needs review statuses as a spaced-repetition queue, how many problems your hours really buy, a time-boxed study session with a 15-minute rule for being stuck, 4-, 8- and 12-week plans, a mock-interview protocol, and checklists for the last week and the last day.

After this chapter you can
  • Turn the weeks and hours you have into a realistic count of new problems and reviews
  • Use the Attempted, Solved and Needs review statuses as a spaced-repetition queue, with a rule for each
  • Run a study session with time boxes, and use the 15-minute rule and the hint ladder when you are stuck
  • Follow a 4-, 8- or 12-week plan and know what to cut when time runs short
  • Run a 45-minute mock interview with a timer, a rubric and a redo
  • Prepare for the last week and the last day, and recognise a plateau and change the method, not the volume

Practice that sticks

▶ Video lesson: Practice that sticks 3:56 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Solving a problem once feels like learning, and mostly it is not. Two things turn exposure into skill, and learning research rates both highly: retrieval (producing the answer from a blank page instead of rereading it) and spacing (coming back after days, not minutes). Rereading and highlighting rate poorly. For this site that means: reading a solution is the weakest thing you can do here, and redoing a problem you failed three days ago is one of the strongest.

The site already gives you the pieces. Every problem page has a Status menu: Not started, Attempted, Solved, Needs review. The Progress page counts them per topic and lists everything marked Needs review, plus your recently opened problems. All of it is stored in this browser only, so use Export on the Progress page now and then. The site does not schedule reviews for you; this chapter supplies the rules.

  state "Needs review" as NeedsReview

  NotStarted --> Attempted: tried, no working solution yet

  Solved --> NeedsReview: a spot-check failed

The three statuses as a state machine. Solved is the only status you have to earn; everything else puts the problem back in a queue.
What happenedSet the status toNext
No working solution when the time box endedAttempteduse the stuck ladder; try again tomorrow
A working solution, but only after a hint, the key idea or the solutionNeeds reviewredo from a blank page in 2 to 3 days

Be strict with 'Solved'. An honest queue is the whole point; a flattering one hides exactly the problems you need.

How many problems your hours buy

Start with the budget. Solving each of the 150 once, at about 40 minutes with notes, is about 100 hours, and the reviews that make it stick come on top. The calculator turns weeks and hours into new problems and reviews. Change the numbers to match your life, not your hopes.

Weeks, hours, problems and revision

'Share of time for reviews and mocks' is a fraction between 0 and 1: 0.3 means three hours in ten go to redoing old problems and mock interviews. The defaults describe eight weeks at ten hours a week.

If the coverage is under 100%, do not speed up. Cut. Drop new problems before you drop reviews, skip Hard problems except a few classics, and leave the last topics (Math & Geometry, Bit Manipulation) for last. In most cases seventy problems done twice are worth more than 150 done once.

One study session

A session is one topic, a timer, and a fixed shape. Staying in one topic per session lets the pattern repeat until it sticks; mixing topics comes later, in mocks, where recognising the pattern is the point.

flowchart TD
  PICK(["Pick the next problem<br/>in today's topic"]) --> READ["Read it, say the signals aloud,<br/>write the brute force (5 min)"]
  READ --> TRY["Attempt alone, timer on<br/>20 min Easy, 40 min Medium"]
  TRY --> OK{"Working solution<br/>in time?"}
  OK -- yes --> CLEAN["Test an edge case,<br/>state the complexity"]
  CLEAN --> SOLVED["Open Key idea and Approaches and compare<br/>Status: Solved, or Needs review"]
  OK -- no --> STUCK["Use the stuck ladder:<br/>hints, key idea, an approach"]
  STUCK --> REDO["Close everything.<br/>Re-implement from a blank page"]
  REDO --> REVIEW["Status: Needs review<br/>redo in 2 to 3 days"]
  SOLVED --> NOTE["One line: the signal and the template"]
  REVIEW --> NOTE
The session loop. The branch at the time box decides the status, and every path ends with the one-line note.

Stuck? The 15-minute rule

▶ Video lesson: Stuck? Climb the ladder 3:44 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

'Stuck' does not mean 'the clock ran out'. It means 15 minutes with no new idea. Past that point more staring rarely helps, but jumping straight to the solution wastes the problem. Climb a ladder instead: the first rungs cost nothing, and each later rung uses up a little more of what you would have learned by solving it yourself. The rungs match the page layout: Think first holds the hints, then Key idea, then Approaches.

flowchart TD
  S(["15 minutes,<br/>no new idea"]) --> L1["Re-read the statement and limits.<br/>Trace the example by hand"]
  L1 --> L2["Write the brute force<br/>and name its bottleneck"]
  L2 --> L3["Ask the pattern map:<br/>which signal did I miss?"]
  L3 --> L4["Open the first hint<br/>under Think first"]
  L4 --> L5["Open the remaining hints,<br/>then the Key idea"]
  L5 --> L6["Read one approach for 10 minutes,<br/>then close it"]
  L6 --> L7["Re-implement from a blank page.<br/>Mark Needs review"]
The ladder, cheapest rung first. Most problems are unlocked by rung 2 or 3 if you were honest about the bottleneck.

In a real interview the ladder is spoken, not silent. Say: 'I'm going to write the brute force first so we have a baseline. It is O(n²) because of this inner scan. I think a hash map could replace the scan, but let me check that with an example.' Interviewers rarely penalise being stuck; they penalise silence. If you are truly stuck, say what you tried and ask a narrow question, such as whether the input can contain duplicates.

Spaced repetition on the 150

Spacing means revisiting a problem at growing intervals: soon enough that you remember the shape, late enough that recalling it takes effort. A simple ladder for interview prep is days 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 after the first solve. You do not need a calendar app: the Needs review list on the Progress page is the queue, and a weekly review block works through it, oldest first.

flowchart LR
  D0["Day 0<br/>First solve"] --> D1["Day 1<br/>Redo if you needed help"]
  D1 --> D3["Day 3<br/>Redo from a blank page"]
  D3 --> D7["Day 7<br/>Redo with the timer"]
  D7 --> D14["Day 14<br/>Explain it aloud"]
  D14 --> D30["Day 30<br/>Spot-check"]
  D3 -->|"failed"| D1
  D7 -->|"failed"| D1
  D14 -->|"failed"| D1
A pass moves a problem one step right; a failure sends it back to day 1 and marks it Needs review. That is the system working.

Questions about reviewing

What counts as a redo?

Opening the problem page, closing the hints and the approaches, and writing a passing solution in the Run it yourself box from nothing. Copying your old solution from memory of its text is not a redo; deriving it again is.

Do I redo Easy problems?

Only the ones you marked Needs review. Easy problems are mostly there to teach a template; once the template is automatic, spend the time on Mediums.

How many reviews per week?

Let the calculator decide: about 30% of your hours. A typical week is one block of redos from the Needs review list (oldest first), one block of spot-checks from topics you finished a week or a month ago, and one mock.

The site has no due dates. How do I track the ladder?

Do not track dates per problem; it costs more than it saves. Review by topic and week: each week, redo the problems in the Needs review list, and re-solve two or three Solved problems from the topic you finished a week ago and the one from a month ago. The matrix in the next section records the passes per topic.

Does reading the Approaches count as review?

No. Recognising a solution when you read it feels like understanding, but the interview asks you to produce it. Use the approaches to compare with your own solution, not to restudy.

Weekly plans: 4, 8 and 12 weeks

All three plans use the roadmap order, which respects topic prerequisites. Pick the one that matches the calculator, not your ambition. In every plan the review blocks are fixed: if you fall behind, cut new problems from the end of the plan.

Mock interviews

A mock is the only practice that tests the things solving alone does not: talking while thinking, the clock, and starting from a problem whose pattern you were not told. Start mocks about a third of the way into a plan (week 3 of 12, week 3 of 8, week 2 of 4) and run one a week, with a friend if you can and alone with a recorder if you cannot.

flowchart LR

  B --> C["10-15 min<br/>Signal, pattern, template"]
  C --> D["15-35 min<br/>Code"]
  D --> E["35-42 min<br/>Test and fix"]
  E --> F["42-45 min<br/>Complexity, follow-ups"]
A 45-minute shape. The deadline that matters is minute 15: if you have not named the pattern by then, climb the ladder aloud.

When progress stalls

Plateaus are common. For many people the first 30 to 40 problems feel like fast progress because each new pattern covers many problems. Then problems start to mix patterns, a solution takes longer to find, and the count of 'solved' stalls while the effort goes up. That feels like failing. It is a common shape of the curve, and it is not a verdict on you.

What helps, and what does not:

  • Measure the right thing. The most useful number is problems solved unaided and on time, on the first try. It is the closest thing to what the interview measures. Problems completed after reading the solution do not move it.
  • Diagnose the failures. For every Needs review problem write one word: signal (did not see the pattern), template (saw it, could not write it), bug (off by one, missed edge case), speed (correct but too slow) or nerves (froze). Fix the most common cause. Signals: classify statements without solving them. Bugs: write the edge-case tests before the code. Speed: time-box and practise the template.
  • Do fewer new problems and redo more old ones. A failed problem redone and solved teaches more than a fresh problem you are also likely to fail.
  • Stop using solutions as the main activity. If your week is mostly reading approaches, you are practising recognition, not production.
  • Rest. One day off a week and enough sleep are part of the plan; burning out at week six loses more than a rest day does.

One honest limit: practice raises your odds, it does not make the outcome certain. Interviews also test communication and judgement, and a bad day happens. If, after four weeks of the plan, your first-try rate has not moved at all, change the method (talk aloud, mock with a partner, diagnose failures) before you add more volume.

A strong answer to 'how did you prepare?' is specific: 'I worked through the 150 by pattern over ten weeks, redoing every problem I did not solve unaided after 3, 7 and 14 days. I tracked first-try solve rate and diagnosed each failure as signal, template, bug or speed, and I ran a weekly mock with a friend.' It shows a method, evidence and feedback, which is what you want to be hired for.

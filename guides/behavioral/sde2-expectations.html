What SDE-2 interviewers probe

SDE-2 interviewers are not asking whether you can finish a task. They are asking whether you can own a problem end to end, work across team lines, make and defend trade-offs, and raise the people around you. This chapter lists the seven signals, what good and not-yet look like for each, the questions and follow-up probes you will meet, how an interviewer drills down, and a drill of twenty questions to practise against.

After this chapter you can
  • Name the seven signals SDE-2 interviewers score, and describe 'good' versus 'not yet' for each
  • Predict the follow-up ladder (role, reasoning, alternatives, measurement, hindsight) and keep a one-line reply ready for each rung
  • Calibrate stories to SDE-2 scope using the SDE-1, SDE-2 and SDE-3 behaviour table
  • Recognise which signal a question targets even when its wording hides it
  • Practise twenty SDE-2 questions with follow-ups under a timer

What an SDE-2 loop is scoring

▶ Video lesson: What an SDE-2 loop is scoring 3:52 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Titles differ by company (Amazon's SDE II, a mid-level engineer elsewhere), so read 'SDE-2' as a band of behaviour rather than a job title. Interviewers at this band are not asking whether you can finish a task; they assume you can. They are asking whether you can own a problem whose edges you had to find, work across team lines, make and defend technical trade-offs, and make the people around you better.

Seven signals come up again and again. Each gets its own section below, with what 'good' looks like, what 'not yet' looks like, the questions that target it and the follow-ups that test it.

flowchart LR
  SD["SDE-2 loop"] --> RUN["Run it"]
  SD --> PEO["Work with people"]
  SD --> JUD["Judge it"]
  RUN --> OWN["Ownership"]
  RUN --> DEL["Delivering under constraints"]
  PEO --> COL["Cross-team collaboration"]
  PEO --> CON["Conflict resolution"]
  PEO --> MEN["Mentoring juniors"]
  JUD --> TRA["Technical trade-offs"]
  JUD --> AMB["Dealing with ambiguity"]
Three questions sit behind the seven signals: do you run it, do you work well with people, do you judge well.

A pattern, not an official rubric: bands differ by company. Read across a row to see how one behaviour grows.

Calibrate to the level. A flawless SDE-1 story told at an SDE-2 interview earns 'strong execution, level unclear', and that is the most common way an otherwise strong candidate lands below the bar. The interviewer is not asking whether you did the work well; they are asking whether the work was the right size. Check each story against the 'Story shape' row.

How interviewers drill

▶ Video lesson: How interviewers drill 4:12 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Probing is the interview. Your opening answer fixes the topic; the follow-ups produce the evidence. Most trained interviewers carry a ladder in their head and climb it until they have enough or find the floor: your role, your reasoning, the alternatives, the measurement, the hindsight. Knowing the rungs lets you put the answer to each one inside the story before it is asked.

flowchart TD
  Q["Opening question"] --> A["Your story<br/>3 to 4 min"]
  A --> P1["Your role?"]
  P1 --> P2["Why that way?"]
  P2 --> P3["What else did<br/>you consider?"]
  P3 --> P4["How did you<br/>measure it?"]
  P4 --> P5["What would you<br/>change?"]
  P5 --> D{{"Enough evidence?"}}
  D -- yes --> N["Next question"]
  D -- no --> P1
A thin rung sends the interviewer back round the loop on a different decision from the same story.

Same project, two kinds of answer

Two candidates tell the same project. The first has a polished three-minute story and 'we' answers to every probe; the second has the same story with decisions in it. Watch the Signals lane: it shows what the interviewer can actually write down after each probe.

Treat a probe as an invitation, not an attack: the interviewer is handing you a chance to supply evidence they could not get from the story alone. A good reply to any probe is to name the specific thing, give the reason, and stop. It is fine to take five seconds to think; a pause reads as care, and a rushed vague answer reads as a thin story.

Ownership: end to end, and after launch

What the interviewer listens for

  • You noticed the problem, or its owner-less state, before being told
  • You carried it past 'merged' to 'working in production': rollout, alarms, on-call, follow-up
  • You fixed causes as well as symptoms, and checked that the fix held
  • You took responsibility at the edge of your remit without waiting for permission

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time you took ownership of something that was not your job.
  2. Describe a service or feature you owned from design to production. What happened after launch?
  3. Tell me about a time you found a problem nobody was working on.

Ownership: good versus not yet

Not yet

"The alert pointed at a bug. I fixed it, closed the ticket and went back to my sprint work."

Competent, and entirely reactive. Ownership ended where the assignment ended, and nothing says the problem will not return.

Good

"I fixed the bug, then asked why it reached production. We had no test on that path and no alarm on the error. I added both, wrote the runbook, and checked a month later that it had not recurred."

The scope grew from the ticket to the system, and the follow-through is checkable.

Follow-up probes for ownership

Whose job was it, and why did it fall to you?

What it tests: initiative versus assignment.

A strong answer names the gap (no owner, or unclear ownership), says how you confirmed that nobody else was on it, and how you told the people who might have been.

What happened after you shipped?

What it tests: operational follow-through.

A strong answer names the metric or alarm you watched, a real incident or near-miss afterwards, and what changed because of it.

What did you stop doing to make time for it?

What it tests: prioritisation and honesty about cost.

A strong answer names the trade-off in time, who you told, and why the problem was worth more than what you dropped.

Working across teams: collaboration and conflict

▶ Video lesson: Working across teams: collaboration and conflict 3:57 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Collaboration: what the interviewer listens for

  • You identified who else was affected and went to them early
  • You understood the other team's constraints and incentives, not only your own
  • You made it easy for them to say yes: a written proposal, a prototype, a smaller first step
  • The relationship and the outcome were shared, not won

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time you depended on another team to deliver something.
  2. Describe a project where you had to align several teams with different priorities.
  3. Tell me about a time you needed buy-in from people who did not report to you.

Conflict: what the interviewer listens for

  • You disagreed on the substance, with data or a clear argument, not about the person
  • You sought to understand the other view before pushing yours
  • The decision was separated from the relationship, and the relationship survived
  • When the decision went against you, you committed and helped it succeed

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or tech lead.
  2. Describe a conflict with a peer. How did you resolve it?
  3. Tell me about a time a decision was made that you thought was wrong.

Collaboration: good versus not yet

Not yet

"The other team was a blocker. I escalated to my manager and they prioritised our request."

The problem was solved by rank, not by understanding. The other team learned that asking you means a fight.

Good

"Their roadmap was full. I asked about their constraints and found the change was two days of work that threatened one launch date, so I offered to write it and have them review it. It shipped in a week and we agreed the interface in a one-page doc."

The candidate learned the other team's constraint, lowered the cost of saying yes, and left a written agreement behind.

Conflict: good versus not yet

Not yet

"My teammate was stubborn and would not listen, so I went along with it to keep the peace."

Avoiding the disagreement is not resolving it, and 'stubborn' is a judgement of the person. Nothing here shows a position, evidence or a commitment.

Good

"We disagreed on dual-write versus cut-over. I asked what he was worried about: rollback time. I rehearsed both on staging and a cut-over rollback took four minutes, inside his limit. He agreed, and I wrote the rollback runbook with him."

The candidate found the real concern, settled it with evidence, and made the other person a co-owner of the result.

flowchart TD
  D["Disagreement surfaces"] --> U["Understand their goal"]
  U --> F["Agree the facts"]
  F --> O["Offer options with data"]
  O --> V{{"Resolved?"}}
  V -- yes --> C["Commit and record"]
  V -- no --> E["Escalate with both views"]
  E --> K["Commit to the decision"]
  C --> R["Review the outcome"]
  K --> R
The escalation branch is part of the pattern, not a failure of it: the goal is a decision and a shared commitment.

Follow-up probes for collaboration and conflict

What did the other team care about?

What it tests: empathy and understanding of incentives.

A strong answer states their goals and constraints specifically ('a launch on the 14th', 'an on-call rota already at limit'), not 'they were busy'.

Why would they say yes?

What it tests: how you design influence.

A strong answer names the specific make-it-easy move: a draft change, a prototype, an offer to share the work, or a benefit to them that you could measure.

What did you do when they said no?

What it tests: resilience and judgement about escalation.

A strong answer shows you tried alternatives first and escalated with options and a joint summary, not with blame.

What was their strongest argument?

What it tests: fairness and listening.

A strong answer states the other side's best point accurately, even if you still disagreed, and says what it changed in your proposal.

What did you say, exactly?

What it tests: candour and tone.

A strong answer gives the substance of what you said, including the data you brought, and shows respect in the framing.

What if they had still said no?

What it tests: disagree and commit.

A strong answer describes what you did in a past case where you lost: committed fully, helped the decision succeed, and looked at the outcome without gloating.

Technical judgement: trade-offs and ambiguity

Trade-offs: what the interviewer listens for

  • The options were real, and you can name at least two
  • The criteria were explicit: latency, cost, time to build, operational burden, reversibility
  • You chose, and you can say what you gave up
  • You noted how you would know the choice was wrong and what would make you revisit it

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about the hardest technical decision you made.
  2. Describe a time you chose between a quick fix and a proper solution.
  3. Tell me about a design you later regretted.

Ambiguity: what the interviewer listens for

  • You asked questions and pinned down the goal before building
  • You made assumptions explicit and checked the riskiest one first
  • You took a small, reversible step instead of waiting for certainty
  • You kept stakeholders informed as the picture changed

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time the requirements were unclear.
  2. Describe a project where you had to make progress without all the information.
  3. Tell me about a time the goal changed halfway through.

Trade-offs: good versus not yet

Not yet

"I used a microservice because it is more scalable."

A slogan in place of a decision. There are no criteria, no alternative and no cost, so the interviewer cannot tell whether it was judgement or fashion.

Good

"Export traffic was five requests a minute, so scale was not the issue; deploy independence was, because export changes blocked the main app's releases twice a month. I chose a separate service and accepted a network hop and one more thing to monitor, and wrote down that if export changes dropped below one a quarter we would fold it back."

The criteria came from the actual numbers, the cost is named, and the reversal condition is written down.

Ambiguity: good versus not yet

Not yet

"The requirements were vague, so I waited for product to finalise them and then built exactly what the spec said."

Waiting is safe and passive. It hands the risk to someone else, and the interviewer learns nothing about how you create clarity.

Good

"The ask was 'make search better'. I wrote down three guesses at what it meant, checked the logs and found 30% of queries returned nothing. I proposed starting with those, got a yes from the PM in fifteen minutes, shipped synonyms for the top 200 queries in a week, and set the next checkpoint."

The candidate reduced the ambiguity with data and a small first step, and kept the stakeholder in the loop.

flowchart LR
  V["Vague<br/>ask"] --> G["Clarify<br/>the goal"]
  G --> U["List the<br/>unknowns"]
  U --> A["State<br/>assumptions"]
  A --> S["Smallest<br/>reversible step"]
  S --> C["Checkpoint with<br/>stakeholders"]
  C -.-> G
Ambiguity shrinks through cycles of small steps and checkpoints, not by waiting for a better spec.

Follow-up probes for trade-offs and ambiguity

What else did you consider?

What it tests: breadth.

A strong answer names two real alternatives, not strawmen, and says why each lost.

How did you decide?

What it tests: criteria.

A strong answer gives explicit criteria, ranks them for this situation, and uses numbers where it can.

What did you give up, and how would you know it was wrong?

What it tests: honesty and reversibility.

A strong answer names a real cost and a trigger for revisiting the decision, ideally one you wrote down at the time.

How did you know what to do first?

What it tests: prioritisation under uncertainty.

A strong answer names the riskiest assumption or the strongest data signal and says why it came first.

What did you assume, and what if it was wrong?

What it tests: whether assumptions were explicit.

A strong answer lists the assumptions, says how a miss would show up, and shows the cost of being wrong was kept small.

How did you keep people aligned?

What it tests: communication while things were changing.

A strong answer names the artefact or rhythm (a one-pager, a weekly checkpoint) and what it prevented.

Growing people and delivering under constraints

Mentoring: what the interviewer listens for

  • You helped on purpose, with a plan, not only when asked
  • You adapted to the person: their level, style and goals
  • Your feedback was specific and kind, in code review and in person
  • The other person is measurably better or more independent, and you can say how

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time you helped a junior colleague grow.
  2. Describe how you onboard a new engineer.
  3. Tell me about a time you gave someone difficult feedback.

Delivering under constraints: what the interviewer listens for

  • You cut scope deliberately and told people what you cut and why
  • You protected what must not break: correctness, security, customer trust
  • You raised risk early, not at the deadline
  • You hit the date, or renegotiated it with evidence

Sample questions

  1. Tell me about a time you had to deliver on a very tight deadline.
  2. Describe a time you had to cut scope. How did you decide?
  3. Tell me about a time you had too much to do and not enough time.
flowchart LR
  A["Explain<br/>the goal"] --> B["Show how<br/>pair or demo"]
  B --> C["They drive<br/>you watch"]
  C --> D["They own it<br/>you review"]
  D --> E["They decide<br/>you advise"]
  E --> F["They teach<br/>others"]
Match the rung to the person and the task, and move up when they show they are ready, not on a calendar.

Mentoring: good versus not yet

Not yet

"I am always happy to answer questions. When our new hire got stuck I would just fix it myself to save time."

Helpful in the moment and no growth for the other person. The interviewer hears availability, not mentoring.

Good

"Our new hire kept bringing me the same class of bug. I asked what he wanted to do alone in six months, and we agreed a plan: he would lead the next small feature, I would review the design rather than the code, and we would review the result together. By the third feature he was shipping without my design review."

A diagnosis, a plan fitted to the person, a step-down in support, and an observable change.

Delivering under constraints: good versus not yet

Not yet

"I worked nights and weekends and we made the date."

Effort is not a strategy. It hides what was cut, what was risked, and whether the team can repeat it.

Good

"Five weeks of work had three weeks to land. I split the list into must, should and could, got the PM to agree in writing to drop two 'could' items, flagged the risk in week one, and delivered the must items on the date, with a plan for the rest."

Deliberate scope cuts, an explicit agreement, early risk communication and a date met without heroics.

Follow-up probes for mentoring and delivery

How did you decide what help to give?

What it tests: diagnosis of the person, not only the problem.

A strong answer describes their level and goal and why you chose that amount of support.

What could they do afterwards that they could not before?

What it tests: evidence of impact.

A strong answer names an observable change: they led a review, owned a component, or onboarded someone else.

What was the hardest feedback to give?

What it tests: candour and care.

A strong answer is specific about what you said, how they reacted, and what you would phrase differently now.

How did you decide what to cut?

What it tests: prioritisation criteria.

A strong answer states the criteria (customer impact, risk, reversibility) and who agreed.

When did you first tell people the date was at risk?

What it tests: early risk communication.

A strong answer gives a specific day early in the timeline, who you told, and what you offered as options.

What did the shortcut cost later?

What it tests: honesty about technical or process debt.

A strong answer names the debt you took knowingly, the trigger to repay it, and whether and when it was repaid.

Drill: twenty SDE-2 questions

Draw a question at random, set the timer, and answer aloud as if to an interviewer. Then reveal the follow-ups and answer two of them. Notice which rung of the ladder you reach for first and which one you skip.

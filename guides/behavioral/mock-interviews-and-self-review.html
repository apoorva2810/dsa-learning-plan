Mock interviews & self-review

Mock interviews turn knowledge into performance: they train you to think aloud, work to a clock and stay composed. This chapter gives you a two-week schedule that mixes DSA, LLD, HLD and behavioural rounds, a protocol for running a mock with a friend or alone, a rubric for each round type, a self-review checklist and debrief template, a simulated loop day with pacing and energy, and the checklists for the day before and the morning of.

After this chapter you can
  • Plan a two-week block that mixes DSA, LLD, HLD and behavioural mocks, and work out how many mocks of each type your hours allow
  • Run a mock with a partner or alone: set-up, rules, time-boxing, recording, and a debrief where you score yourself first
  • Score each round type against what interviewers commonly look for, using a 1 to 4 rubric
  • Turn a mock into one specific fix per gap, and re-test it within three days
  • Pace a loop day: use the gaps for rest and a reset routine, and split the time inside each round
  • Follow the day-before and morning-of checklists, and avoid the common last-week mistakes

Why mocks beat more reading

There is a gap between recognising a solution and producing it while talking, with a clock running and someone watching. Reading, watching and solving silently close the first gap. Only a mock closes the second. A mock trains the skills the interview actually uses: thinking aloud, working to a time limit, recovering from a stuck moment, taking a hint without losing your thread, and staying composed for an hour.

The practice is the loop, not any single mock. Each lap should change one thing.

Interviewers score the process you show, not only the answer you reach. In every round type that means: clarify before you build, think aloud, test before you declare done, and treat a hint as a collaboration rather than a failure. A mock is the only practice that exercises all of these at once.

Two weeks, four round types

The schedule below assumes about 15 hours a week: roughly 1.5 hours on a weekday and a longer block at the weekend. It covers all four round types: DSA is the most frequent, LLD and HLD each get dedicated mocks, and behavioural gets short daily drills plus a few full mocks with follow-ups. It assumes day 1 is a Monday and the interview is on day 15; shift it to fit your calendar, but keep the rest day, the loop simulation two days out and the light final day. For the long-term view of spacing and topics, see A practice plan that works.

How many mocks fit in your calendar?

The default split is 35% DSA, 25% LLD, 25% HLD and 15% behavioural; behavioural gets the smallest share of full mocks because it also has daily drills. Change it to match the rounds in your loop. If the 'split adds up to' line is not 100, the per-type numbers will not sum to the total.

Running a mock, with a friend or alone

▶ Video lesson: A mock that teaches you something 3:55 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A mock has the same eight steps whether you have a partner or not. The two rules that make it realistic are do not pause and stop at the bell. The rule that makes it useful is score yourself before you hear anyone else.

flowchart TD
  Setup["1. Set up<br/>question, timer, recorder"] --> Brief["2. Brief the interviewer<br/>rules and rubric"]
  Brief --> Run["3. Run it to time<br/>no pausing"]
  Run --> Stop["4. Stop at the bell<br/>even mid-sentence"]
  Stop --> Self["5. Self-score first<br/>before any feedback"]
  Self --> Fb["6. Partner feedback<br/>against the rubric"]
  Fb --> Log["7. Log gaps and fixes<br/>debrief template"]
  Log --> Sched["8. Schedule the re-test<br/>within 3 days"]
Steps 1 to 4 are the performance, steps 5 to 8 are the learning. Skipping 5 to 8 is the most common way to waste a mock.

Three ways to run it

Choose roles and swap next time: being the interviewer is excellent practice because you learn what a good answer looks like from the other side.

  • The interviewer uses a question sheet and the rubric from the next section, and reads the prompt as written.
  • Hint rule: the first hint comes only after about five minutes of being stuck. Count every hint; each one lowers the score on that dimension.
  • The interviewer does not fill silences. If you go quiet for more than about 20 seconds, that is itself a note for the review.
  • 45 to 60 minutes of mock, then 15 minutes of feedback. Do not discuss the solution until both of you have scored.

Solo mocks are less realistic but available at any hour. Make up for the missing interviewer with discipline.

  • Record screen and voice. Talk out loud the whole time, as if someone were listening.
  • Use a problem you have not seen and a visible timer. No hints: if you are stuck for five minutes, say aloud what you would ask the interviewer.
  • Stop at the bell. Review the recording at 1.25x with the rubric open.
  • Timestamp three moments: your best moment, your worst moment and your longest silence. Those are the review.

Record yourself, then send the recording to a friend or mentor with three specific questions, such as 'where did I lose you?', 'when did I go silent?' and 'did my complexity analysis convince you?'.

Specific questions get specific answers; 'how did I do?' gets 'fine'. Ask for a reply within 24 hours so the mock is still fresh, and log the answers in the same debrief template.

The debrief is a short, structured conversation. Step through a typical one: the candidate speaks first, the partner brings timestamps, and the end product is a written gap and a written fix, not a vague feeling.

What each round scores

Score each dimension from 1 to 4: 1 not shown, 2 partly shown, 3 solid, 4 strong without prompting. Keep a running average per round type so you can see the trend. These tables are a practice rubric built from what interviewers commonly look for; companies weight the dimensions differently, so treat them as a guide and not as any employer's scorecard.

Self-review after every mock

▶ Video lesson: From a bad mock to one fix 3:36 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Review within 24 hours: soon enough that the memory is fresh, late enough that the emotion has settled. Always review from the recording, not from how it felt. A mock that felt terrible may turn out to be solid on three dimensions and weak on one, and that one dimension is the whole lesson.

After every mock

Writing a fix you can act on

A trait

'Be less nervous. Think more clearly. Communicate better.'

Nothing here can be done at 9 am on Thursday, or checked afterwards.

An action with a check

'Spend the first three minutes on two written examples before touching code. Ask "does that match what you expect?" before coding. Re-test on Thursday.'

You can do it, a partner can see it, and the next recording shows whether it happened.

Not every bad mock has the same cause, and the repair depends on the cause. Step through the triage before deciding what to practise next.

A loop day: five rounds, one body

▶ Video lesson: A loop day: protect the gaps 3:56 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A full loop packs four or five rounds into a single day. You do not choose the schedule, but you do choose what happens in the gaps. The simulation runs the same five interviews twice: once with deliberate rest, and once with the common alternative of replaying the last round, cramming notes and skipping lunch.

A five-round loop day: the same interviews, two ways of using the gaps

Each column is 15 minutes, starting at 09:30. The bars show energy at the start of each round; the dashed line marks where slips start. The figures are invented to show the shape, not measured.

Pace yourself inside each round as well. The splits below are typical and should give way to whatever the interviewer tells you. The most common time-waster in each round type is in the last column.

RoundA workable split (minutes)The usual time-waster
DSA (45 min)5 clarify and examples, 10 approach (brute force, then better), 15 code, 8 test and fix, 5 complexity and follow-ups, 2 your questionsCoding before the approach is agreed
LLD (90 min)10 requirements and entities, 10 interfaces, 40 main flow in code, 15 tests, 15 extensionsPerfecting one class while the main flow does not yet run
HLD (45 min)5 requirements, 5 estimates, 5 API and data model, 10 high-level design, 15 deep dives, 5 trade-offs and bottlenecksEstimating for 15 minutes, or skipping the deep dive
Behavioural (45 min)3 introduction, about 4 questions at 9 minutes each (2 for the answer, 7 for follow-ups), 6 your questionsA four-minute first answer that eats the follow-up time
Time plans per round type. Scale them if your round is 60 minutes.

The three-line reset between rounds: 1. 'That round is over; I do not need to judge it.' 2. 'Next is a [type] round, and my first move is [clarify / requirements / a headline].' 3. One slow breath out. Then stand, drink water, and keep your phone in your pocket.

In most loops each interviewer writes up their own round, so one weak round does not automatically sink the day. Carrying it into the next round can. That is the real reason to protect your gaps.

The last week, the day before, the morning of

The last week is for consolidating and rehearsing, not for new learning. Four mistakes recur every season; each is shown below with its repair.

Mistake 1: cramming a new topic

Learn something new in the last days

'I have never done segment trees, so I will spend three days on them just in case.'

New material this late is shallow and fragile, and it displaces practice on what is likely to come up.

Redo your lowest-scoring work

'I will redo the three problems and the one design I scored lowest, from scratch, aloud. A one-page overview of anything new, no more.'

Re-doing consolidates what you already half know.

Mistake 2: the marathon night before

One more problem at 2 am

'I will stay up and do a few hard ones to be safe.'

You trade the sharpness you need tomorrow for marginal knowledge tonight.

A light review and a normal bedtime

'I stop at 6 pm, read my story headlines and gap log for 30 minutes, prepare the room and go to bed at my normal time or earlier.'

Mistake 3: only silent practice

Thirty problems solved silently

A week of quiet solving feels productive, but on the day you have to explain, and explaining is a different skill.

A few solved aloud, to a timer

Two or three problems, designs and stories per day, spoken out loud and recorded. Fewer reps, but they match the interview.

Mistake 4: treating one mock as a verdict

'That mock was terrible, so I am not ready'

One bad session carries more emotion than information, and chasing a perfect mock turns practice into anxiety.

'What do the last four scores show?'

Compare rubric scores over the last three or four mocks of that type. An upward trend with one dip is a good week.

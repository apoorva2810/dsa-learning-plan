Question drills

Reading about STAR does not make you fluent; saying stories out loud against a clock does. This chapter gives you a bank of 58 behavioural questions with follow-ups and notes on what a strong answer contains, a drill protocol that takes five minutes a round, a rubric for scoring your own answers, and a way to turn every gap a drill exposes into a story in your bank.

After this chapter you can
  • Run a five-minute drill round: draw a question, think for 30 seconds, answer aloud in two minutes, self-score, log the gap
  • Steer one of 6-8 prepared stories to fit a question from any of ten themes
  • Score an answer on six dimensions and turn the weakest one into a specific fix
  • Handle three levels of follow-up (your role, the numbers, hindsight) without stalling
  • Work out how many drills per day you need before your interview date
  • Capture a missing story in the STAR builder at the moment a drill exposes the gap

Why drill out loud

▶ Video lesson: One story, five questions 3:49 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Reading about STAR builds recognition: you see a good answer and nod. An interview asks for recall under pressure. Someone asks a question in wording you have not seen, and within seconds you must pick a story, shape it to the question and say it clearly in about two minutes. That is a spoken skill, and it improves only by speaking, against a clock, with questions you did not choose.

A drill trains five things at once:

  • Retrieval: finding the right story in 30 seconds instead of 30 minutes.
  • Compression: fitting three months of work into two minutes without losing the point.
  • Delivery: pace, filler words, how you start and how you stop.
  • Depth: surviving the follow-up questions that come after the first answer.
  • Self-diagnosis: scoring yourself honestly, so each round fixes something specific.

A behavioural question is a request for evidence: 'show me a time you did this.' The interviewer is scoring three things. Can they picture what happened (specifics)? What did you do, as opposed to the team? What did you learn? The follow-ups exist to find out whether the story is real, so a polished first answer with nothing behind it fails at question two.

You do not need one story per question. A well-prepared story serves many questions, because each question asks you to emphasise a different part of the same events. Step through the diagram: one project, five questions, five different openings.

The drill protocol

▶ Video lesson: One drill round, step by step 4:08 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A round has five moves and takes about five minutes. The 30 seconds of thinking is deliberate: in the room you also get only a few seconds, and the skill being trained is choosing the story quickly, not composing a perfect answer.

flowchart TD
  Pick["1. Draw a question"] --> Think["2. Think 30 s<br/>story + first line"]
  Think --> Answer["3. Answer aloud<br/>2 min, recorded"]
  Answer --> Score["4. Self-score<br/>six dimensions"]
  Score --> Gap{"Below 3 anywhere,<br/>or average under 4?"}
  Gap -->|yes| Note["5. Log the gap<br/>one line, one fix"]
  Gap -->|no| Next["Next question"]
  Note --> Redo["Redo the weak part<br/>60 to 90 s"]
  Redo --> Next
  Next --> Pick
The loop never ends with 'felt good'. Every round either logs a gap or moves a question up a level.

Two details that matter more than they look. First, answer aloud: murmuring or typing hides rambling, because you can edit as you go. Second, stop when the timer rings. A 45 to 60 minute round usually has room for only a handful of questions plus follow-ups, so a four-minute monologue uses up the time in which the interviewer would have probed your best material.

Themed drills: ten themes in five decks

Each deck below holds ten questions from two themes. Next question draws one at random, with no repeats until the deck is used up. Start the 2-minute timer and answer aloud. Only then press Show follow-ups & notes and compare your answer with what a strong one contains. A question that leaves you with nothing to say is the most useful draw of all: log it, and see the last section of this chapter.

Deck 1: Ownership & ambiguity

Ownership: work beyond your remit, end-to-end responsibility after launch. Ambiguity: unclear goals, incomplete data, no process.

Deck 2: Conflict & influence

Conflict: technical disagreements, difficult people, review arguments. Influence: persuading without authority, changing minds, changing process.

Deck 3: Failure & learning

Failure: mistakes, missed goals, hard feedback. Learning: new technology, root-cause digging, staying current.

Deck 4: Deadlines & trade-offs

Deadlines: risk, saying no, colliding priorities. Trade-offs: simple vs elegant, speed vs quality, build vs buy, debt vs features.

Deck 5: Mentoring & customer

Mentoring: growing others, onboarding, feedback. Customer: customer-driven change, proactive detection, simplifying for users.

Mixed rounds and follow-ups

Real interviews do not announce the theme, and they do not stop after the first answer. The first answer opens the door; the follow-ups test what is behind it. Three probes come up again and again: your own role, the evidence, and hindsight.

sequenceDiagram
  participant I as Interviewer
  participant Y as You
  I->>Y: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate
  Y->>I: Two-minute answer with context, actions and result
  I->>Y: What was your own role in that decision?
  Y->>I: I wrote the prototype and ran the benchmark
  I->>Y: What numbers made you confident?
  Y->>I: p99 dropped from 480 to 190 ms on the same data
  I->>Y: What would you do differently?
  Y->>I: Bring the benchmark to the first review, not the third
The first answer takes two minutes; the three follow-ups take another three and are where most of the scoring happens.

Once the themed decks feel comfortable, switch to the mixed deck. It holds all 50 themed questions plus eight openers and curveballs ('Tell me about yourself', 'What is your biggest weakness?', a Friday-evening production bug). Run it as a session of four: draw four questions back to back, answer each in two minutes, and score all four only at the end. That trains recovery between answers, which is the real interview situation, and it removes the urge to polish one answer forever.

Mixed deck: all themes plus openers and curveballs (58 questions)

After each answer, reveal the follow-ups and answer one of them aloud as well. If you stall on a follow-up, the story is missing a fact; add it to the story's notes.

Scoring yourself honestly

▶ Video lesson: Score it, then fix the worst part 4:10 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Score every answer on six dimensions, 1 to 5, straight after you finish. The table shows what 1, 3 and 5 look like. Use the in-between scores when an answer sits between two descriptions. The target before you stop drilling a question: no dimension below 3 and an average of 4 or more.

Dimension1 (weak)3 (acceptable)5 (strong)
StructureWanders; no clear start or endSituation, action and result are all present but unbalancedHeadline, context in 25 s or less, actions about 60%, result, learning; easy to follow
SpecificsGeneral claims ('we improved things')Some detail: one number or one named systemTwo or more numbers, named systems and dates; a listener could picture it
OwnershipMostly 'we'; your role is unclearYour role is clear but your decisions are not explained'I' decided, built or persuaded, with reasons; credit given where it is due
ResultNo outcome, or one not tied to youOutcome stated, no measureBefore/after figures, effect on customers or team, honest about what fell short
DeliveryOver 3 min or under 60 s; fillers; reading notesNear 2 min; some fillers; uneven pace2:00 give or take 15 s; steady pace; pauses instead of fillers; ends on purpose
RelevanceThe story does not answer the question askedIt answers the question, but a better story existedThe question's theme is the centre of the story; you know which principle it shows
Six dimensions. Specifics and ownership are the two most often scored low, and the two easiest to fix with a recording.

The score is only useful if it triggers a fix. Step through the decision diagram: find the lowest score and apply the matching repair before you draw another question.

flowchart LR
  Low{"Lowest score?"}
  Low -->|Structure| F1["Rewrite as STAR or CARL<br/>one sentence per part"]
  Low -->|Specifics| F2["Add two numbers<br/>and one system name"]
  Low -->|Ownership| F3["Replace we with I<br/>in the action part"]
  Low -->|Result| F4["State the outcome<br/>and what changed since"]
  Low -->|Delivery| F5["Cut context to 20 s<br/>and re-record"]
  Low -->|Relevance| F6["Pick a better story<br/>or capture a new one"]
Each low score maps to one concrete repair. 'Practise more' is not on the list.

After every drill

Score from the recording, not from memory. Ratings made from memory tend to be kinder than ratings made while listening, and the two dimensions people overrate most are delivery and ownership.

Answering aloud and recording

A phone voice memo is enough. Put the phone on the desk, record, answer. Listen to at least one answer a day at 1.25x speed, and listen for three things: whether the first 30 seconds state the headline, how often you say 'we' where you did the work yourself, and the timestamp where you start to ramble.

Tips for answering aloud

Write beats, not scripts

A card with four to six beats ('headline, context, decision, rollout, result, learning') survives a re-worded question. A memorised script collapses the moment the interviewer asks it differently.

Know your word budget

People speak at roughly 130 to 150 words a minute, so two minutes is about 260 to 300 words. If your written draft is 450 words, it is a three-minute answer, whatever it feels like.

Pause instead of filling

A one-second pause sounds considered; 'um, so, basically' sounds lost. A pause usually feels much longer to you than it sounds to the listener, so practise tolerating it.

Practise the way you will sit

Same chair, same distance from a screen, eyes on the camera lens or a fixed point. Delivery shifts with posture and setting, so rehearse in the setting you will face.

End on purpose

Finish with the learning or a closing line ('That is the short version, happy to go deeper on the design') and stop. Trailing off is the most common unscored weakness.

When you blank

Say 'let me think for a second', take a sip of water, and restate the question in your own words. It buys ten seconds and sounds composed.

The opening sentence

Context first, headline never

'So, um, this was at my last company, and we had this payments service, and basically the team was kind of struggling with retries, and my manager asked if I could look at it, so I started reading the code...'

Forty seconds in, the listener still does not know what you did or why it matters.

Headline first

'I owned the move of our payment retries from a cron job to a queue. It took duplicate charges from about 40 a week to zero in two months. The hard part was making retries idempotent, so let me walk you through that.'

In 20 seconds: your role, the result, and where the interesting part is.

Who did what

'We' hides your contribution

'We decided to move to a queue, we built the consumer, and we rolled it out in stages.'

Nothing here tells the interviewer what you decided or built.

'I' with reasons, credit where due

'I proposed the queue after I measured that 70% of duplicates came from overlapping cron runs. I designed the idempotency keys and wrote the consumer; Priya built the dashboards. I ran the rollout at 5%, 50% and 100% and watched the duplicate-charge alarm at each step.'

Decisions, measurements and credit, in four sentences.

Planning your reps

Drills only work if they are frequent. The calculator turns your calendar into a daily target. The default numbers are a three-week plan against a 50-question bank, with each question seen twice.

Change the days left and the drills per day. The last two lines tell you whether your target is realistic: if 'drills per day needed' is higher than what you can do, lower the passes, not the quality of the review.

A three-week shape. Week one finds gaps, week two repairs them, week three adds pressure.

Treat each question like a flashcard with a level. A question you have never drilled is Unseen. A weak result makes it Shaky and puts it back in tomorrow's queue; a strong result makes it Solid, and it returns after a few days to check that you still remember it. Forgetting is normal, and the schedule exists to catch it.

stateDiagram-v2
  direction LR
  [*] --> Unseen
  Unseen --> Shaky: below 4
  Unseen --> Solid: 4 or more
  Shaky --> Solid: redo scores 4 or more
  Solid --> Sharp: holds after 3 days
  Solid --> Shaky: slips
  Sharp --> Shaky: slips
A question climbs from Unseen to Sharp only by surviving repeats. Anything that slips goes back to Shaky.

Capture the story you just discovered

When a drill leaves you with nothing to say, do not skip the question and do not invent a story. Spend ten minutes mining your real work. Good places to look: code reviews and pairing (mentoring), on-call incidents and post-mortems (failure, deadlines, learning), migrations and rewrites (ownership, trade-offs), a disagreement in a design review (conflict), a tool you introduced (influence), a support escalation (customer), a stack you picked up fast (learning). Write the draft in the builder below, in STAR form. Aim for 60 or more words in the action part, and put numbers in the result.

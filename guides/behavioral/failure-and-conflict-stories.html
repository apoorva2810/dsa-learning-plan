Failure & conflict stories

Failure and conflict questions are where interviewers learn how you behave when something goes wrong. This chapter gives you a seven-part structure for a failure story, a six-part structure for a conflict story, the four de-escalation moves that make a conflict story credible, a worked example of disagree-and-commit, three full examples, and a drill of 15 questions with a CARL builder for your own stories.

After this chapter you can
  • Explain what an interviewer scores in a failure or conflict question (ownership, growth, trust, judgement) and what sinks an answer
  • Tell a failure story in seven parts, with the impact stated in numbers and the learning shown as a mechanism with evidence
  • Recognise and repair four anti-patterns: blaming others, the failure that was really a success, no stated impact, no learning
  • Tell a conflict story that states the other person's position fairly and reaches a decision through data or a well-run escalation
  • Use the four de-escalation moves (restate, shared goal, data, escalate well) in a story and in a real disagreement
  • Show disagree-and-commit as a sequence, and say where it stops applying

What the interviewer is testing

Failure and conflict questions are the two places where a rehearsed answer falls apart, which is why interviewers like them. Nobody can fake how they behave when something they built breaks, or when a colleague says their design is wrong. The interviewer listens for four things:

  • Ownership: do you say 'I' about the mistake, and name the decision that was yours?
  • Growth: did something durable change afterwards (a check, a habit, a design), or only your intentions?
  • Trust: do you speak fairly about the other people in the story, including the ones you disagreed with?
  • Judgement: was your response proportionate, raising the issue early and escalating only when it was warranted?
flowchart LR
  Q["Failure or<br/>conflict question"] --> Own["Ownership<br/>do you say I?"]
  Q --> Grow["Growth<br/>what changed?"]
  Q --> Trust["Trust<br/>fair to others?"]
  Q --> Judge["Judgement<br/>right-sized response?"]
  Own --> Sig["Signal: safe to give<br/>a system and a team"]
  Grow --> Sig
  Trust --> Sig
  Judge --> Sig
Every sentence of your story should feed one of the four boxes in the middle.

Underneath both questions the interviewer is asking: if this person breaks something, or disagrees with someone on my team, will I be glad it was them? A strong answer lets them conclude yes within two minutes, by showing a real mistake handled well rather than a flawless record.

At SDE-2 the stakes are expected to be larger than a typo or a late-night bug. You own components, you make design calls that other teams build on, and you disagree with seniors, product managers and neighbouring teams. A story where the only person affected was you tends to read as a more junior story. Pick failures where your decision mattered, the impact was real but recoverable, and enough time has passed to show that something lasting changed.

The failure story, part by part

▶ Video lesson: A failure story, told badly and told well 4:13 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A failure story has seven parts, in this order: facts first, then ownership, then change. The whole answer runs about 2 minutes 20 seconds, a little longer than a standard answer because it has more parts; stay under 2:30 so there is room for follow-ups. The example below is one running incident, told as it would be said.

flowchart TD
  Ctx["1. Context<br/>20 s"] --> Dec["2. My decision<br/>20 s"]
  Dec --> Wrong["3. What went wrong<br/>20 s"]
  Wrong --> Impact["4. Impact, honestly<br/>10 s"]
  Impact --> Fix["5. What I did first<br/>20 s"]
  Fix --> Change["6. What I changed<br/>30 s"]
  Change --> Proof["7. Evidence it worked<br/>20 s"]
The first three parts are the facts, the next two are your behaviour under pressure, and the last two are the growth.

Seven parts is a lot to fit into 140 seconds, so where the time goes matters. The simulation draws two versions of the same incident on a clock. In the strong version the facts take under a minute and the change gets the most time. In the weak version the context swallows the answer, the decision hides behind 'we', and the last two parts, the ones that show growth, are missing.

The clock of a failure answer

Step through each part and read the note under the chart: it says what the interviewer knows at that moment. Switch to the weak version to see which parts disappear.

Three rules for the parts:

  • 'My decision' starts with 'I' and contains the reason you made the choice. The reason shows you were not careless; the 'I' shows you own it.
  • 'What went wrong' names a mechanism: 'the DELETE held a table lock on a 40-million-row table', not 'it did not go well'.
  • 'What I changed' is something that works without you remembering it: a check, an alarm, a test, a template. 'I will be more careful' is not a mechanism.

A strong answer sounds like: 'I shipped a bulk delete that locked our sessions table for 11 minutes and failed about 9% of logins. It was my call to test on 10,000 rows when production had 40 million. I killed the job four minutes after the page and wrote the review that day. I changed three things: batched deletes, a statement timeout, and a production-scale load test on our release checklist. The next three batch jobs shipped clean.'

Four anti-patterns that sink failure stories

Most weak failure answers fall into one of four traps. Each pair below shows the trap and the repair, using the same facts.

Anti-pattern 1: blaming others

The failure belongs to someone else

'The requirements kept changing and QA did not catch the bug, so we missed the date.'

Every cause sits outside you. The interviewer hears an engineer who will blame the next person too.

Your part first, then the context

'I committed to a date before the requirements were stable. When they changed twice I had no slack, and I did not flag the risk until a week before. Now I ask for a two-day discovery spike before I give any date.'

The context is still there. Your decision comes first.

Anti-pattern 2: a failure that was really a success

The humble-brag

'My biggest failure is that I work too hard. I burned myself out, but we shipped three weeks early.'

Nothing failed, nothing cost anyone anything, and nothing changed.

A real miss with a real cost

'I estimated a migration at three weeks without reading the data. It took six. The downstream team had to re-plan their launch and lost a sprint.'

Someone else paid for the miss, which is what makes it a failure.

Anti-pattern 3: no impact stated

'It was fixed quickly'

'There was a bug in production, but we fixed it quickly.'

The listener cannot tell whether this was a typo or a disaster, so they assume the smaller one, or probe.

Scale, money and duration in one sentence

'A bug in the refund path double-refunded 212 orders, about $9,400, before I caught it the next afternoon.'

Now the listener can weigh your response.

Anti-pattern 4: no learning

An intention

'I learned to communicate more and be more careful.'

It cannot be checked, and it will not survive the first follow-up ('what specifically changed?').

A mechanism with evidence

'I added an idempotency check to the refund path and a daily report that flags any refund above the order total. It caught two bad refunds in the next quarter.'

Something changed, and someone can verify it.

The humble-brag test: if your failure could sit on your CV as a strength, it is not a failure. Neither is a story whose only cost was your own sleep.

The conflict story

▶ Video lesson: Four moves that cool a disagreement 4:01 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Conflict questions are not asking whether you won. They ask how you behave when someone with a different view, and sometimes more power, disagrees with you: with curiosity, with evidence, and with respect. The structure has six parts, and the fourth, what you did to resolve it, is the one the interviewer is really scoring.

flowchart TD
  Ctx["1. Context and stakes"] --> Them["2. Their position,<br/>stated fairly"]
  Them --> Me["3. My position<br/>and evidence"]
  Me --> Move["4. What I did<br/>to resolve it"]
  Move --> Out["5. Outcome and<br/>relationship"]
  Out --> Learn["6. What I do<br/>differently now"]
The longest part is part 4. If your story jumps from 'we disagreed' straight to 'and then we decided', the interviewer learns nothing about you.

Part 4 is built from four de-escalation moves. They work in a real argument for the same reason they work in a story: each one shifts the conversation from who is right to what we are trying to achieve.

flowchart TD
  Heat["Disagreement<br/>heating up"] --> Restate["1. Restate their position<br/>until they say yes"]
  Restate --> Goal["2. Name the shared goal"]
  Goal --> Data["3. Agree what evidence<br/>would settle it"]
  Data --> Settled{"Settled?"}
  Settled -->|yes| Commit["Decide and commit"]
  Settled -->|no| Esc["4. Escalate well<br/>one page, both views"]
  Esc --> Decider["Decision owner decides"]
  Decider --> Commit
Escalation is the fourth move, not the first. Whatever the route, the ending is the same: a decision, and both people committed to it.
MoveWhat you sayWhy it worksTrap to avoid
Restate their position'So you want X because of Y. Have I got that right?'People soften when they feel understood, and misreadings surface early.Restating a weaker version of their view, or doing it with sarcasm.
Name the shared goal'We both want a safe launch on time. We differ on how.'It moves the talk from winning to solving.Pretending the goals match when they do not. If one of you optimises for speed and the other for safety, say so.
Seek data'What would we expect to see if each of us is right? Can we measure it this week?'It turns an opinion contest into an experiment.Asking for evidence you would not accept if it went against you.
Escalate well'We have reached the limit of what the two of us can settle. I will put both views on one page. Does it state yours fairly?'A fair summary, no surprise, and the decision sits with whoever owns it.Escalating before you have talked directly, or framing it as 'they are wrong'.
The four moves with words you can borrow. In a story, the phrase you quote is often the most convincing detail.

A design-review disagreement, three ways

Four lanes: your colleague, you, the evidence, and the decision. Arrows are messages; the cursor moves one turn at a time. Run all three and compare how many turns and how much trust each one costs.

Say something generous about the other person, and mean it: 'He was right that on-call cost was the real constraint.' If you cannot find one generous true thing to say about the person in your story, the story may be showing poor judgement of people, not of the conflict.

Disagree and commit

▶ Video lesson: Disagree, then commit 3:50 · 8 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Amazon's Leadership Principles name it Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, and a good conflict story often contains it. It has two halves with a hard boundary between them. Before the decision, you challenge respectfully and with evidence, even when it is uncomfortable. After the decision, you commit fully, including doing your share of the work of making it succeed. The failure modes sit on both sides: staying silent when you disagree, and complying while quietly undermining the decision.

sequenceDiagram
  participant Y as You
  participant L as Lead
  participant T as Team
  Y->>L: I think the Friday cutover is too risky, here are the numbers
  L->>Y: Understood, but the customer date is fixed so we go on Friday
  Y->>L: Decision made, can I add a canary and a rollback drill?
  L->>Y: Yes, add both
  Y->>T: We cut over on Friday, I raised a risk and we decided
  T->>Y: Is the risk real?
  Y->>T: Yes, and the canary covers it, I own the canary
  Y->>L: Cutover done with no customer impact, my estimate was too high
  L->>Y: Thanks, the canary made the call safe
Challenge in private and early, accept the decision, then own the mitigation in public. The last two messages are the honest report.

After the decision

Comply, but keep the objection alive

'Fine, we will do it your way. For the record, I said it was a bad idea.'

The work gets done with minimum effort, the objection reappears at every stand-up, and the risk gets no mitigation. The team hears doubt, and the lead learns that challenging you is costly.

Commit and own the risk

'We decided on Friday. I raised the risk because of last quarter's cutover numbers, and the decision is made. I will own the canary and the rollback drill so the risk we discussed is covered.'

The disagreement made the plan better, and the team sees someone who can lose an argument well.

Disagree and commit is for judgement calls, where reasonable people differ. It does not cover safety, security, legal or ethical problems. If you believe a change breaks a security policy or could harm customers, you escalate and you do not ship it. Saying so briefly in an interview shows judgement.

Interviewers probe three things: what data you brought, what you did after the decision, and how it turned out. Do not pick a story where you were right and everyone else was wrong. Pick one where you were overruled and made it work, or where you changed your mind. A good line to aim for: 'I disagreed, I said why with numbers, I lost the argument, and then I volunteered to own the mitigation.'

Three worked examples

Each example is written in CARL (context, action, result, learning), the framework from STAR & CARL: structuring a story, and shows the parts above in order. The numbers are illustrative. Use them as a model for the shape, never as a script; your own facts are what make the story credible.

Worked examples

Context. I owned the session-cleanup job for our login service. The nightly job took four hours because it deleted expired sessions row by row, so I changed it to one bulk DELETE. I tested it on a staging table with 10,000 rows.

Action. Production had 40 million rows. The DELETE held a table lock and login errors began at 14:02. I was paged at 14:05, killed the job at 14:09 and confirmed recovery at 14:13. I posted updates in the incident channel and wrote the review that same day. The cause was my decision: my staging table was 4,000 times smaller than production and the job had no statement timeout. I changed it to delete in batches of 5,000 with a pause, set a statement timeout, and added a production-scale load test to the release checklist for any batch job touching a hot table.

Result. About 9% of login attempts failed for 11 minutes, roughly 18,000 failures. No data was lost. The next three batch jobs shipped without incident, and two neighbouring teams adopted the checklist item.

Learning. 'Tested on staging' is not evidence when staging is 4,000 times smaller. Before I merge anything that writes in bulk, I now ask 'what is the biggest table this touches in production?'

Why it works: the impact is in numbers, the decision is named as mine, the fix is a mechanism, and there is evidence that it worked.

Context. We were designing how our order service tells other teams about status changes, about 200 events a second at peak. A staff engineer proposed a new topic on the shared streaming platform. I preferred a transactional outbox table with a polling API, which I thought was simpler for our scale. We went back and forth twice in review, and it was getting tense.

Action. I stopped arguing the solution and asked what he was optimising for. He expected many consumers in future. I restated that to check, said I shared the goal, and explained my worry: nobody on our team had run that platform on-call. We agreed what would settle it. I spent two days measuring the outbox and writing a one-page comparison; he added the three consumers he knew were coming. The outbox handled 2,000 events a second, ten times our peak. We agreed to ship the outbox behind an interface that hides the transport, and wrote down a trigger to revisit: more than 8 consumers or 1,500 events a second.

Result. We shipped in three weeks instead of the six the streaming option needed. Nine months later we reached nine consumers and moved to the streaming platform in four days, because of the interface. He later asked me to review his design doc for another project.

Learning. I ask what the other person is optimising for before I defend my option, and I write down the condition under which I would change my mind.

Why it works: his position is stated fairly, the resolution is data plus a written trigger, and the relationship improved.

Context. I committed to delivering a reporting export for the finance team before month-end close, three weeks away. I had estimated from the schema without looking at the data.

Action. On day 8 I found that about 3% of the source rows were duplicates, which would make the totals wrong. I told the finance lead the same day, with an estimate of five more days to deduplicate correctly. I offered three options: ship on time with a duplicate warning and manual reconciliation; ship five days late with correct totals; or ship on time for the two accounts they needed for close and the rest later. They chose the third. I delivered the two-account export on the original date and the full export four days after.

Result. Finance closed on time. The full export was four days late, and the slip was my doing: I had not profiled the data in week one.

Learning. I now put a data-quality spike in the first 10% of any data project and list the assumptions to verify in the estimate. On my next project the spike found a schema mismatch on day two, before the date was committed.

Why it works: it admits a real miss, it raises the risk the same day with options, and the learning shows up in the next project.

Practice: drill, build, check

Draw a question, think for 30 seconds, and answer aloud using the seven-part or six-part structure. The timer is set to three minutes because these answers have more parts than a standard one; aim to finish by 2:30. Reveal the follow-ups afterwards and answer at least one of them aloud, because in these two themes the follow-ups are where the interviewer checks your honesty.

Failure and conflict drill (15 questions)

Eight failure questions and seven conflict questions, drawn at random. Use the story you built below as your first answer for any of them that fit.

Hard cases (click each)

I do not have a dramatic failure

Use a smaller real one: a missed estimate, a design flaw you caught late, a review comment you ignored. The stakes can be modest if the learning is real. Do not invent drama; the follow-ups will find it.

The failure was a team decision

Use 'we' for the decision and name your part: 'I proposed the date', 'I approved the change'. The interviewer wants to know what you did, and a team decision usually had someone's fingerprints on it.

My conflict was with my manager

That is fine and often excellent. Show that you disagreed privately and early, with data, and committed visibly once decided. Never make the manager the villain.

I was right and they were wrong

Prefer a story where you changed your mind or the answer was mixed. If you were right, the interesting part is how you made it easy for them to agree and what you did for the relationship afterwards.

The other person was genuinely difficult

Describe behaviours and their impact ('he rewrote my PRs without comment'), not character ('he was toxic'). Show what you did within your control, and mention escalation if you needed it.

The story happened a long time ago

Use something from the last two or three years if you can. If it is older, show how the lesson still shapes the way you work today.

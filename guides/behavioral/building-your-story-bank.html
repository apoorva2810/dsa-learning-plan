Building your story bank

You cannot rehearse an answer for every question, but you can build eight to ten strong stories and learn to retell each from several angles. This chapter shows how to mine your projects for raw material, extract and tag stories, check them against the ten themes interviewers keep returning to, calibrate scope and freshness to SDE-2, and audit the bank for gaps before the interview does.

After this chapter you can
  • Mine three to four years of projects for conflicts, failures, wins and decisions with a repeatable process
  • Extract eight to ten stories and tag each with the two or three themes it genuinely shows
  • Retell one story from different angles without inventing anything, and recognise when a story does not fit
  • Judge a story's scope and freshness against SDE-2 expectations
  • Audit the bank for coverage gaps with the matrix and a short script, then fill them

Why a bank beats a script

Interviewers do not ask your questions; they ask theirs. 'Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate' and 'Describe a decision where you did not have all the data' arrive in whatever wording the interviewer prefers, and you cannot memorise an answer to every variant. What you can prepare is raw material: a small set of true, detailed stories that you know well enough to retell from different angles.

A loop also has breadth built in. Several interviewers each ask two or three behavioural questions, and their feedback is read together afterwards. Telling the same story to three of them reads as having one story. Eight to ten stories from different projects, each tagged with the two or three themes it shows best, cover the ground with a margin for the question you did not expect.

Rehearsed scriptStory bank
PreparationOne polished answer per predicted questionEight to ten stories with the facts, numbers and decisions behind each
Unexpected questionBlank, or a stretched answerPick the closest story and lead with the relevant angle
First follow-upFalls apart as soon as the wording driftsSurvives, because you know the facts rather than the sentences
Breadth across a loopThe same story, repeatedDifferent projects for different interviewers
Effort over timeGrows with every new questionFixed; each finished project adds a story
The bank trades polish for coverage and resilience, which is what a loop tests.

Interviewers can tell a recited script from a remembered story within one follow-up. The bank is a set of facts you own, not a set of sentences you learned. You are allowed to pause, think, and choose the angle before you start talking.

Mining your projects

▶ Video lesson: Mining your projects 4:12 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Most people start from the questions ('what is my conflict story?') and come up empty, because good stories are not filed under question headings in memory. Start from the projects instead and ask what happened in them. The process is mechanical on purpose: it works when you have no inspiration.

Memory alone is unreliable, so dig in records. Good sources:

  • your last two performance reviews and any promotion document
  • pull requests and design documents you wrote
  • incident reports and postmortems you took part in
  • retrospectives, 1:1 notes and your manager's written feedback
  • thank-you messages from other teams or customers
  • on-call logs and tickets you closed

Jog your memory from version control

# everything you committed in the last three years, grouped by month
git log --author="$(git config user.name)" --since="3 years ago" \
        --date=format:%Y-%m --pretty="%ad %s" | sort | less
Commit messages are a surprisingly good index of what you worked on. Pick the clusters, then open the pull requests.

Prompts that jog memory

  • When did you push back on a design, a deadline or a priority, and what happened?
  • Which code review turned into a long thread, and how did it end?
  • Where did two teams want different things from the same API or service?
  • When did your manager or a tech lead overrule you, and what did you do next?
  • Which disagreement did you lose and still support the outcome?
  • What did you ship that you later had to roll back or hotfix?
  • What did you estimate badly, and what did you do when you noticed?
  • Which incident could you have prevented with a different habit?
  • What feedback stung, and what did you change because of it?
  • What did you build that nobody used?
  • What exists today because you pushed for it?
  • What do other teams still use that you built?
  • Which metric moved because of your work, and by how much?
  • When did a customer or another team thank you, and for what exactly?
  • What did you simplify or delete that made things better?
  • Where did you pick between two real options with incomplete information?
  • What did you decide not to build, and why?
  • When did you choose the boring technology on purpose?
  • Where did you cut scope to hit a date, and what did you protect?
  • Which decision would you make differently now?

The ten themes

Interviewers use different words for the same handful of competencies. These ten themes are a working vocabulary, not an official list; between them they cover most of the behavioural questions you will meet at this level. A theme is a kind of evidence. A question is one way of asking for it, and one story can supply several kinds.

ThemeWhat the interviewer is checkingA typical question
OwnershipYou act on problems that are not strictly yours and follow through past the hand-offTell me about a time you took on something outside your role.
ConflictYou can disagree on substance, stay respectful and still move forwardTell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or manager.
FailureYou own mistakes, recover, and change how you workTell me about a time you failed.
AmbiguityYou make progress when the goal or the spec is unclearTell me about a time the requirements were vague.
Tight deadlineYou prioritise, cut scope deliberately and protect qualityTell me about a time you had to deliver under pressure.
Influence without authorityYou move people who do not report to youTell me about a time you persuaded another team to change course.
MentoringYou make others better on purposeTell me about a time you helped a colleague grow.
Customer focusYou start from the user's problem and measure by their outcomeTell me about a time you went out of your way for a customer.
Technical trade-offYou compare options on explicit criteria and own the compromiseTell me about the hardest technical decision you made.
LearningYou pick up new things quickly and apply themTell me about a time you had to learn something fast.
The themes overlap: many conflict stories are also influence stories. Tag the two or three where the decisive moment sits.

These themes map onto Amazon's Leadership Principles but are not the same list; Amazon's Leadership Principles covers the sixteen. Tagging by theme first keeps the bank useful at companies that do not use the principles, and the mapping to principles is a second pass over the same stories.

One story, many questions

▶ Video lesson: One story, many questions 3:34 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

A good story has several faces. The events are fixed, but a question about ownership, one about trade-offs and one about mentoring each care about a different part of them. The skill is choosing the angle: what to open with, what to lean on, and what to trim.

The retelling must stay true. You can change the emphasis; you cannot change the facts. If a question needs evidence your story does not contain, the story does not answer it.

flowchart LR
  ST["One story:<br/>duplicate notifications"]
  ST --> Q1["Ownership question"]
  ST --> Q2["Trade-off question"]
  ST --> Q3["Mentoring question"]
  ST --> Q4["Ambiguity question"]
  Q1 --> L1["Lean on: choosing to own it"]
  Q2 --> L2["Lean on: options and criteria"]
  Q3 --> L3["Lean on: how I delegated"]
  Q4 --> L4["Lean on: the investigation"]
Same events, four openings. What changes is the first sentence and where the detail goes.

The same story, four angles and one non-fit

Ownership: 'Tell me about a time you took ownership beyond your role'

Open with: "A provider slowdown was duplicating notifications for 3% of customers. I was on call and my job that night was to mitigate. I chose to own the root cause."

Lean on: the moment you chose to own it (the follow-up would have been a backlog ticket), the follow-through (alarm, runbook, adoption by two other teams), and that it held when the provider slowed down again.

Trim: circuit-breaker thresholds and load-test details.

Technical trade-off: 'Tell me about a difficult technical decision'

Open with: "I had to choose between an exactly-once queue and idempotency keys with backoff and a circuit breaker, and two other teams were affected either way."

Lean on: the options, the criteria (migration time, blast radius, operational burden), why the queue lost (four weeks, two other teams), and what you accepted by choosing: at-least-once delivery with deduplication.

Trim: the night of the incident and the support-ticket numbers.

Mentoring: 'Tell me about a time you helped someone grow'

Open with: "In a fix I was leading, I paired with a new grad, Priya, and had her build the circuit breaker, so that she could own it afterwards."

Lean on: how you delegated (we designed it together, she wrote it, I reviewed every pull request) and what changed for her afterwards: she owned the breaker. Have one concrete fact ready, such as carrying its pager or presenting it at the team review.

Trim: the idempotency design and the diagnosis.

Ambiguity: 'Tell me about solving a problem with incomplete information'

Open with: "3% of notifications were duplicated and nobody knew why: our logs said timeout, the provider's said delivered."

Lean on: how you made progress without the answer: pulling 200 duplicated sends, comparing timestamps, forming the timeout hypothesis and testing it against the provider's log.

Trim: the design, the load test and the team.

Failure: 'Tell me about your biggest failure' (does not fit)

The only failure in this story is the first mitigation, a longer timeout that backed up the workers and was rolled back after 20 minutes. That is a small mistake corrected quickly. Using it for 'biggest failure' would sound like dodging the question.

Tag the story with Ownership, Technical trade-off and Ambiguity, not Failure, and find a story with a real cost and a real lesson for that theme. Knowing which questions a story cannot answer is part of knowing the story.

Calibrating to SDE-2: scope and freshness

Two checks decide whether a story belongs in an SDE-2 bank: scope (how big was the problem you owned?) and freshness (how recent is the behaviour?).

Scope. SDE-1 stories are about doing a well-defined piece of work well: fix this bug, build this feature to this spec. SDE-2 interviewers expect you to have owned a problem whose boundaries you had to find: a service, a recurring failure, an interface between teams. The same project can supply either kind of story depending on what you emphasise. Levels and titles vary by company, so treat the ladder below as a pattern, not a rubric.

Freshness. Prefer stories from the last two to three years. An older story is acceptable when it is exceptional and still describes how you work today, but a bank made only of early-career stories signals that nothing since has been as hard. School and internship projects belong in the bank only if you have under two years of experience.

flowchart LR
  A["Task<br/>fix a bug"] --> B["Feature<br/>build to a spec"]
  B --> C["System<br/>own a service"]
  C --> D["Cross-team<br/>shape an interface"]
  D --> E["Organisation<br/>set direction"]
SDE-2 stories sit around System and Cross-team. Task and Feature stories are fine as supporting detail, not as the headline.

The same project at two scopes

Task scope: reads as SDE-1

"I was assigned a ticket to add retries to the export job. I followed the design doc, wrote the retry loop, got two reviews and shipped it. Export failures went down."

The problem was handed over, the approach was given, and the result is a direction with no number.

System scope: reads as SDE-2

"Exports were failing about 4% of the time and nobody owned the problem. I traced it to three causes across two teams, wrote a one-page proposal, got the storage team to fix their timeout, and shipped retries with idempotent writes myself. Failures fell to 0.3% and I set up the alarm that now pages us."

The candidate found the problem, crossed a team boundary, chose the approach, measured the result and stayed on the hook afterwards.

Smell testSDE-1 versionSDE-2 version
Who defined the problem?Someone handed it to youYou found it or framed it
BoundaryOne component or ticketA service, a recurring failure or an interface between teams
AutonomyFollowed a design or a patternChose the approach and defended it
People involvedYour teamOther teams with different priorities
After launchHanded offYou watched the metric, carried the pager or ran the follow-ups
ResultTask completedA metric moved or a risk was removed
If a story scores SDE-1 on four or more rows, look for a different story or a different part of the same project.

If your honest answer is mostly task scope. Many engineers have more system-scope evidence than they think; it hides in places that do not feel like projects: the component you quietly became the owner of, the recurring alert you finally traced, the code review that changed another team's design, the runbook you wrote after an incident, the dependency you chased across a team boundary. Ask of each one: did I decide the problem or the approach, and did I stay with it after the merge?

The quality gate

▶ Video lesson: The quality gate 3:36 · 6 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Not every story that surfaces from the mining step deserves a place. Run each candidate through five questions in order; the first 'no' tells you what to fix.

flowchart TD
  C(["Candidate story"]) --> G1{{"True, and my role decisive?"}}
  G1 -- no --> X1["Drop, or use as supporting detail"]
  G1 -- yes --> G2{{"Recent and SDE-2 scope?"}}
  G2 -- no --> X2["Find a newer story or rescope"]
  G2 -- yes --> G3{{"A real decision or tension?"}}
  G3 -- no --> X3["Dig for the decision"]
  G3 -- yes --> G4{{"A result I can state?"}}
  G4 -- no --> X4["Recover the data first"]
  G4 -- yes --> G5{{"Survives three follow-ups?"}}
  G5 -- no --> X5["Rehearse and trim claims"]
  G5 -- yes --> K["Keep in the bank"]
Most stories fail at gate three: they describe execution without a decision.

Pick a story because you can survive questions about it, not because it sounds impressive. A modest project you know cold beats a prestigious one you only watched from the next desk.

Write them down

Add a story to your bank

Write each story that passed the gate. Give it a headline as the title: one sentence with the situation and your decisive move ('Stopped duplicate notifications by treating timeouts as unknowns'). Tag only the two or three themes where its decisive moment shows. Saved stories are shared across all behavioural chapters and become the columns of the matrix below.

Keep the bank alive. Add a story at the end of every project while the details are fresh, and keep a one-page sheet with the figures behind each result so that the numbers survive for the interview. Re-run the audit below before every loop, and demote stories as they age. The card below is a template for the notes behind each story; the builder holds the spoken version, the card holds everything you need to survive the follow-ups.

A story card, one per story

Headline:     Stopped duplicate notifications by treating timeouts as unknowns
Themes (2-3): Ownership, Technical trade-off
Scale:        Last spring, 1.2M messages a day, 3% duplicated, 40 tickets a day
My part:      On call; chose to own the root cause; wrote the design; built the key and backoff;
              reviewed every pull request on the breaker
Decisions:    Idempotency key from order ID + template ID (shipped and delivered never merge)
              Rejected an exactly-once queue: four weeks, two other teams
              Priority lane for password resets after the integration lead's concern
Numbers:      3.1% -> 0.02% (duplicate-rate dashboard); tickets 40/day -> ~1/week (support queue)
People:       Priya (breaker), the integration lead (priority lane), my manager (agreed the trade-off)
Learning:     A timeout is an unknown. I now ask what if it succeeded and I did not hear back
Fits:         Ownership, trade-off, ambiguity, mentoring (needs one more fact)
Does not fit: Biggest failure (the only mistake was small)
Likely probes: why not the queue? how did you pick 20% over 30 s? what did Priya own afterwards?
Plain text on purpose: it should fit on one screen and be quick to skim before an interview.

Map the bank and find the gaps

Once you have seven or eight stories, look at them together. Count how many stories each theme has, then act on the counts. The picture below is the audit of a sample bank (the same seven stories as in the script further down); step through it to see what each count means and what to do about it.

flowchart TD
  A["Sample bank<br/>7 stories, 10 themes"] --> S["Safe: 2 or more stories<br/>Ownership 3, Trade-off 3<br/>Influence 2, Deadline 2<br/>Customer focus 2, Learning 2"]
  A --> T["Thin: exactly one story<br/>Conflict, Failure, Ambiguity"]
  A --> G["Gap: no story<br/>Mentoring"]
  S --> K["Keep, and give different<br/>interviewers different stories"]
  T --> D["Dig for a second story<br/>Conflict and Failure first"]
  G --> M["Back to the mining step<br/>with one pointed question"]
The fix for a thin or empty theme is never to retag a story. It is to go back to the projects with a specific question.

Your bank against the ten themes

Rows are the ten themes; columns are the stories you have saved in any behavioural chapter. Click a cell to mark that the story gives strong evidence for that theme. Aim for two marks in every row, and no more than three in any column. Finish editing your stories before you tick: marks stay in their column position, and the story you saved most recently moves to the first column.

Audit the bank like a test suite

from collections import Counter

THEMES = [
    "Ownership", "Conflict", "Failure", "Ambiguity", "Tight deadline",
    "Influence without authority", "Mentoring", "Customer focus",
    "Technical trade-off", "Learning",
]

bank = {
    "Retry storm": ["Ownership", "Technical trade-off"],
    "Rewrite vs patch": ["Technical trade-off", "Influence without authority"],
    "Contract dispute": ["Conflict", "Influence without authority"],
    "Missed launch": ["Failure", "Learning", "Tight deadline"],
    "Vague search ask": ["Ambiguity", "Customer focus"],
    "Export escalation": ["Customer focus", "Tight deadline", "Ownership"],
    "Infra cost cut": ["Technical trade-off", "Ownership", "Learning"],
}


def audit(bank: dict[str, list[str]], minimum: int = 2, max_tags: int = 3) -> None:
    counts = Counter(tag for tags in bank.values() for tag in tags)
    unknown = sorted(set(counts) - set(THEMES))
    if unknown:
        print("Unknown themes:", ", ".join(unknown))
    for theme in THEMES:
        n = counts[theme]
        status = "ok" if n >= minimum else "THIN" if n == 1 else "GAP"
        print(f"{theme:<28} {n}  {status}")
    for title, tags in bank.items():
        if len(tags) > max_tags:
            print(f"Over-tagged: {title} ({len(tags)} themes)")
    if len(bank) < 8:
        print(f"Only {len(bank)} stories: aim for 8 to 10")


audit(bank)
Output for the sample bank: Mentoring GAP; Conflict, Failure and Ambiguity THIN; and a reminder that seven stories is one short.

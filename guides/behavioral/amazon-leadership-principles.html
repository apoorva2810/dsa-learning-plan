Amazon's Leadership Principles

Amazon scores behavioural answers against sixteen written principles. This chapter gives you each principle with what it really tests, sample questions, what a strong story contains and the trap; a method for working out which principles a question targets; the pairs that pull against each other; a matrix to map your story bank onto the sixteen; the Bar Raiser's role; and a drill of 24 principle-tagged questions.

After this chapter you can
  • Say each of the sixteen principles in one line and describe the evidence an interviewer wants for it
  • Work out which principles a question targets, including when its wording hides them
  • Handle the principles that pull against each other: Bias for Action and Are Right, A Lot; Frugality and Think Big; Disagree and Commit
  • Map your story bank onto the sixteen principles and fill the gaps
  • Explain what a Bar Raiser is for and how that changes your preparation

How Amazon uses the principles in a loop

Amazon says its evaluation process is rooted in the Leadership Principles, and interviewers collect evidence against them. You do not need to quote the principles. You need to tell stories whose decisions are recognisably the behaviour a principle describes. The principles are short paragraphs and their wording is the rubric: when a question says 'tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision', it is asking for the second half of one particular sentence, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit.

Everything in the earlier chapters still applies. What changes is that each question is routed through a named principle, so the shape of the evidence is predictable: every principle has a typical thing the interviewer wants to see, a typical way candidates fall short, and a typical set of follow-ups. That is what the rest of this chapter catalogues.

The details of formats change over time, so confirm with your recruiter. The constant is that evidence is written down against principles.

The Bar Raiser. One interviewer in the loop is typically a Bar Raiser: a specially trained Amazon interviewer from outside the hiring team, brought in as an objective third party. Amazon describes the role as stewardship of the Leadership Principles and of the hiring standard, which it states as every hire being better than at least half of the people currently in similar roles: that is the bar being raised. Bar Raisers also make sure the whole loop takes part in an open and fair discussion of the candidate, and they help hiring managers prepare. It is widely reported that the Bar Raiser's view carries particular weight in the debrief, though Amazon's own description stresses the role of objective advisor. For you this changes two things: expect at least one interviewer who has no stake in your team's deadlines and who will probe your stories for long-term judgement and depth of ownership, and treat every round as part of the same evidence file rather than a throwaway.

You do not need sixteen stories. Eight to ten good ones, each mapped to two or three principles, cover the ground, and the follow-ups probe depth rather than breadth. Name a principle only if it comes naturally; the interviewer is listening for the behaviour, not the label.

Which principles is this question testing?

Questions rarely name the principle. Read the wording for cues (a verb, a constraint, a kind of outcome) and route it. A question usually hits a primary principle and one or two secondary ones; answer the primary first, then weave the others into the decision and the learning. The tree below checks the strongest cues in order; the table after it is a longer lookup.

Principles 1 to 5: customer, ownership, invention, judgement, curiosity

▶ Video lesson: Principles 1 to 5: the customer and your judgement 3:59 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

Each entry has the same four parts: what the principle really tests, three sample questions, what a strong story contains, and the trap. Numbers follow Amazon's own order. 'In one line' is a paraphrase to jog your memory, not the official text; read the official wording once on Amazon's Leadership Principles page.

Customer Obsession to Learn and Be Curious

1. Customer Obsession

In one line. Start from the customer's problem and work backwards; earn and keep their trust; watch customers more than competitors.

What it really tests. Whether your decisions start from a specific customer problem, and whether you acted on it when it cost you something. 'Customer' can be an end user, an internal team, or an engineer consuming your API.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you went above and beyond for a customer.
  • Describe a decision that was right for the customer but hard for your team.
  • Tell me about a time customer feedback or data changed what you built.

A strong story has. A named customer or segment, how you learned their problem first-hand (tickets, logs, calls), a choice that cost you time or convenience, and a measured change in their experience.

The trap. Treating your manager as the customer, or describing a feature with no evidence about what the customer needed. If the customer never appears, only the ticket does, it is not this principle.

2. Ownership

In one line. Think long term, act for the whole company and not just your team, and never say 'that is not my job'.

What it really tests. Whether you act on problems beyond your remit and weigh long-term consequences against short-term wins.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you took on work outside your role.
  • Describe a time you gave up a short-term gain for long-term value.
  • Tell me about a time you saw something broken and fixed it although it was not yours.

A strong story has. Noticing before being asked, the choice to own it (what was the easy alternative?), follow-through past the merge, telling the people who nominally owned it, and a long-term payoff.

The trap. The hero story: everything done alone at 2 a.m. Ownership is not heroics, and 'I worked extra hours' contains no decision. Also beware of taking over a problem without telling its owner, which clashes with Earn Trust.

3. Invent and Simplify

In one line. Expect invention from yourself and your team, always look for the simpler way, and take good ideas from anywhere.

What it really tests. Whether you reduce complexity or find a new approach, and whether you borrow ideas rather than insisting on building everything yourself.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you simplified a complex process.
  • Describe an innovative solution you created.
  • Tell me about a time you adopted an idea from outside your team or company.

A strong story has. The complexity before, in numbers (steps, services, lines, minutes, incidents), what you removed or reframed and why it was safe, where the idea came from, and the measurement after.

The trap. Novelty for its own sake: building a framework where a library existed, or adding moving parts and calling it invention. Simplify is half of the principle.

4. Are Right, A Lot

In one line. Good judgement and instincts, backed by seeking other views and trying to prove your own beliefs wrong.

What it really tests. The quality of your judgement under uncertainty, and the process behind it: how you test your beliefs and take in dissent. It is not a claim that you are never wrong.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you made a decision with incomplete information.
  • Describe a time you were wrong and how you found out.
  • Tell me about a time someone else's input changed your mind.

A strong story has. The belief you held, the evidence you looked for that could have disproved it, whose view you asked, the decision, and an honest account of how it turned out, including partial misses.

The trap. Claiming a perfect record, or telling a hindsight-tidy story where the right answer was obvious. Interviewers want the process more than the outcome.

5. Learn and Be Curious

In one line. Never finished learning; curious about new possibilities and willing to act on them.

What it really tests. Whether you learn deliberately and quickly, and turn what you learn into something useful.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly.
  • What did you learn recently, and how did you apply it?
  • Tell me about a time curiosity led you to a better solution.

A strong story has. A specific gap, a learning plan (what you read, who you asked, what you built to test understanding), the time it took to become useful, and an application with an outcome.

The trap. A list of courses and certificates, or learning without application. 'I learned a new tool' is weak unless the learning was hard and the use was real.

Principles 6 to 10: people, standards, ambition, speed, thrift

▶ Video lesson: Principles 6 to 10: people, the bar, speed and thrift 4:06 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

These five are about raising people and the bar, aiming high, and the speed and thrift that keep ambition honest. Two of them (Bias for Action and Frugality) are easy to misread as 'be fast' and 'be cheap'; the traps below say what interviewers actually look for.

Hire and Develop the Best to Frugality

6. Hire and Develop the Best

In one line. Raise the bar with every hire and promotion, spot exceptional talent, and coach others to grow.

What it really tests. Whether you make the people around you better: through interviewing, mentoring and honest feedback.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you coached someone to a higher level.
  • Describe how you have contributed to hiring.
  • Tell me about a time you gave feedback that was hard to hear.

A strong story has. A diagnosis of the other person's gap, a plan, specific feedback, and an observable change. For hiring: the criteria you held to, and a time you said no to protect the bar.

The trap. Taking credit for a colleague's growth, or 'I am approachable' with no plan. At SDE-2 you are not expected to have managed people, but you are expected to mentor and to interview well.

7. Insist on the Highest Standards

In one line. Hold standards others may call unreasonable, keep raising them, and fix problems so that they stay fixed.

What it really tests. Whether you refuse to ship below the bar and fix root causes with mechanisms, even under pressure.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you would not compromise on quality.
  • Describe a time you raised the bar for your team.
  • Tell me about a defect you found late and what you did.

A strong story has. A measurable standard, the pressure to lower it, what you did (tests, review rules, automation), the mechanism that keeps the problem fixed, and the cost you accepted, such as a delay.

The trap. Perfectionism with no customer reason, or acting as a gatekeeper. Pair it with Bias for Action: show you judged which standards were non-negotiable.

8. Think Big

In one line. Small thinking is self-fulfilling; set and communicate a bold direction and look around corners for customers.

What it really tests. Whether you can see beyond the immediate scope, set an ambitious direction and bring others with you. At SDE-2 this is usually about extending a project's reach.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you expanded the scope of a project in a way that mattered.
  • Describe a bold idea you pushed for.
  • Tell me about a time you thought bigger than the team's plan.

A strong story has. The starting scope, the bigger vision and the customer reason for it, how you made it concrete (a prototype, a one-pager), who you convinced, and what was delivered or how it was staged.

The trap. Vision with no delivery, or scope creep dressed as vision. Tie the ambition to a customer outcome and a staged plan.

9. Bias for Action

In one line. Speed matters; many decisions are reversible and do not need extensive study; take calculated risks.

What it really tests. Whether you act on reversible decisions with the information you have, take sensible risks, and can tell which decisions are not reversible.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you made a decision quickly with limited information.
  • Describe a calculated risk you took.
  • Tell me about a time you did not wait for approval or perfect data.

A strong story has. Why the decision was reversible (or how you made it so), what you knew, the risk and how you bounded it, a checkpoint, and an honest result.

The trap. Recklessness presented as speed: acting on irreversible decisions with no checks, or 'I just did it and asked forgiveness'. Always show how you would detect a miss.

10. Frugality

In one line. Accomplish more with less; constraints breed resourcefulness and invention.

What it really tests. Resourcefulness under constraint, not cheapness. Did the constraint make the solution better?

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you delivered with limited resources.
  • Describe how you reduced cost without hurting the customer.
  • Tell me about a time you built something yourself instead of asking for headcount or budget.

A strong story has. The constraint in numbers (budget, people, time), the resourceful choice (reuse, simplify, automate), the saving, and proof that the customer experience did not suffer.

The trap. Cost cutting that hurts quality or customers ('we cancelled our monitoring tool'), or confusing frugality with long hours.

Principles 11 to 16: trust, depth, conviction, results, responsibility

▶ Video lesson: Principles 11 to 16: trust, depth, backbone and results 4:14 · 7 scenes · narrated, pause anytime and explore

The last six cover candour, depth and conviction, finishing the job, and the effect of what you build on other people. The final two were added to the list in 2021, so there is less candidate folklore about them; have at least one honest, specific story ready for each.

Earn Trust to Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility

11. Earn Trust

In one line. Listen, speak candidly, treat people respectfully, and be openly self-critical.

What it really tests. Candour and credibility: do you own mistakes openly, give direct feedback respectfully, and keep your commitments?

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you made a mistake and how you handled it.
  • Describe a time you had to deliver bad news.
  • Tell me about a time you won over a sceptic.

A strong story has. A real mistake of yours, the moment you told people (early and plainly), what you did to repair it, and how trust was kept or rebuilt. For feedback: specific, respectful and timely.

The trap. A 'weakness' that is a strength in disguise ('I work too hard'), blaming others, or an apology with no repair.

12. Dive Deep

In one line. Stay connected to the details, audit often, and be sceptical when the metrics and the anecdotes disagree.

What it really tests. Whether you go to the data and the code, find the real cause, and notice when a dashboard and a customer story do not match.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you dug into a problem and found the root cause.
  • Describe a time metrics and customer anecdotes disagreed.
  • Tell me about a time you had to understand a system you did not build.

A strong story has. The surface symptom, the method (logs, traces, sampling, hypotheses), the discrepancy you resolved, a specific root cause, and the prevention.

The trap. 'I investigated and found the issue' with no method, or diving so deep that the customer problem waited. Also avoid describing micromanagement: the principle is about staying connected, not controlling.

13. Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit

In one line. Challenge decisions respectfully even when it is uncomfortable, and once a decision is made, commit wholly.

What it really tests. Both halves: willingness to challenge with evidence, and full commitment afterwards.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.
  • Describe a time you pushed back on a decision.
  • Tell me about a time you disagreed, lost, and had to commit.

A strong story has. Your position and evidence, how and to whom you raised it, the decision, what you did afterwards to make it succeed, and the outcome or what you learned.

The trap. Telling only one half: all backbone (you were right and won) or all commitment (you complied silently). Never criticise the other person.

14. Deliver Results

In one line. Focus on the key inputs, deliver at the right quality and on time, and rise to setbacks.

What it really tests. A track record of finishing: setbacks, quality, timeliness, impact.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about the project you are proudest of delivering.
  • Describe a time you overcame a major setback to deliver.
  • Tell me about a time you delivered under a tight deadline.

A strong story has. A measurable goal, the key inputs you chose to focus on, the setback and how you recovered, the delivered outcome against target, and the quality you maintained.

The trap. Output without outcome ('we shipped'), results claimed for the team with no personal contribution, or heroics instead of planning.

15. Strive to be Earth's Best Employer

In one line. Make the workplace safer, more productive, more diverse and more just; lead with empathy; care about people's growth and success.

What it really tests. Whether you make your team a better place to work: inclusion, safety, growth, a sustainable pace.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you made your team more inclusive.
  • Describe how you supported a teammate through a difficult time.
  • Tell me about a time you improved how your team works.

A strong story has. A specific problem for people (an unsustainable on-call load, a quiet colleague being talked over, a review culture that discouraged juniors), the change you made, the outcome for people, and how you checked it.

The trap. Perks and social events ('I organised pizza Fridays'), vague statements about values, or 'work-life balance' with no concrete change.

16. Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility

In one line. Being big brings obligations: think about secondary effects on communities, the planet and future generations, and leave things better.

What it really tests. Whether you consider the wider effects of your work (privacy, accessibility, load on partners, cost, energy) and act on them. At SDE-2 this usually means the consequences of a design at scale.

Sample questions

  • Tell me about a time you considered the wider impact of a technical decision.
  • Describe a time you made something more accessible, secure or sustainable.
  • Tell me about a time you raised a concern about a feature's side effects.

A strong story has. A secondary effect you spotted, a decision to address it, who you involved, and the result.

The trap. Grand statements about the world, or volunteering unrelated to your work. Anchor it in an engineering decision.

When principles pull against each other

The principles are deliberately in tension. Interviewers know that Bias for Action can excuse recklessness, that Are Right, A Lot can excuse delay, and that Frugality can strangle ambition. A strong story shows that you know which principle should dominate in this situation and why, and what you did to protect the other. Two pairs come up constantly.

Bias for Action versus Are Right, A Lot

Bias for Action: move now

Looks like: deciding on the information you have, shipping the reversible change today, taking a calculated risk.

Strong when: the decision is a two-way door, waiting has a real cost, and a mistake can be undone cheaply.

Weak when: it slides into guessing, with no check on what you did not know.

Question words: 'quickly', 'without all the information', 'calculated risk', 'did not wait for approval'.

Are Right, A Lot: get it right

Looks like: seeking other views, testing your own beliefs, deciding with data.

Strong when: the decision is hard to undo, the downside is severe, and a few hours of checking is cheap.

Weak when: it becomes analysis paralysis, waiting for certainty that never arrives.

Question words: 'decision', 'judgement', 'you were wrong', 'changed your mind'.

  D["Decision to make"] --> R{{"Can it be undone cheaply?"}}
  R -- yes --> T["Two-way door"]
  R -- no --> O["One-way door"]
  T --> F["Decide with about 70% of the information"]
  F --> M["Ship behind a flag, set a checkpoint"]
  O --> S["Slow down: seek dissent, test your belief"]
  S --> C["Decide, record the reasoning"]
  M --> L["Learn: did the checkpoint catch a miss?"]
  C --> L
One question settles which principle should lead. The same candidate can use both in different stories, or in one.

Frugality versus Think Big

Frugality: do more with less

Looks like: a constraint treated as a design input: reuse, simplify, automate, build small before asking for investment.

Strong when: the constraint is real and the customer outcome is unchanged.

Weak when: it is cost cutting that hurts quality, or small thinking mistaken for thrift.

Question words: 'limited resources', 'reduce cost', 'with less'.

Think Big: aim higher

Looks like: a bold direction for customers, communicated clearly and staged into deliverable steps.

Strong when: the ambition is tied to a customer problem and a plan.

Weak when: it is vision with no delivery, or scope creep.

Question words: 'ambitious', 'vision', 'beyond the scope', 'look around corners'.

More tensions worth rehearsing

Insist on the Highest Standards versus Deliver Results

A quality bar that never meets the date is not high standards, it is a missed delivery. Show which standards were non-negotiable (correctness, security, data integrity), which could be staged, and how you made the trade explicit with stakeholders. The strong story ships on time with the non-negotiables intact and a dated plan for the rest.

Ownership versus Earn Trust

Taking over another team's problem without telling them is ownership that damages trust. Show that you told the owning team, offered to help or hand back, and left them with a better situation than you found.

Dive Deep versus Bias for Action

During an incident, depth can wait. Restore service first, then dig for the root cause. The strong story separates the two phases and shows that the mitigation was reversible and the investigation did happen afterwards.

Customer Obsession versus Frugality

Going far for one customer can cost the many. Show how you weighed the cost to others and whether the fix generalised (a feature, a runbook, a policy) rather than being a one-off favour.

Say the tension out loud. 'The change was reversible, so I leaned on speed, and I set a checkpoint at 48 hours to catch a miss.' Interviewers reward candidates who show they know which principle should dominate and why, and what they did to protect the other.

Map your stories to the principles

Map the stories from Building your story bank onto the sixteen principles. Tag each story with the two or three principles where its decisive moment shows, and do not tag it for a principle it only brushes. Aim to cover at least twelve of the sixteen with your eight to ten stories. For an SDE-2 loop, give two stories each to Ownership, Dive Deep, Bias for Action, Deliver Results, Customer Obsession and Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, because they line up with the signals in What SDE-2 interviewers probe; the rest need at least one honest story.
